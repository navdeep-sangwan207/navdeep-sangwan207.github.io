1. Introduction

Embedded systems interact with the physical world through sensors and actuators. Sensors collect information from the environment, while actuators perform physical actions based on control signals generated by the embedded system.

These components allow an embedded controller to monitor temperature, pressure, light, motion, position, speed and other physical parameters.

Sensor: A device that detects a physical quantity and produces a corresponding electrical or digital signal.

Actuator: A device that converts an electrical control signal into physical action.

Examples

  • Temperature sensor measures temperature.
  • Pressure sensor measures fluid or gas pressure.
  • Light sensor detects illumination.
  • Motor converts electrical energy into mechanical motion.
  • Relay switches an electrical circuit.
  • Solenoid produces linear mechanical movement.

4. Classification of Sensors

4.1 Analog Sensors

Analog sensors produce a continuously varying electrical signal corresponding to the measured physical quantity.

Examples:

  • LM35 temperature sensor.
  • Analog light-dependent resistor circuit.
  • Analog pressure transducer.
  • Analog potentiometer position sensor.

An analog sensor may produce a voltage or current that varies with the measured quantity.

4.2 Digital Sensors

Digital sensors provide information in digital form. Depending on the sensor, the output may be a logic state, pulse train or digitally encoded data.

Examples:

  • Digital temperature sensors.
  • Digital humidity sensors.
  • Digital proximity sensors.
  • Digital motion sensors.

4.3 Active and Passive Sensors

Sensors can also be classified according to their operating principles.

  • Self-generating sensors: Produce an electrical output from the measured physical quantity without requiring external excitation for the sensing conversion, such as certain thermocouples.
  • Modulating sensors: Require external excitation and change an electrical property such as resistance, capacitance or inductance.
The terms active and passive are used differently in some sensor classifications. Always identify the classification convention being used.

5. Common Types of Sensors

5.1 Temperature Sensor

A temperature sensor measures the temperature of an object or surrounding environment.

Examples:

  • LM35 analog temperature sensor.
  • Thermistor.
  • Thermocouple.
  • Digital temperature sensor.

5.2 Light Sensor

A light sensor detects the intensity of incident light.

Examples:

  • Light Dependent Resistor (LDR).
  • Photodiode.
  • Phototransistor.

5.3 Pressure Sensor

A pressure sensor measures the pressure of a gas or liquid. It is used in industrial monitoring, automotive systems and medical equipment.

5.4 Proximity Sensor

A proximity sensor detects the presence or nearness of an object without necessarily making physical contact.

Examples:

  • Inductive proximity sensor.
  • Capacitive proximity sensor.
  • Infrared proximity sensor.
  • Ultrasonic distance sensor.

5.5 Motion and Acceleration Sensor

Accelerometers and gyroscopes are used to detect acceleration, orientation and rotational motion.

5.6 Humidity Sensor

A humidity sensor measures the amount of water vapor present in the surrounding air.

6. Important Sensor Characteristics

Sensor characteristics determine how accurately and reliably a sensor measures a physical quantity.

Characteristic Description
Range Minimum and maximum measurable values.
Accuracy Closeness of a measurement to the true value.
Precision Closeness of repeated measurements to one another.
Resolution Smallest change that can be distinguished.
Sensitivity Change in output per unit change in input.
Linearity Closeness of the sensor response to a straight-line relationship.
Response Time Time required to respond to a change in the measured quantity.
Hysteresis Difference in output for the same input depending on the direction of change.
Repeatability Ability to produce consistent results under the same conditions.
Drift Gradual change in sensor output over time.

Example of Sensitivity

Suppose a temperature sensor produces an output change of 10 mV for every 1°C increase. 

Sensitivity = Change in Output / Change in Input

             = 10 mV / 1°C

             = 10 mV/°C

9. ADC-Based Sensor Measurement

An Analog-to-Digital Converter converts an analog voltage into a digital number that the processor can use.

ADC Conversion Formula

For an ideal unipolar N-bit ADC:

LSB size = VREF / 2N

Approximate digital code: Code = Vin × (2N - 1) / VREF

The result is limited to the valid ADC code range. Exact transfer behavior and endpoint conventions depend on the ADC implementation.

Example: 10-Bit ADC

ADC Resolution = 10 bits

Number of Levels = 2^10

                  = 1024

Reference Voltage = 3.3 V

Input Voltage = 1.65 V

Digital Code ≈ 1.65 × 1023 / 3.3

             ≈ 511.5

             ≈ 512

Sensor Measurement Steps

  1. Read the sensor output voltage.
  2. Ensure the signal is within the ADC input range.
  3. Select the required ADC channel.
  4. Start the conversion.
  5. Wait for conversion completion.
  6. Read the digital result.
  7. Convert the digital value into the required physical unit using the sensor's transfer function.

10. Example: Temperature Sensor Interfacing

Consider an analog temperature sensor whose output increases by 10 mV for every 1°C.

For an ideal sensor with a zero-voltage offset: 

Temperature = Sensor Voltage / 10 mV

Example:

Sensor Voltage = 250 mV

Temperature = 250 / 10

            = 25°C

12. Classification of Actuators

12.1 Electrical Actuators

Electrical actuators use electrical energy to produce mechanical or other physical action.

Examples:

  • DC motors.
  • Stepper motors.
  • Servo motors.
  • Solenoids.
  • Relays.

12.2 Hydraulic Actuators

Hydraulic actuators use pressurized fluid to produce mechanical movement.

Applications:

  • Heavy machinery.
  • Industrial presses.
  • Construction equipment.

12.3 Pneumatic Actuators

Pneumatic actuators use compressed air or gas to generate mechanical movement.

Applications:

  • Industrial automation.
  • Robotic grippers.
  • Packaging machinery.

12.4 Thermal Actuators

Thermal actuators use temperature changes or thermal expansion to produce movement.

13. Common Types of Electrical Actuators

13.1 DC Motor

A DC motor converts electrical energy into rotational mechanical energy.

Its speed can be controlled using Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) with a suitable motor driver.

13.2 Stepper Motor

A stepper motor rotates through discrete angular steps in response to electrical control pulses.

It is useful in positioning applications.

13.3 Servo Motor

A servo motor is used in controlled position, speed or torque applications. Many hobby servo motors accept a periodic control pulse whose width specifies the commanded position.

13.4 Relay

A relay is an electrically operated switch. It allows a low-power control circuit to switch another electrical circuit through appropriately rated contacts.

13.5 Solenoid

A solenoid converts electrical energy into linear mechanical movement using a magnetic field.

14. Actuator Interfacing with Microcontrollers

Most actuators require more current or voltage than a microcontroller GPIO pin can safely provide. Therefore, an appropriate driver circuit is generally required.

Why Is a Driver Circuit Required?

  • To provide the required load current.
  • To switch higher supply voltages.
  • To protect the microcontroller output.
  • To provide suitable electrical isolation where needed.
  • To handle inductive load transients.

15. Motor Driver Circuits

15.1 Transistor Driver

A transistor can be used as an electronic switch to control a motor or relay from a microcontroller.

The microcontroller provides the control signal, while the transistor switches the load current from the external supply.

15.2 MOSFET Driver

A suitable MOSFET can switch motor current efficiently. Its gate-drive voltage, current rating, switching losses and thermal requirements must be considered.

15.3 H-Bridge Driver

An H-bridge is a circuit arrangement used to control the direction of a DC motor by reversing the polarity across its terminals.

H-Bridge Concept

High-Side Switch 1
High-Side Switch 2
Motor Terminal A
DC Motor
Motor Terminal B
Low-Side Switch 1
Low-Side Switch 2

Different switch combinations reverse the motor's applied voltage.

H-bridge switching must include appropriate dead time or interlocking to prevent shoot-through. Never turn on both switches in the same bridge leg simultaneously.

16. Relay Interfacing

A relay coil is an inductive load and normally requires more current than a microcontroller GPIO pin can supply.

Basic Relay Driver Arrangement

Microcontroller GPIO
↓
Transistor Driver
↓
Relay Coil

A suitable flyback protection arrangement is commonly used with a DC relay coil.

Flyback Diode

When current through a relay coil is interrupted, the collapsing magnetic field can produce a high-voltage transient.

A correctly oriented flyback diode provides a path for the inductive current and helps protect the switching transistor.

17. PWM Control of Actuators

Pulse Width Modulation controls the average power delivered to a load by varying the duty cycle of a digital waveform.

Duty Cycle

Duty Cycle = (ON Time / Total Period) × 100

Example:

ON Time = 4 ms

Total Period = 10 ms

Duty Cycle = (4 / 10) × 100

           = 40%

Applications

  • DC motor speed control.
  • LED brightness control.
  • Power control for suitable heating elements.
  • Servo motor command generation.
PWM provides a control waveform. The actual electrical power delivered to an actuator depends on the driver circuit, supply voltage, load characteristics and switching frequency.

19. Closed-Loop Control System

A closed-loop control system uses sensor feedback to compare the actual output with the desired reference value.

The controller uses the resulting error to adjust the actuator command.

Closed-Loop Control Architecture

Set Point
↓
Controller
↓
Actuator
↓
Physical Process
↓
Sensor Feedback

Sensor feedback is returned to the controller for comparison with the set point.

Example: Temperature Control

  1. The user sets the desired temperature to 25°C.
  2. The temperature sensor measures the actual temperature.
  3. The controller compares the measured value with 25°C.
  4. The controller calculates the required heating or cooling action.
  5. The actuator operates the heater or cooling system.
  6. The sensor measures the updated temperature.
  7. The process repeats.

21. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a sensor?

A sensor detects a physical quantity and converts it into a measurable signal.

Q2. What is an actuator?

An actuator converts an electrical control signal into physical action.

Q3. What is the difference between analog and digital sensors?

Analog sensors produce continuously varying signals, while digital sensors provide information in digital form.

Q4. What is signal conditioning?

It is the processing of a sensor signal through operations such as amplification, filtering and scaling.

Q5. Why is an ADC required?

An ADC converts an analog voltage into a digital value that a microcontroller can process.

Q6. What is sensor sensitivity?

It is the change in sensor output per unit change in the measured input.

Q7. Why is a driver circuit required for a motor?

A motor generally requires more current and power than a GPIO pin can provide.

Q8. What is an H-bridge?

It is a switching circuit used to reverse the polarity applied to a DC motor.

Q9. What is a flyback diode?

It provides a path for inductive current when a switching device interrupts current through a DC inductive load.

Q10. What is closed-loop control?

It is a control method that uses feedback to adjust system operation.

Q11. What is the difference between a stepper motor and a servo motor?

A stepper motor moves in discrete steps. A servo system uses feedback or an internal position-control mechanism to achieve a commanded position.

Q12. Why is signal filtering required?

Filtering reduces unwanted noise and improves the quality of measurements.

24. Lesson Summary

  • Sensors detect physical quantities and produce signals.
  • Actuators convert control signals into physical actions.
  • Sensors may provide analog or digital outputs.
  • Important sensor characteristics include accuracy, sensitivity, resolution and response time.
  • Signal conditioning prepares sensor outputs for processing.
  • ADC converts analog signals into digital values.
  • Digital sensors may communicate through GPIO, I2C, SPI or UART.
  • Actuators include motors, relays, solenoids and other devices.
  • Driver circuits provide the required electrical power to actuators.
  • PWM can control power delivered to suitable loads.
  • Closed-loop systems use sensor feedback for control.
Learning Outcome: You should now be able to distinguish sensors and actuators, explain their classifications, understand signal conditioning and ADC-based interfacing, and describe actuator driver circuits in embedded systems.
