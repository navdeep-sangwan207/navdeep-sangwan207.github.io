Chapter 18: Embedded System Design, Debugging and Projects

Level: Advanced

Understand power consumption, low-power operating modes, clock management, peripheral control, sleep strategies and energy-efficient firmware design.

1. Introduction to Low-Power Embedded Systems

Low-power embedded system design focuses on reducing electrical energy consumption while maintaining the required functionality and performance.

It is particularly important in battery-operated devices, wireless sensor nodes, portable electronics, remote monitoring systems and industrial equipment.

Why Low-Power Design Is Important

  • Increases battery operating time.
  • Reduces heat generation.
  • Improves energy efficiency.
  • Reduces battery replacement frequency.
  • Supports compact power supply designs.
  • Can improve long-term system reliability.
Example: A remote temperature sensor may need to measure temperature every few minutes and transmit the result wirelessly. Keeping the processor active continuously wastes energy. A better design performs the measurement, transmits the result and enters a low-power state between measurement cycles.

2. Sources of Power Consumption

An embedded system consumes power through its processor, memory, peripherals, external components and power conversion circuitry.

2.1 Processor Power

The CPU consumes energy while executing instructions. Higher operating frequency and switching activity generally increase dynamic power consumption.

2.2 Memory Power

Memory access and storage circuits consume power. SRAM, Flash and external memory have different operating and standby characteristics.

2.3 Peripheral Power

Timers, ADCs, UARTs, SPI, I2C, PWM and other peripheral blocks may consume power when enabled.

2.4 External Component Power

Sensors, displays, communication modules, LEDs, relays and motor drivers may consume significant power.

2.5 Power Supply Losses

Voltage regulators and DC-DC converters are not perfectly efficient. Their losses contribute to total system power consumption.

Power Source Possible Optimization
CPU Reduce unnecessary execution and frequency
Memory Reduce unnecessary accesses
Peripherals Disable unused peripheral blocks
Sensors Use duty cycling and standby modes
Display Reduce brightness and display-on time
Power supply Select efficient regulation and conversion

3. Power and Energy Fundamentals

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or consumed.

DC Power Formula

For a DC load: 

P = V × I

Where:

  • P = Power in watts (W)
  • V = Voltage in volts (V)
  • I = Current in amperes (A)

Energy Formula

E = P × t

Where E is energy and t is operating time. If power is in watts and time is in hours, energy is in watt-hours (Wh).

Example Calculation

Assume a device operates at 3.3 V and consumes 20 mA. 

Voltage = 3.3 V
Current = 20 mA = 0.020 A

Power = V × I
      = 3.3 × 0.020
      = 0.066 W

Power = 66 mW

Battery Operating Time

A simplified battery-life estimate is: 

Operating Time (hours)
    = Battery Capacity (Ah) / Average Current (A)
This is an ideal estimate. Actual battery life depends on usable battery capacity, discharge characteristics, temperature, regulator efficiency, peak current and battery ageing.

4. Dynamic and Static Power Consumption

4.1 Dynamic Power

Dynamic power is associated with switching activity in digital circuits.

A commonly used approximation is:

Pdynamic = α × C × V² × f

Where:

  • α = Switching activity factor
  • C = Effective switched capacitance
  • V = Supply voltage
  • f = Operating frequency

Dynamic power can be reduced by lowering unnecessary switching activity, operating frequency or supply voltage within the device's valid limits.

4.2 Static Power

Static power is consumed even when the circuit is not actively switching. It is mainly associated with leakage currents and other static circuit currents.

Dynamic Power Static Power
Related to switching activity Related to leakage and static current
Generally increases with frequency Can remain present when idle
Can be reduced by reducing switching Can be reduced through suitable device and operating conditions

5. Low-Power Operating Modes

Microcontrollers commonly provide operating modes that reduce power consumption when the CPU or peripherals are not required.

5.1 Normal Operating Mode

The CPU and configured peripherals operate normally. This mode provides full application functionality but generally consumes more power than sleep modes.

5.2 Idle Mode

In an idle mode, the CPU stops executing instructions while selected peripheral functions may continue operating. An enabled interrupt can wake the CPU.

5.3 Power-Down Mode

In a power-down mode, more internal activity is stopped to reduce power consumption. Wake-up sources and retained hardware states depend on the microcontroller.

5.4 Deep Sleep or Standby Modes

Some microcontrollers provide additional low-power states with different trade-offs between power consumption, wake-up latency and retained state.

Mode CPU Activity Wake-Up Behaviour
Normal Executing instructions Not applicable
Idle CPU execution stopped Supported interrupt sources
Power-down Most internal activity stopped Device-supported wake-up sources
Deep sleep / standby Highly reduced activity Device-specific wake-up sequence
Mode names and retained peripherals vary between microcontrollers. Do not assume that every device supports all four modes or that every peripheral remains active in a low-power state.

6. LPC2129 Low-Power Modes

The LPC2129 provides Idle and Power-down operating modes to reduce power consumption.

Idle Mode

  • CPU execution is suspended.
  • Peripheral operation can continue according to the device configuration.
  • An enabled interrupt can terminate Idle mode.
  • Firmware can resume execution after the wake-up event.

Power-Down Mode

  • Internal activity is reduced further.
  • The main oscillator and clock operation are affected according to the device specification.
  • Only supported wake-up sources can restore operation.
  • Wake-up and restart behaviour must be handled by firmware.

PCON Register

The LPC2129 Power Control Register (PCON) controls entry into low-power modes.

Bit Name Function
0 IDLE Requests entry into Idle mode
1 PD Requests entry into Power-down mode
Configure wake-up sources and interrupt conditions before entering a low-power mode. Follow the LPC2129 user manual for the exact entry and wake-up sequence.

7. Clock Management for Low-Power Design

Clock management is an important method of reducing power consumption in microcontrollers.

7.1 Reduce Operating Frequency

Lowering the CPU clock frequency can reduce dynamic switching power, although the effect on total system power depends on voltage, peripheral activity and the work required.

7.2 Disable Unused Clock Sources

Where supported, unused clock sources and clock branches can be disabled to reduce unnecessary switching activity.

7.3 Use Peripheral Clock Control

Peripherals that are not required can be disabled through the appropriate peripheral clock control mechanism, if provided by the microcontroller.

7.4 Use Event-Driven Processing

Instead of continuously checking a condition, use interrupts or other supported events to perform work only when necessary.

LPC2129 project clock: CCLK = 60 MHz and PCLK = 15 MHz. These are the configured project values. A lower clock configuration may be considered if the application timing requirements permit it, but PLL and peripheral clock settings must be recalculated together.

8. Peripheral Power Management

Peripheral power can be reduced by enabling only the hardware blocks required for the current application operation.

Peripheral Power-Saving Approach
ADC Perform conversions only when required and use supported power control
UART Transmit only required data and avoid unnecessary continuous activity
SPI Disable transfers and associated external devices when idle
I2C Avoid unnecessary bus activity and manage target-device standby modes
PWM Disable unused outputs and external loads where safe
Timer Use suitable timer intervals and disable unused timers
Disabling a peripheral must not interfere with a safety-critical function or an active communication transaction. Check the peripheral's hardware state before removing its clock or power.

9. GPIO and External Component Power

GPIO pins can consume power when driving loads. External LEDs, pull-up resistors, sensor interfaces and other circuits also contribute to system current.

LED Power Consumption

An LED connected to a GPIO pin draws current through its current-limiting resistor. 

LED Current = (Supply Voltage - LED Forward Voltage)
              / Series Resistance

Example

Assume a 3.3 V supply, an LED forward voltage of 2.0 V and a 1 kΩ resistor. 

Current = (3.3 - 2.0) / 1000
        = 0.0013 A
        = 1.3 mA

GPIO Power-Saving Practices

  • Use appropriate current-limiting resistors.
  • Avoid unnecessary LED illumination.
  • Place external devices in standby when supported.
  • Set unused GPIO pins to a documented safe state.
  • Avoid leaving inputs floating.
  • Check pin current and total port current limits.

10. Duty Cycling

Duty cycling is a technique in which a device operates for a short period and remains inactive or in a low-power state for the rest of the cycle.

Duty Cycle Formula

Duty Cycle (%) = (Active Time / Total Period) × 100

Example

Assume a sensor node operates for 100 ms and sleeps for 900 ms. 

Active Time = 100 ms
Sleep Time  = 900 ms
Total Period = 1000 ms

Duty Cycle = (100 / 1000) × 100
           = 10%

The device is active for 10% of the cycle and inactive for 90%.

A lower duty cycle can reduce average energy consumption, but the design must still meet sampling rate, response time and communication requirements.

11. Average Current and Battery-Life Estimation

Average current is useful when a system alternates between active and sleep states.

Average Current Formula

Iavg = (Iactive × Tactive + Isleep × Tsleep)
       / (Tactive + Tsleep)

Example Calculation

Assume:

  • Active current = 20 mA
  • Sleep current = 0.5 mA
  • Active time = 100 ms
  • Sleep time = 900 ms
Iavg = (20 × 100 + 0.5 × 900) / 1000

     = (2000 + 450) / 1000

     = 2.45 mA

The estimated average current is 2.45 mA.

Ideal Battery-Life Estimate

For an ideal 1000 mAh battery: 

Operating Time = 1000 mAh / 2.45 mA

               ≈ 408.16 hours

               ≈ 17 days
This is a mathematical estimate, not a guaranteed battery life. Actual results depend on battery capacity under the load profile, conversion losses, sleep current, self-discharge, temperature and other external loads.

12. Interrupt-Driven Low-Power Design

Interrupt-driven design allows the processor to remain inactive until an event requires attention.

Polling-Based Approach

while (1)
{
    if (sensor_ready)
    {
        Read_Sensor();
    }
}

In this example, the CPU repeatedly checks the condition. This can waste processing time and energy.

Event-Driven Approach

volatile unsigned int sensor_event = 0U;

void Sensor_ISR(void)
{
    sensor_event = 1U;
}

int main(void)
{
    while (1)
    {
        Enter_Idle_Mode();

        if (sensor_event != 0U)
        {
            sensor_event = 0U;
            Read_Sensor();
        }
    }
}

This is a conceptual example. The interrupt source, interrupt flag handling, wake-up behaviour and critical-section requirements must be implemented for the actual hardware.

The volatile qualifier helps ensure that accesses to the shared event variable are not optimized away. It does not make compound operations atomic or automatically provide synchronization.

13. Low-Power Firmware Design Techniques

  • Use event-driven programming instead of continuous polling where appropriate.
  • Reduce unnecessary CPU execution.
  • Use suitable sleep modes between operations.
  • Disable unused peripherals when safe.
  • Reduce unnecessary communication activity.
  • Use efficient algorithms and avoid repeated calculations.
  • Reduce display brightness or update frequency where appropriate.
  • Use sensor standby modes when supported.
  • Minimize GPIO switching.
  • Choose an appropriate operating clock frequency.
  • Measure actual current during active and sleep states.
  • Verify that low-power changes do not violate real-time requirements.

14. Power Measurement Techniques

Power optimization should be based on measurements rather than assumptions.

Multimeter

A digital multimeter can measure supply current when connected correctly in series with the load.

Oscilloscope

An oscilloscope can help observe current-sense resistor voltage, supply transients and wake-up activity.

Current Measurement Tool

A suitable power analyzer or low-power current measurement instrument can capture small sleep currents and short active-current peaks.

Measurement Checklist

  1. Measure current in normal operation.
  2. Measure current in each low-power mode.
  3. Measure wake-up current peaks.
  4. Measure external sensor and communication loads.
  5. Calculate average current for the intended duty cycle.
  6. Compare results with the battery budget.

15. Low-Power Design Challenges

Challenge Possible Solution
Long wake-up time Select a suitable sleep mode and wake-up strategy
High active current Optimize CPU frequency and active time
High sleep current Check GPIO states and external loads
Frequent wake-ups Review event frequency and batching opportunities
Missed real-time events Balance sleep duration with response requirements
Communication energy cost Reduce unnecessary transmissions

17. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is low-power embedded system design?

It is the process of reducing energy consumption while maintaining the required functionality and performance.

Q2. What is dynamic power?

Dynamic power is associated with switching activity in digital circuits.

Q3. What is static power?

Static power is consumed due to leakage and other non-switching current paths.

Q4. What is Idle mode?

Idle mode suspends CPU execution while selected peripheral functions may continue operating.

Q5. What is Power-down mode?

Power-down mode reduces internal activity further to lower power consumption.

Q6. What is duty cycling?

Duty cycling operates a device for a limited portion of each cycle and keeps it inactive or in a low-power state for the remaining time.

Q7. What is average current?

Average current is the time-weighted current consumed across the operating cycle.

Q8. Why are interrupts useful in low-power design?

Interrupts allow the CPU to wait for events instead of continuously polling hardware conditions.

Q9. What is clock gating?

Clock gating disables clock delivery to selected circuit blocks to reduce switching activity.

Q10. What is the purpose of the LPC2129 PCON register?

PCON controls entry into the microcontroller's supported low-power modes.

Q11. How can battery life be improved?

Reduce active time, select suitable low-power modes, minimize unnecessary communication and optimize external component power consumption.

Q12. Why must power consumption be measured?

Actual current depends on the complete hardware, operating conditions and firmware behaviour. Measurement verifies the effectiveness of optimization.

20. Mini-Project: Low-Power Temperature Monitoring System

Objective

Design a temperature monitoring system that measures a sensor periodically, displays or transmits the result and reduces power consumption between measurements.

Required Modules

  • LPC2129 microcontroller.
  • Temperature sensor connected to an ADC input.
  • LCD or UART output.
  • Timer or supported wake-up source.
  • Power supply or battery.

Functional Sequence

  1. Initialize the microcontroller and required peripherals.
  2. Configure the sensor interface.
  3. Read the temperature measurement.
  4. Validate and process the measurement.
  5. Display or transmit the result.
  6. Place external components in standby where supported.
  7. Enter an appropriate low-power mode.
  8. Wake up for the next measurement cycle.
  9. Repeat the measurement and monitoring process.

Project Evaluation

  • Correct sensor sampling.
  • Correct wake-up and scheduling behaviour.
  • Safe peripheral shutdown and restart.
  • Average current estimation.
  • Measurement of actual power consumption on hardware.
For the Keil simulator-only workflow, demonstrate the firmware sequence and inspect supported registers. Physical power reduction and battery-life claims require actual hardware measurements.
