Chapter 18: Embedded System Design, Debugging and Projects

Level: Advanced

Understand electromagnetic interference, electromagnetic compatibility, grounding, shielding, PCB layout, decoupling and electrical protection in embedded systems.

1. Introduction to EMI and EMC

Embedded systems contain high-speed digital signals, switching circuits, clocks, communication interfaces and power electronics. These circuits can generate unwanted electromagnetic energy or become disturbed by electromagnetic energy from other sources.

What is EMI?

Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) is unwanted electromagnetic disturbance that affects the operation of an electronic circuit or system.

What is EMC?

Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC) is the ability of equipment to operate satisfactorily in its electromagnetic environment without introducing unacceptable electromagnetic disturbance into that environment.

EMIEMC
Unwanted electromagnetic disturbanceAbility to operate reliably in an electromagnetic environment
Can cause communication errors or resetsFocuses on controlling emissions and improving immunity
Can originate from internal or external sourcesRequires system-level design and verification
Example: A microcontroller-based control board installed near a motor drive may experience interference from motor switching. The result may be incorrect sensor readings, communication errors or unexpected resets.

2. Sources of Electromagnetic Interference

2.1 Internal Sources

  • Microcontroller clock signals.
  • High-speed digital switching.
  • Switching power supplies.
  • PWM motor-control circuits.
  • Relay and solenoid switching.
  • Fast signal transitions on PCB traces.

2.2 External Sources

  • Electric motors and motor drives.
  • Mobile phones and radio transmitters.
  • Nearby power cables.
  • Industrial switching equipment.
  • Lightning and electrostatic discharge.
  • Wireless communication equipment.

2.3 Conducted and Radiated Interference

TypeDescriptionExample
Conducted EMIInterference travels through electrical conductors.Noise on a shared power supply.
Radiated EMIInterference travels through electromagnetic fields.Noise coupled from a nearby switching circuit.

3. Effects of EMI on Embedded Systems

Electromagnetic interference can cause different types of hardware and firmware problems.

  • Unexpected microcontroller resets.
  • Corrupted memory or communication data.
  • UART framing errors.
  • I2C communication failures.
  • Incorrect ADC readings.
  • Unstable sensor measurements.
  • False interrupt triggering.
  • Unexpected GPIO transitions.
  • Malfunctioning control systems.
An intermittent fault that appears only when a motor, relay or switching power supply operates may indicate an EMI, grounding or supply-integrity problem. It still requires systematic diagnosis rather than assuming EMI is the cause.

4. Electromagnetic Compatibility: Emissions and Immunity

4.1 Electromagnetic Emissions

Emissions are electromagnetic disturbances generated by equipment and released into its environment.

EMC design attempts to keep emissions within applicable limits.

4.2 Electromagnetic Immunity

Immunity is the ability of equipment to continue operating correctly when exposed to electromagnetic disturbances.

4.3 EMC Design Objectives

  • Reduce unwanted electromagnetic emissions.
  • Improve resistance to external disturbances.
  • Maintain stable power and signal integrity.
  • Prevent interference between circuit sections.
  • Meet applicable product and regulatory requirements.
Design AreaObjective
Emissions controlReduce electromagnetic energy generated by the equipment.
Immunity improvementPrevent external interference from disrupting operation.
PCB designControl current paths, coupling and signal integrity.
Enclosure designProvide suitable shielding and mechanical protection.

5. Grounding Techniques

Grounding provides electrical reference and return-current paths. Poor grounding can create unwanted voltage differences and interference.

5.1 Common Ground

Multiple circuit sections share a common reference. The return-current paths must be planned to avoid sensitive signals sharing high-current paths.

5.2 Star Grounding

In a star-ground arrangement, selected circuit return paths connect to a common point. It can help prevent high-current load returns from disturbing sensitive analog references.

5.3 Ground Plane

A ground plane is a continuous conductive PCB region connected to the circuit ground network. It can provide a low-impedance return path when designed correctly.

5.4 Analog and Digital Grounding

Analog and digital sections may require careful placement and return-current management. Separate ground labels do not automatically improve performance; the connection strategy must be designed around the actual current paths and converter requirements.

Important: Avoid splitting a ground plane without a clear signal-integrity reason. A signal crossing a gap in its return path can create a large current loop and increase interference.

6. Decoupling and Bypass Capacitors

Decoupling capacitors help supply transient current to an IC and reduce supply-voltage disturbances caused by switching activity.

Why Decoupling Is Required

  • Digital ICs draw rapidly changing current.
  • PCB power traces have resistance and inductance.
  • Supply voltage can fluctuate during switching.
  • Noise can affect processor and peripheral operation.

Typical Decoupling Arrangement

       Power Supply
            |
       Power Trace
            |
      +-------------+
      | Microcontroller
      |             |
      |         VDD Pin
      +-------------+
            |
       +----+----+
       |         |
      100 nF    GND
       |
      GND

A small ceramic capacitor, often 100 nF, is commonly placed close to each relevant IC supply pin. Additional bulk capacitance may be required near the IC or power entry, depending on the design.

Design Practices

  • Place decoupling capacitors close to the supply pins.
  • Keep capacitor-to-pin and capacitor-to-ground paths short.
  • Use suitable capacitor voltage and dielectric ratings.
  • Follow the microcontroller manufacturer's layout recommendations.
  • Consider bulk capacitance for lower-frequency load transients.
A capacitor value alone does not guarantee effective decoupling. Placement, PCB geometry, mounting inductance and the IC's current profile also matter.

7. PCB Layout Techniques for EMI Reduction

PCB layout strongly affects electromagnetic emissions, susceptibility and signal integrity.

7.1 Reduce High-Current Loop Area

Keep switching-current loops compact. Large loops can act as efficient antennas and increase radiated interference.

7.2 Keep Clock Traces Short

High-frequency clock traces should be routed carefully, with a continuous return path and suitable separation from sensitive analog signals.

7.3 Separate Noisy and Sensitive Circuits

  • Keep switching power circuits away from sensitive analog inputs.
  • Place motor-driver circuits away from low-level sensor circuitry.
  • Route communication signals with suitable return paths.
  • Avoid unnecessary parallel routing of noisy and sensitive traces.

7.4 Use Appropriate Trace Width

Trace width must be selected based on current, temperature rise, copper thickness and manufacturing constraints.

7.5 Minimize Unnecessary Stubs

Long branches and stubs can create reflections and signal-integrity problems, especially on fast digital interfaces.

8. Shielding Techniques

Shielding uses conductive or magnetic materials to reduce coupling between an electromagnetic source and a sensitive circuit.

8.1 Conductive Shielding

Conductive enclosures or shields can reduce electric-field coupling and electromagnetic radiation when properly designed and bonded.

8.2 Magnetic Shielding

Special materials with suitable magnetic properties can reduce magnetic-field coupling in selected applications.

8.3 Cable Shielding

Shielded cables can reduce external coupling into signal conductors. The termination and bonding arrangement must be selected according to the frequency range, installation and system grounding strategy.

Shielding Considerations

  • Use an enclosure with suitable material and construction.
  • Minimize gaps and apertures where practical.
  • Provide suitable bonding between enclosure sections.
  • Plan cable shield termination carefully.
  • Consider connector and enclosure bonding.
Shielding is not a substitute for good circuit layout, grounding, filtering and cable routing.

9. Filtering Techniques

Filters can reduce unwanted frequency components in power and signal paths.

9.1 Low-Pass Filter

A low-pass filter passes lower-frequency components and attenuates higher-frequency components.

9.2 High-Pass Filter

A high-pass filter passes higher-frequency components and attenuates lower-frequency components.

9.3 Band-Pass Filter

A band-pass filter passes a selected range of frequencies.

9.4 EMI Filters

EMI filters commonly use capacitors, inductors, ferrite components or combinations of these elements to reduce unwanted conducted noise.

FilterTypical Application
Low-passReducing high-frequency noise on sensor signals.
High-passBlocking DC while passing higher-frequency signals.
Band-passSelecting a desired communication frequency band.
Common-mode chokeAttenuating common-mode conducted noise.
Ferrite beadReducing high-frequency noise on suitable power or signal paths.
Filter selection requires consideration of source impedance, load impedance, frequency range, signal bandwidth, current rating and component parasitics.

10. Protection Against ESD and Electrical Transients

10.1 Electrostatic Discharge (ESD)

ESD is a rapid transfer of electrical charge between objects at different electrical potentials.

It can damage semiconductor devices or cause temporary malfunction.

10.2 Transient Voltage Suppression

Transient Voltage Suppressor (TVS) diodes are commonly used to clamp voltage transients on exposed power and signal interfaces.

10.3 Reverse Polarity Protection

Reverse-polarity protection prevents damage when a power supply is connected with the wrong polarity.

10.4 Overvoltage Protection

Suitable protection circuits help prevent supply voltages from exceeding the safe operating range of the electronics.

10.5 Overcurrent Protection

Fuses, resettable fuses and current-limiting circuits can protect against excessive current under specified fault conditions.

Protection MethodPurpose
TVS diodeClamp fast voltage transients.
FuseInterrupt excessive current.
Resettable fuseLimit sustained overcurrent and recover after the fault is removed, within its ratings.
Reverse-polarity circuitProtect against incorrect supply polarity.
Input filteringReduce selected conducted disturbances.

11. Relay and Motor Switching Protection

Relays, solenoids and motors are inductive loads. When current through an inductive load is interrupted, the collapsing magnetic field can produce a high voltage transient.

Flyback Diode

A flyback diode is commonly connected across a DC relay coil to provide a current path when the switching transistor turns off.

       +V
        |
     Relay Coil
        |
        +------ Transistor
        |          |
        |         GND
        |
     Flyback Diode
   (connected across coil,
    reverse-biased normally)

Additional Protection

  • Use a suitable transistor or MOSFET driver.
  • Check the inductive load voltage and current ratings.
  • Use an appropriate suppression network.
  • Keep high-current switching paths compact.
  • Separate load return currents from sensitive analog paths.
A flyback diode is suitable for many DC relay coils, but it is not a universal solution for every inductive load. AC loads, motors and fast-release requirements may need a different suppression network.

12. Signal Integrity and Transmission Lines

Signal integrity describes the quality of an electrical signal as it travels through an interconnection.

Common Signal Integrity Problems

  • Ringing.
  • Overshoot and undershoot.
  • Signal reflections.
  • Crosstalk.
  • Ground bounce.
  • Slow or distorted signal edges.

Transmission Line Effects

When signal propagation time is significant compared with the signal transition time, a PCB trace or cable may need to be treated as a transmission line.

Possible Solutions

  • Use controlled-impedance routing where required.
  • Provide appropriate termination.
  • Maintain continuous reference planes.
  • Reduce unnecessary stubs.
  • Control signal edge rates where supported.

13. EMC Considerations for LPC2129 Interfaces

The LPC2129 can communicate with external circuits through GPIO, UART, SPI, I2C, ADC, PWM and CAN interfaces. Each interface has different electrical and layout requirements.

InterfaceEMC Considerations
GPIOControl edge rates and avoid long, noisy connections.
UARTUse suitable signal levels, cable routing and interface protection.
SPIKeep clock and data paths short and provide a suitable return path.
I2CChoose suitable pull-ups and manage bus capacitance.
ADCProtect analog inputs and minimize noise coupling.
PWMManage switching currents and output routing.
CANUse a suitable transceiver, differential routing and termination.

CAN Interface Example

The LPC2129 CAN controller requires an external CAN transceiver to connect to the physical CAN bus. The transceiver interfaces the controller's logic-level signals with the differential CANH and CANL bus.

For CAN wiring, use the correct bus topology and termination. High-speed CAN commonly uses 120 Ω termination at each physical end of the bus, subject to the network design.

14. EMC Testing and Verification

EMC testing evaluates equipment emissions and immunity under specified test conditions.

Emission Testing

  • Conducted emissions testing.
  • Radiated emissions testing.

Immunity Testing

  • Electrostatic discharge immunity.
  • Radiated RF electromagnetic field immunity.
  • Electrical fast transient immunity.
  • Surge immunity.
  • Conducted RF immunity.
  • Voltage dips and interruptions where applicable.

Common EMC Standards

Standards depend on product type, intended environment and market. Examples include:

  • IEC 61000-4 series for electromagnetic immunity test methods.
  • CISPR standards for emissions requirements.
  • Product-specific standards for industrial, automotive and household equipment.
The applicable standard, test level, performance criterion and regulatory requirement must be selected for the actual product and market. Listing a standard does not establish compliance.

15. Practical EMC Debugging Procedure

  1. Identify the operating condition under which the fault occurs.
  2. Check whether motors, relays or switching supplies are active.
  3. Inspect power supply stability and grounding.
  4. Check decoupling capacitor placement and connections.
  5. Inspect signal and power routing.
  6. Check cable routing and shielding.
  7. Observe supply and signal waveforms using suitable instruments.
  8. Apply controlled changes one at a time.
  9. Repeat the test under the same operating conditions.
  10. Document the results and verify the correction.

16. Practical Design Exercise: Noise-Resistant Sensor Interface

Objective

Design a sensor interface that is less susceptible to noise from nearby switching circuits.

Design Requirements

  • Use an appropriate sensor input circuit.
  • Provide suitable supply decoupling.
  • Use an appropriate analog filter if the sensor bandwidth allows it.
  • Keep analog signal routing away from noisy switching paths.
  • Provide a suitable ground return.
  • Protect exposed connectors against applicable electrical disturbances.

Verification

  1. Measure the sensor signal under normal conditions.
  2. Operate the nearby switching load.
  3. Observe the sensor signal and supply voltage.
  4. Compare readings before and after filtering or layout changes.
  5. Confirm that the filter does not remove useful signal information.

18. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is EMI?

Electromagnetic Interference is unwanted electromagnetic disturbance that affects electronic equipment.

Q2. What is EMC?

Electromagnetic Compatibility is the ability of equipment to operate satisfactorily in its electromagnetic environment without causing unacceptable interference to other equipment.

Q3. Differentiate conducted and radiated EMI.

Conducted EMI travels through electrical connections, while radiated EMI travels through electromagnetic fields.

Q4. What is a decoupling capacitor?

It provides a local transient-current path and helps reduce supply-voltage disturbances near an IC.

Q5. Why is grounding important?

Grounding provides reference and return-current paths. Poor grounding can cause noise and unwanted voltage differences.

Q6. What is shielding?

Shielding uses conductive or magnetic materials to reduce electromagnetic coupling.

Q7. What is ESD?

Electrostatic Discharge is a rapid transfer of electrical charge between objects at different electrical potentials.

Q8. What is a TVS diode?

A Transient Voltage Suppressor diode is used to clamp voltage transients within its specified operating limits.

Q9. Why is a flyback diode used?

It provides a current path for an inductive DC load when its driving switch turns off, reducing the resulting voltage transient.

Q10. What is signal integrity?

Signal integrity describes how accurately an electrical signal maintains its intended characteristics while travelling through an interconnection.

Q11. What is crosstalk?

Crosstalk is unwanted coupling of a signal from one conductor or circuit into another.

Q12. How can EMI be reduced in PCB design?

Use compact current loops, suitable grounding, decoupling, controlled routing, filtering and appropriate shielding.
