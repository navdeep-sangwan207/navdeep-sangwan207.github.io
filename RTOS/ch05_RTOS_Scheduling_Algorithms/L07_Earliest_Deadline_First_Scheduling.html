1. Introduction

Earliest Deadline First (EDF) is a dynamic-priority scheduling algorithm used in real-time operating systems. It selects the Ready task whose absolute deadline is closest in time.

Unlike Rate Monotonic Scheduling, which assigns priorities according to task periods, EDF determines priorities using the deadlines of individual task jobs.

Basic principle: The Ready task with the earliest absolute deadline gets the highest scheduling priority.

EDF is commonly studied for periodic and sporadic real-time workloads, particularly on single-processor systems.

2. What Is a Deadline?

A deadline is the latest time by which a task job must complete its required execution.

Relative Deadline

The relative deadline is the permitted time interval between a job's release and its deadline. 

Relative Deadline = D

Absolute Deadline

The absolute deadline is the actual time instant by which the job must finish. 

Absolute Deadline = Release Time + Relative Deadline

Example

A task job is released at 10 ms and has a relative deadline of 8 ms. 

Release Time = 10 ms

Relative Deadline = 8 ms

Absolute Deadline = 10 + 8

                  = 18 ms

The job must complete by 18 ms.

3. What Is Dynamic Priority Scheduling?

In dynamic-priority scheduling, a task's scheduling priority can change during system execution.

EDF dynamically ranks Ready jobs according to their absolute deadlines.

Task Job Absolute Deadline EDF Preference
Job A 20 ms Highest
Job B 25 ms Second
Job C 40 ms Third

Job A is selected because its absolute deadline is the earliest.

EDF priority is associated with individual jobs. Successive jobs of the same periodic task can have different absolute deadlines.

4. Working Principle of EDF

  1. Identify all tasks and their release times.
  2. Calculate the absolute deadline of each released job.
  3. Identify jobs that are Ready for execution.
  4. Compare their absolute deadlines.
  5. Select the Ready job with the earliest absolute deadline.
  6. If a newly Ready job has an earlier deadline, preempt the running job when preemption is permitted.
  7. Repeat the selection process as jobs are released and completed.

5. EDF Numerical Example: Priority Selection

Consider three Ready jobs with the following deadlines:

Job Execution Time Remaining Absolute Deadline
A 2 ms 15 ms
B 3 ms 12 ms
C 1 ms 20 ms

Step 1: Compare Deadlines

Job A deadline = 15 ms

Job B deadline = 12 ms

Job C deadline = 20 ms

Step 2: Select the Earliest Deadline

Job B has the earliest absolute deadline of 12 ms.

Step 3: Determine Scheduling Order

First  = Job B

Second = Job A

Third  = Job C
Job B receives the highest EDF scheduling preference because its absolute deadline is the earliest.

6. EDF Numerical Example: Preemptive Scheduling

Consider two jobs on a single processor:

Job Release Time Execution Time Absolute Deadline
A 0 ms 4 ms 10 ms
B 1 ms 2 ms 5 ms

Step 1: At Time 0 ms

Only Job A is Ready, so Job A starts execution.

Step 2: At Time 1 ms

Job B is released. Its deadline is 5 ms, earlier than Job A's deadline of 10 ms.

Under preemptive EDF, Job A is preempted and Job B executes.

Step 3: At Time 3 ms

Job B completes. Job A resumes and finishes at 6 ms.

Execution Timeline

A: 0–1 B: 1–3 A: 3–6

Time: 0 ms → 6 ms

Job Completion Time Deadline Result
A 6 ms 10 ms Met
B 3 ms 5 ms Met
Both jobs complete before their deadlines.

7. EDF CPU Utilization

For independent periodic tasks on one preemptive processor, EDF has an important schedulability result.

Under the classical task model, if each task's relative deadline equals its period, the task set is schedulable by EDF when: 

U = Σ (Ci / Ti) ≤ 1

Where:

  • Ci = Worst-case execution time of task i.
  • Ti = Period of task i.
  • U = Total CPU utilization.
Under these ideal assumptions, EDF can theoretically schedule periodic tasks up to 100% processor utilization on a single processor.

Example

Task Execution Time Period
T1 2 ms 10 ms
T2 3 ms 15 ms
T3 4 ms 20 ms

Calculate Utilization

U1 = 2 / 10 = 0.20

U2 = 3 / 15 = 0.20

U3 = 4 / 20 = 0.20

Utotal = 0.20 + 0.20 + 0.20

       = 0.60

       = 60%

Check Schedulability

Utotal = 0.60

EDF limit = 1.00

0.60 ≤ 1.00
The task set satisfies the classical EDF utilization test, assuming independent periodic tasks, preemptive scheduling, deadlines equal to periods and negligible unmodeled overhead.

8. EDF vs RMS Utilization

EDF and RMS have different schedulability characteristics under their respective classical assumptions.

Feature RMS EDF
Priority policy Fixed Dynamic
Priority basis Task period Absolute deadline
Classical utilization result Sufficient bound depends on task count Up to 100%
Priority reassignment Not normally required As deadlines change
Scheduling complexity Generally simpler priority assignment Requires deadline-based job selection
Typical analysis Fixed-priority schedulability analysis Dynamic-priority schedulability analysis
The 100% EDF result is not a guarantee for every practical application. Blocking, non-preemptive sections, interrupt interference, release jitter and scheduling overhead can reduce schedulable capacity.

9. EDF with Deadlines Different from Periods

In some real-time systems, the relative deadline of a task differs from its period.

For such systems, simply checking: 

U = Σ (Ci / Ti) ≤ 1

is not generally sufficient to establish schedulability.

The relationship between execution time, period and relative deadline must be analyzed using an appropriate demand-based or processor-demand schedulability test.

Example

Parameter Value
Release time 0 ms
Execution time 3 ms
Period 10 ms
Relative deadline 6 ms
Absolute deadline of first job 6 ms

The next job is released at 10 ms, but the first job must complete by 6 ms. The period and deadline are therefore different.

10. EDF Scheduling for Embedded Systems

EDF can be useful in embedded applications where several activities have different completion deadlines.

Example: Embedded Monitoring System

Activity Example Deadline Function
Current Monitoring 5 ms Monitor motor current and detect abnormal conditions.
Temperature Processing 20 ms Process temperature sensor measurements.
Display Refresh 100 ms Update system information on a display.

If all three jobs are Ready, the current-monitoring job has the earliest deadline and is selected first.

A safety-critical protection function should not rely solely on a general-purpose task scheduler if its response time cannot be guaranteed. Independent hardware protection may be required.

11. EDF Implementation Concepts

An EDF scheduler needs to track the absolute deadline of every Ready job.

Basic Scheduler Pseudocode

START

    Initialize task/job list

    WHILE system is running

        Detect newly released jobs

        Calculate absolute deadlines

        Identify all Ready jobs

        Select job with earliest absolute deadline

        IF selected job is different
           from the currently running job

            Perform context switch if required

        END IF

        Execute selected job

        Update remaining execution time

        Remove completed jobs

    END WHILE

STOP

A practical EDF implementation also needs mechanisms for job release, completion, deadline tracking, preemption, blocking and overload handling.

12. EDF and FreeRTOS

Standard FreeRTOS uses fixed-priority scheduling. It does not automatically implement an EDF scheduler simply because tasks have different deadlines.

An EDF design requires additional deadline-aware scheduling logic or a suitable EDF-capable RTOS.

Conceptual EDF Job Structure in C

typedef struct
{
    unsigned int job_id;

    unsigned int release_time;

    unsigned int absolute_deadline;

    unsigned int execution_time;

    unsigned int remaining_time;

} EDF_Job;

Conceptual Selection Logic

int Find_Earliest_Deadline(
    EDF_Job jobs[],
    int count
)
{
    int selected = -1;

    for (int i = 0; i < count; i++)
    {
        if (jobs[i].remaining_time == 0)
        {
            continue;
        }

        if (selected == -1 ||
            jobs[i].absolute_deadline <
            jobs[selected].absolute_deadline)
        {
            selected = i;
        }
    }

    return selected;
}

This function illustrates deadline-based selection among unfinished jobs. A complete scheduler must additionally manage release times, Ready/Blocked states, preemption, job completion and deadline misses.

This is a standalone conceptual C example, not a replacement for the FreeRTOS scheduler and not a complete production EDF implementation.

13. Advantages of EDF

  • Uses actual job deadlines for scheduling decisions.
  • Supports dynamic priority assignment.
  • Can achieve high processor utilization under ideal assumptions.
  • Can respond to changing job deadlines.
  • Suitable for periodic and sporadic real-time task models with appropriate analysis.
  • Provides a clear rule for selecting the next Ready job.

14. Limitations of EDF

  • Requires deadline tracking for individual jobs.
  • Dynamic scheduling decisions can increase implementation complexity.
  • Overload conditions may cause multiple deadline misses.
  • Shared-resource blocking needs careful handling.
  • Deadline changes can cause frequent preemptions.
  • Practical scheduling overhead reduces available processor capacity.
  • Standard FreeRTOS fixed-priority scheduling is not EDF.

16. Common Mistakes

  • Confusing relative deadlines with absolute deadlines.
  • Assuming EDF uses fixed priorities.
  • Selecting the job with the longest deadline first.
  • Assuming EDF automatically guarantees every deadline.
  • Applying the 100% utilization result outside its assumptions.
  • Ignoring execution time and remaining execution time.
  • Assuming standard FreeRTOS uses EDF.
  • Ignoring blocking and scheduler overhead.

17. Lesson Summary

  • EDF is a dynamic-priority scheduling algorithm.
  • The Ready job with the earliest absolute deadline is selected.
  • Absolute deadline = Release time + Relative deadline.
  • EDF can preempt a running job when an earlier-deadline job becomes Ready in a preemptive implementation.
  • Under the classical periodic task model with deadlines equal to periods, utilization up to 100% is schedulable.
  • Different deadlines and periods require additional analysis.
  • EDF and RMS use different priority assignment strategies.
  • Standard FreeRTOS uses fixed-priority scheduling rather than built-in EDF scheduling.
