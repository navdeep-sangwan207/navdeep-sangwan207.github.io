2. I2C Addressing

I2C uses device addresses to identify the slave that should respond to a master transaction. Most commonly used I2C devices have a 7-bit address.

7-Bit Address Format

+-----------------------------+
| A6 | A5 | A4 | A3 | A2 | A1 | A0 | R/W |
+-----------------------------+
       7-Bit Address             1 Bit

The first seven bits represent the slave address. The eighth bit specifies the direction of transfer.

R/W BitOperation
0Master writes to slave.
1Master reads from slave.
The R/W bit is not part of the 7-bit slave address. It is transmitted as part of the address byte on the bus.

3. Example of 7-Bit Addressing

Consider an I2C slave with a 7-bit address of 0x48. 

7-Bit Address = 0x48
Binary         = 1001000

Write Address Byte:
10010000 = 0x90

Read Address Byte:
10010001 = 0x91

The address byte is formed by shifting the 7-bit address left by one bit and placing the R/W bit in bit position zero. 

Address Byte = (7-Bit Address << 1) | R/W

Write:
(0x48 << 1) | 0 = 0x90

Read:
(0x48 << 1) | 1 = 0x91
STM32 HAL master APIs commonly expect the 7-bit address shifted left by one bit, such as (0x48 << 1). The HAL handles the read/write direction internally for those APIs. Always check the exact API documentation.

4. Ten-Bit I2C Addressing

I2C also supports 10-bit addressing. It allows a larger address space than the standard 7-bit addressing scheme.

10-Bit Address Structure

First Address Byte:
11110 A9 A8 R/W

Second Address Byte:
A7 A6 A5 A4 A3 A2 A1 A0

The first byte contains a reserved prefix and the two most significant address bits. The second byte carries the remaining eight bits.

  • 10-bit addressing uses two address bytes.
  • It supports more possible slave addresses.
  • It requires the corresponding transaction sequence.
  • Support depends on the STM32 peripheral and HAL configuration.

5. ACK – Acknowledge

ACK stands for Acknowledge. It indicates that the receiving device has accepted the transmitted byte at the protocol level.

ACK Condition

  • The transmitter releases SDA after sending eight bits.
  • The receiver pulls SDA LOW during the ninth clock pulse.
  • The LOW level represents ACK.
Data Bits:  D7 D6 D5 D4 D3 D2 D1 D0
Clock:      1  2  3  4  5  6  7  8  9

ACK:
SDA = LOW during the ninth clock pulse
ACK indicates that the receiving device acknowledged the byte. It does not guarantee that the application-level operation succeeded.

6. NACK – Not Acknowledge

NACK indicates that the receiver did not acknowledge the transmitted byte.

NACK Condition

  • The receiver leaves SDA HIGH during the ninth clock pulse.
  • The transmitter interprets this as NACK.
  • The master may terminate the transaction or take recovery action.
ConditionSDA at Ninth Clock
ACKLOW
NACKHIGH

Possible Reasons for NACK

  • Incorrect slave address.
  • Slave device is not powered.
  • Device is busy or not ready.
  • Incorrect wiring.
  • Device does not support the requested operation.

7. ACK and NACK During Read Operations

During a master read operation, the slave transmits data and the master receiver controls the acknowledgement after each byte.

  • The master sends ACK when it wants another byte.
  • The master normally sends NACK after the final byte.
  • The master then issues STOP or a repeated START as required.
Slave sends Byte 1
        |
Master sends ACK
        |
Slave sends Byte 2
        |
Master sends ACK
        |
Slave sends Byte 3
        |
Master sends NACK
        |
Master sends STOP
The final NACK informs the slave that the master has finished receiving data.

9. I2C Clock and Timing

The master generates SCL clock pulses to synchronize communication.

Common I2C Clock Modes

ModeMaximum Clock Frequency
Standard Mode100 kHz
Fast Mode400 kHz
Fast Mode Plus1 MHz
High-Speed Mode3.4 MHz

These are standardized mode limits. A particular STM32 and its connected devices may support only a subset of them.

Clock Period

Clock Period = 1 / Clock Frequency

At 100 kHz:

T = 1 / 100000
  = 10 microseconds

At 400 kHz:

T = 1 / 400000
  = 2.5 microseconds

10. I2C Data Transfer Timing

I2C data is normally stable while SCL is HIGH. Data changes while SCL is LOW, except for START and STOP condition generation. 

             ____      ____      ____
SCL  _______|    |____|    |____|    |____

SDA  ----< Data Bit >----< Data Bit >----

Important Timing Parameters

  • Clock HIGH time (tHIGH).
  • Clock LOW time (tLOW).
  • Data setup time (tSU;DAT).
  • Data hold time (tHD;DAT).
  • START setup and hold times.
  • STOP setup time.
  • Bus free time between STOP and START.
Timing limits differ by I2C speed mode. Always use the I2C specification and the selected device datasheet when calculating timing.

11. Clock Stretching

Clock stretching allows a slave to hold SCL LOW to delay the next clock pulse while it prepares data or completes an operation.

Working

  1. The master releases SCL HIGH.
  2. The slave holds SCL LOW.
  3. The master waits while SCL remains LOW.
  4. The slave releases SCL when ready.
  5. The bus continues when SCL reaches HIGH.
Clock stretching is supported by many I2C devices, but the actual behavior and timeout requirements must be checked for the connected slave and STM32.

12. I2C Multi-Master Arbitration

I2C supports multiple masters. If two masters attempt to control the bus at the same time, arbitration determines which master continues.

Because the bus uses wired-AND signaling, a master that releases SDA HIGH but observes SDA LOW during arbitration loses arbitration and must stop transmitting.

  • Arbitration prevents conflicting master transmissions.
  • The winning master continues the transaction.
  • The losing master releases the bus.
  • Multi-master behavior must be supported by the hardware and firmware design.

13. I2C Timing Configuration in STM32

STM32 I2C timing configuration depends on the peripheral generation and selected device.

Older I2C Peripheral Example

hi2c1.Init.ClockSpeed = 100000;
hi2c1.Init.DutyCycle = I2C_DUTYCYCLE_2;
hi2c1.Init.AddressingMode = I2C_ADDRESSINGMODE_7BIT;
hi2c1.Init.DualAddressMode = I2C_DUALADDRESS_DISABLE;
hi2c1.Init.GeneralCallMode = I2C_GENERALCALL_DISABLE;
hi2c1.Init.NoStretchMode = I2C_NOSTRETCH_DISABLE;

This style is used by some older STM32 HAL I2C peripheral implementations.

Newer I2C Peripheral Example

hi2c1.Init.Timing = DEVICE_SPECIFIC_I2C_TIMING;
hi2c1.Init.OwnAddress1 = 0;
hi2c1.Init.AddressingMode = I2C_ADDRESSINGMODE_7BIT;
hi2c1.Init.DualAddressMode = I2C_DUALADDRESS_DISABLE;
hi2c1.Init.OwnAddress2 = 0;
hi2c1.Init.GeneralCallMode = I2C_GENERALCALL_DISABLE;
hi2c1.Init.NoStretchMode = I2C_NOSTRETCH_DISABLE;
These examples illustrate different HAL configuration styles, not interchangeable code. The timing value must be calculated or generated for the exact STM32 peripheral clock, I2C kernel clock and required electrical timing. Use CubeMX or the ST timing calculation guidance for the selected MCU.

14. I2C Address Scanning

An address scan checks which 7-bit addresses acknowledge on the I2C bus.

HAL Example

#include "main.h"

extern I2C_HandleTypeDef hi2c1;

void I2C_Scan_Bus(void)
{
    uint16_t address;

    for (address = 1; address < 128; address++)
    {
        HAL_StatusTypeDef status;

        status = HAL_I2C_IsDeviceReady(
            &hi2c1,
            (uint16_t)(address << 1),
            2,
            10
        );

        if (status == HAL_OK)
        {
            // A device acknowledged this address.
            // Record the 7-bit address.
        }
    }
}

The scan checks address acknowledgement. It does not identify the device type or guarantee that its application-level functions are working.

Reserved addresses should not be treated as ordinary general-purpose slave addresses. Scan only when the bus is idle and the connected devices permit it.

16. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a 7-bit I2C address?

It is a seven-bit identifier used by the master to select an I2C slave device.

Q2. What is the R/W bit?

It is the direction bit in the address byte. Zero indicates write and one indicates read.

Q3. What is ACK?

ACK is a LOW level on SDA during the ninth clock pulse, indicating acknowledgement.

Q4. What is NACK?

NACK is a HIGH level on SDA during the ninth clock pulse, indicating no acknowledgement.

Q5. Who sends ACK during an I2C read?

The master receiver sends ACK when it wants additional data from the slave.

Q6. Why does the master send NACK after the final byte?

It indicates that the master has finished receiving data.

Q7. What is repeated START?

It is a new START condition generated without first issuing a STOP condition.

Q8. What is clock stretching?

It is a mechanism in which a device holds SCL LOW to delay further communication.

Q9. What is I2C arbitration?

It is the mechanism that resolves simultaneous attempts by multiple masters to use the bus.

Q10. What is the standard I2C clock frequency?

Standard Mode supports up to 100 kbit/s.

Q11. What is the difference between 7-bit and 10-bit addressing?

Seven-bit addressing uses one address byte with the R/W bit, while 10-bit addressing uses a two-byte addressing sequence.

Q12. Why is I2C timing configuration important?

Correct timing ensures reliable communication within the electrical and timing limits of the connected devices.

18. Lesson Summary

  • I2C commonly uses 7-bit slave addressing.
  • The R/W bit selects read or write direction.
  • ACK is represented by SDA LOW during the ninth clock.
  • NACK is represented by SDA HIGH during the ninth clock.
  • The master normally sends NACK after the final read byte.
  • START, STOP and repeated START control bus transactions.
  • I2C timing determines reliable data transfer.
  • Clock stretching allows a device to delay communication.
  • Arbitration supports multiple-master bus operation.
  • STM32 I2C timing configuration is peripheral-specific.
