Lesson 3: Ideal and Practical Op-Amp Characteristics

1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Differentiate between ideal and practical operational amplifiers.
  • Understand open-loop voltage gain.
  • Explain input impedance and output impedance.
  • Understand common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR).
  • Explain slew rate and gain-bandwidth product.
  • Understand input offset voltage and bias current.
  • Solve numerical problems based on Op-Amp characteristics.

2. What are Op-Amp Characteristics?

Op-Amp characteristics describe the electrical properties and performance of an operational amplifier. These parameters determine how accurately and effectively an Op-Amp can amplify and process analog signals.

An ideal Op-Amp is a theoretical model with perfect characteristics. A practical Op-Amp has limitations due to its internal electronic components, manufacturing variations and operating conditions.

3. Ideal Op-Amp Characteristics

An ideal operational amplifier is assumed to have the following properties:

3.1 Infinite Open-Loop Voltage Gain

An ideal Op-Amp has infinite open-loop voltage gain. Therefore, even an extremely small differential input voltage would produce a large theoretical output voltage.

AOL = ∞

Vout = AOL(V+ − V−)

3.2 Infinite Input Impedance

An ideal Op-Amp draws no current through either input terminal. Its input impedance is infinite.

Rin = ∞
I+ = I− = 0

3.3 Zero Output Impedance

An ideal Op-Amp has zero output impedance. Its output voltage does not change due to the load current, within the ideal model.

Rout = 0 Ω

3.4 Infinite Bandwidth

An ideal Op-Amp can amplify signals of all frequencies without any reduction in gain.

BW = ∞

3.5 Infinite CMRR

An ideal Op-Amp completely rejects signals that are common to both input terminals.

CMRR = ∞

3.6 Infinite Slew Rate

An ideal Op-Amp can change its output voltage instantaneously.

Slew Rate = ∞

3.7 Zero Input Offset Voltage

An ideal Op-Amp produces zero output when both input terminals are at the same voltage.

VOS = 0 V

4. Practical Op-Amp Characteristics

Real operational amplifiers have finite gain, finite input impedance, non-zero output impedance and limited bandwidth. They also exhibit offset voltage, bias currents and noise.

4.1 Open-Loop Voltage Gain

Open-loop gain is the voltage gain of an Op-Amp when no external feedback network is connected between the output and the input.

AOL = Vout / (V+ − V−)

Practical Op-Amps have high but finite open-loop gain. The gain also decreases as the operating frequency increases.

4.2 Input Impedance

Input impedance is the effective resistance seen by a signal source connected to the Op-Amp input.

High input impedance is desirable because it reduces loading on the signal source.

4.3 Output Impedance

Output impedance is the effective impedance at the Op-Amp output terminal. A low output impedance helps the circuit drive its intended load.

6. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR) indicates the ability of an Op-Amp to reject signals that appear equally at both input terminals.

For example, electrical interference picked up by two sensor wires may appear as a common-mode voltage. A differential amplifier with high CMRR can reject much of this interference.

CMRR:

CMRR = Ad / Acm

Where:
  • Ad = Differential-mode gain
  • Acm = Common-mode gain
CMRR in decibels:

CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(Ad/Acm)

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Differential gain = 1000
  • Common-mode gain = 0.01

Step 1: Calculate CMRR.

CMRR = 1000 / 0.01
CMRR = 100,000

Step 2: Convert into decibels.

CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(100,000)
CMRR(dB) = 100 dB
Higher CMRR generally means better rejection of common-mode interference, assuming the amplifier is operating within its specified input common-mode range.

7. Slew Rate

Slew rate is the maximum rate of change of an Op-Amp output voltage with respect to time.

It determines how quickly the output can respond to a rapidly changing input signal.

Slew Rate:

SR = Maximum |dVout/dt|

Unit: V/µs

Numerical Example

An Op-Amp output changes from 1 V to 7 V in 3 µs. Calculate its average rate of change over this interval.

ΔV = 7 − 1 = 6 V
Δt = 3 µs

Average rate = ΔV / Δt
= 6 / 3
= 2 V/µs

The calculated value is the average output transition rate. It represents the actual slew rate only if the measured transition is the specified maximum-rate portion of the response.

Slew Rate Limitation for a Sine Wave

For a sine-wave output:

Vout = Vp sin(2πft)

Required maximum slew rate:
SR = 2πfVp

Where Vp is the output peak amplitude and f is the frequency. If the required rate exceeds the available slew rate, the output waveform becomes distorted.

8. Bandwidth and Gain-Bandwidth Product

8.1 Bandwidth

Bandwidth is the frequency range over which an amplifier provides useful amplification according to a specified gain criterion.

For a typical single-pole response, the upper cutoff frequency is commonly measured at the point where gain falls to approximately 0.707 of its low-frequency value, corresponding to −3 dB.

At the −3 dB frequency:

A = 0.707 × Amidband

8.2 Gain-Bandwidth Product (GBP)

For a typical internally compensated voltage-feedback Op-Amp, the gain-bandwidth product is approximately constant over its useful operating range.

GBP ≈ Closed-loop Gain × Bandwidth

Bandwidth ≈ GBP / Closed-loop Gain

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Gain-bandwidth product = 1 MHz
  • Closed-loop gain = 10
Bandwidth = 1 MHz / 10
Bandwidth = 100 kHz
This is an approximate single-pole model. Actual bandwidth depends on the Op-Amp, feedback network, load and stability conditions. Not every Op-Amp has a constant GBP over all gains.

9. Input Offset Voltage

Input offset voltage is the small differential DC voltage that must be applied between the input terminals to make the output voltage zero under specified conditions.

Ideally, both inputs at the same voltage should produce zero output. In a practical Op-Amp, internal transistor mismatches can create a small output error.

Input Offset Voltage = VOS

Unit: µV or mV

Why is Offset Voltage Important?

  • It can cause output error in precision amplifiers.
  • It can affect low-level sensor signal measurements.
  • Its effect can increase with closed-loop noise gain.
  • It can vary with temperature and time.

10. Input Bias Current and Input Offset Current

10.1 Input Bias Current

Input bias current is the average of the DC currents flowing into the two input terminals of an Op-Amp.

IB = (|IB+| + |IB−|) / 2

10.2 Input Offset Current

Input offset current is the absolute difference between the two input bias currents.

IOS = |IB+ − IB−|
Input current directions and signs depend on the Op-Amp input technology and datasheet convention. The equations above use current magnitudes.

11. Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR)

Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR) describes how well an Op-Amp prevents changes in its power supply voltage from affecting its input-referred offset voltage.

A high PSRR is desirable when the supply voltage contains ripple or noise.

PSRR is commonly expressed in dB.

A common offset-based convention is:
PSRR = 20 log10(ΔVsupply/ΔVOS)

The exact definition and sign convention must be checked in the device datasheet.

12. Other Important Practical Parameters

Parameter Meaning Importance
Noise Unwanted electrical signal generated by the device. Affects precision measurements.
Output Swing Maximum usable output voltage range. Prevents output clipping.
Input Common-Mode Range Permitted input voltage range relative to supply rails. Ensures correct input-stage operation.
Output Current Maximum current the output can source or sink. Determines load-driving capability.
Temperature Drift Change in parameters with temperature. Important in precision circuits.
Settling Time Time required for output to reach and remain within a specified error band. Important in fast signal processing.

13. Practical Applications of Op-Amp Characteristics

Sensor Signal Conditioning

A sensor may produce a very small voltage. A high-input-impedance amplifier can help amplify the signal without excessively loading the sensor.

Noise Rejection

Differential amplifiers with high CMRR are useful for measuring small signals in the presence of common-mode electrical interference.

High-Frequency Signal Processing

Bandwidth and slew rate must be checked when amplifying high-frequency or large-amplitude signals.

Precision Measurement

Low input offset voltage, low bias current and low noise are important in circuits that measure small sensor signals.

14. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is the open-loop gain of an ideal Op-Amp?

It is infinite.

Q2. What is the input impedance of an ideal Op-Amp?

It is infinite, so ideally no current flows into its input terminals.

Q3. What is the output impedance of an ideal Op-Amp?

It is zero.

Q4. Define CMRR.

CMRR is the ratio of differential-mode gain to common-mode gain.

Q5. Why is high CMRR important?

It improves rejection of unwanted signals common to both inputs.

Q6. Define slew rate.

It is the maximum rate of change of the output voltage with respect to time.

Q7. What happens when the input signal requires a slew rate higher than the Op-Amp can provide?

The output cannot follow the required slope and becomes slew-rate distorted.

Q8. What is gain-bandwidth product?

For a typical single-pole voltage-feedback Op-Amp, it is approximately the product of closed-loop gain and bandwidth.

Q9. What is input offset voltage?

It is the small differential input voltage required to make the practical Op-Amp output zero under specified conditions.

Q10. What is PSRR?

It describes how effectively an Op-Amp rejects the effect of supply-voltage variations on its input-referred offset.

15. Numerical Practice Questions

  1. An Op-Amp has differential gain of 2000 and common-mode gain of 0.02. Calculate CMRR in ratio and dB.
  2. An Op-Amp output changes by 8 V in 4 µs. Calculate the average rate of change.
  3. An Op-Amp has a gain-bandwidth product of 2 MHz. Calculate approximate bandwidth at a closed-loop gain of 20.
  4. An Op-Amp has a differential gain of 100,000 and an input differential voltage of 100 µV. Calculate the theoretical open-loop output before considering output saturation.
  5. Two input bias currents are 80 nA and 100 nA. Calculate average input bias current and input offset current.

16. Lesson Summary

  • Ideal Op-Amps have infinite gain and input impedance, zero output impedance and unlimited bandwidth.
  • Practical Op-Amps have finite electrical characteristics.
  • CMRR measures rejection of common-mode signals.
  • Slew rate specifies the maximum output voltage transition rate.
  • Bandwidth determines the usable frequency range.
  • Gain-bandwidth product helps estimate closed-loop bandwidth for suitable Op-Amps.
  • Input offset voltage and bias current affect DC accuracy.
  • PSRR describes sensitivity to supply variations.
  • All practical parameters must be checked against the manufacturer's datasheet.
