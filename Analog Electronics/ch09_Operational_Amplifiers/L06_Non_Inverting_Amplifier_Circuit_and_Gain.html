2. Introduction to Non-Inverting Amplifier

A non-inverting amplifier is an op-amp circuit in which the input signal is directly applied to the non-inverting terminal (+).

A feedback resistor Rf connects the output to the inverting terminal (−), and resistor R1 connects the inverting terminal to ground or the circuit reference.

The input signal is applied to the non-inverting terminal. The feedback network controls the closed-loop gain.

4. Working Principle

  1. The input signal is applied to the non-inverting terminal.
  2. The output is connected to the inverting terminal through Rf.
  3. R1 connects the inverting terminal to ground or a reference.
  4. Negative feedback adjusts the output voltage.
  5. Under linear operation, the input differential voltage approaches zero.
  6. Therefore, V− approximately equals V+.
  7. The resistor network determines the output voltage relative to the input.

5. Virtual Short Concept

When an op-amp operates in its linear region with negative feedback:

V− ≈ V+

Since V+ is connected to Vin:

V− ≈ Vin

This is called the virtual short concept. It means the input terminals are approximately at the same voltage, but they are not physically shorted.

The virtual short approximation does not apply when the op-amp is saturated, when feedback is absent, or when the circuit is outside its practical operating limits.

6. Gain Derivation

Assume the op-amp is ideal and operating linearly with negative feedback.

Step 1: Input terminal voltages

V+ = Vin
V− ≈ Vin

Step 2: Feedback network

R1 and Rf form a voltage divider from Vout to ground.

V− = Vout × R1/(R1 + Rf)

Step 3: Apply virtual short

Vin = Vout × R1/(R1 + Rf)

Step 4: Rearrange

Vout/Vin = (R1 + Rf)/R1

Step 5: Final equation

Av = 1 + Rf/R1
Non-inverting voltage gain: Av = 1 + Rf/R1

7. Voltage Gain

Av = Vout/Vin = 1 + Rf/R1

8. Worked Numerical Example

Given:

  • R1 = 10 kΩ
  • Rf = 90 kΩ
  • Vin = 0.2 V

Step 1: Calculate gain

Av = 1 + 90kΩ/10kΩ
Av = 10

Step 2: Calculate output voltage

Vout = 10 × 0.2 V
Vout = 2 V
Output voltage = +2 V

11. Input Impedance

The non-inverting configuration offers very high input impedance because the signal is applied directly to the op-amp's non-inverting input.

Ideal op-amp

Infinite input impedance and zero input current.

Practical op-amp

Input impedance is finite and depends on the input stage and operating conditions.

Advantages

  • Minimal loading of the signal source.
  • Useful for high-impedance sensor signals.
  • Does not require a resistor in series with the signal input for the standard gain configuration.

12. Gain Selection and Design

For a desired non-inverting gain:

Rf = (Av − 1) × R1

Example

Design a gain of 6 using R1 = 10 kΩ.

Rf = (6 − 1) × 10 kΩ
Rf = 50 kΩ
A standard non-inverting amplifier has a minimum closed-loop gain of 1. It cannot provide a gain below unity using the ordinary feedback configuration shown.

13. Frequency Response and Bandwidth

For a single-pole op-amp model, the approximate closed-loop bandwidth is:

BW ≈ GBW / Av

This approximation applies to a suitable single-pole model and assumes the closed-loop gain is sufficiently established.

Example

GBW = 1 MHz, Av = 10.

BW ≈ 1 MHz / 10
BW ≈ 100 kHz

As the closed-loop gain increases, the available bandwidth generally decreases for a fixed GBW.

14. Practical Limitations

  • Output swing is limited by the device and supply.
  • Output current is limited.
  • Input common-mode range must be respected.
  • Finite GBW limits amplification at high frequencies.
  • Slew rate limits large signal transitions.
  • Input offset voltage causes output error.
  • Noise affects the circuit.
  • Resistor tolerance affects gain accuracy.
  • Capacitive loads may affect stability.

15. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

To construct and verify a non-inverting amplifier.

Components

  • Op-amp IC
  • R1 = 10 kΩ
  • Rf = 90 kΩ
  • Signal generator
  • Oscilloscope
  • Current-limited regulated supply
  • Breadboard and connecting wires

Procedure

  1. Identify the op-amp pinout and supply requirements.
  2. Connect Vin to the non-inverting input.
  3. Connect Rf from output to the inverting input.
  4. Connect R1 between the inverting input and ground.
  5. Power the circuit according to the datasheet.
  6. Apply a small sinusoidal signal at a suitable frequency.
  7. Observe input and output waveforms.
  8. Measure the gain and compare it with the calculated value.

Expected Result

Av = 1 + 90kΩ/10kΩ = 10

For a 0.1 V peak input, the ideal output is 1 V peak, subject to practical operating limits.

Verify the supply requirements and input common-mode range before wiring. Use current limiting and avoid shorting the output.

17. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is a non-inverting amplifier?

An op-amp circuit where the signal is applied to the non-inverting input and negative feedback controls the gain.

2. What is the gain formula?

Av = 1 + Rf/R1.

3. Why is the gain positive?

The output signal is in the same phase as the input within the operating bandwidth.

4. What is a virtual short?

Under linear operation with negative feedback, the two input voltages are approximately equal.

5. What is the minimum gain?

Unity gain, or 1.

6. Can a standard non-inverting amplifier have gain below 1?

Not with the ordinary resistor-feedback configuration. A unity-gain buffer is the minimum.

7. What is the input impedance?

Ideally infinite; practically finite and dependent on the op-amp and circuit.

8. Why is input impedance high?

The input signal is applied to the op-amp input terminal, which draws very little input current in typical designs.

9. What is negative feedback?

Feedback that opposes the differential input and stabilizes closed-loop operation.

10. What is the output equation?

Vout = (1 + Rf/R1)Vin.

11. What happens when Rf increases?

The gain increases if R1 is unchanged and the circuit remains within its limits.

12. What happens when R1 increases?

The gain decreases if Rf is unchanged.

13. What is GBW?

Gain-bandwidth product, which characterizes the gain-bandwidth relationship of an op-amp.

14. What is slew rate?

The maximum rate of output voltage change with time.

15. What is a voltage follower?

A unity-gain non-inverting configuration where output is directly connected to the inverting input.

16. How is the non-inverting amplifier useful?

It amplifies signals while generally providing high input impedance and no phase inversion.

17. What is output saturation?

When the output reaches its practical voltage limit and can no longer follow the linear gain relationship.

18. Why can a capacitive load be problematic?

It can alter phase margin and cause ringing or instability in some op-amp circuits.

18. Numerical Practice

  1. R1 = 5 kΩ and Rf = 20 kΩ. Calculate gain.
  2. Vin = 0.25 V, R1 = 10 kΩ and Rf = 40 kΩ. Calculate Vout.
  3. Design a gain of 8 using R1 = 10 kΩ. Calculate Rf.
  4. R1 = 20 kΩ and Rf = 60 kΩ. Find gain and output for Vin = −0.5 V.
  5. GBW = 1.5 MHz and gain = 15. Estimate bandwidth.
  6. Explain why the non-inverting amplifier has higher input impedance than the standard inverting configuration.
Show answers
  1. Av = 1 + 20/5 = 5.
  2. Av = 5; Vout = 1.25 V.
  3. Rf = (8 − 1) × 10 kΩ = 70 kΩ.
  4. Av = 1 + 60/20 = 4; Vout = −2 V.
  5. BW ≈ 1.5 MHz/15 = 100 kHz.
  6. The input is connected directly to the op-amp's non-inverting input rather than through a resistor to a virtual-ground node.

19. Lecture Summary

  • Input signal is applied to the non-inverting input.
  • Negative feedback is established through Rf and R1.
  • The input voltages approximately match during linear operation.
  • Closed-loop gain is 1 + Rf/R1.
  • The output is in phase with the input.
  • The standard circuit has a minimum gain of 1.
  • Practical op-amp limitations determine actual performance.
