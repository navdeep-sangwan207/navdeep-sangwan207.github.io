2. Ideal Op-Amp Assumptions for Circuit Analysis

When an op-amp operates in its linear region with negative feedback, ideal approximations are frequently used.

Virtual short

V+ ≈ V−

The input voltage difference is approximately zero for an ideal op-amp operating with negative feedback.

Input current

I+ ≈ I− ≈ 0

Ideal input terminals draw no current.

Output

The output adjusts to satisfy feedback conditions, provided it is not limited.

Linear operation

Assumes the amplifier is not saturated and is operating within its valid input range.

These assumptions are not valid in every circuit. Verify feedback polarity, supply rails, input common-mode range and output swing.

3. Inverting Amplifier

The input signal is applied through Rin to the inverting terminal. The non-inverting terminal is grounded, and Rf provides negative feedback.

Gain equation

Av = Vout/Vin = −Rf/Rin

Output equation

Vout = −(Rf/Rin)Vin

The negative sign indicates phase inversion.

4. Inverting Amplifier Numerical

Given:

  • Rin = 10 kΩ
  • Rf = 100 kΩ
  • Vin = 0.2 V
Av = −100k/10k = −10
Vout = −10 × 0.2 = −2 V
The expected output is −2 V, assuming the op-amp operates within its linear region and the output swing supports this voltage.

6. Non-Inverting Amplifier

The input signal is applied to the non-inverting terminal. A resistor divider provides negative feedback to the inverting terminal.

Av = 1 + Rf/R1
Vout = (1 + Rf/R1)Vin

The output is in phase with the input under linear operation.

Numerical

Rf = 90 kΩ, R1 = 10 kΩ and Vin = 0.2 V.

Av = 1 + 90/10 = 10
Vout = 10 × 0.2 = 2 V

8. Voltage Follower

A voltage follower connects the output directly to the inverting input. The signal is applied to the non-inverting input.

Av ≈ 1
Vout ≈ Vin
  • High input impedance.
  • Low output impedance relative to the input source in practical operation.
  • Provides impedance buffering.
  • Does not provide voltage amplification.

Numerical

Vin = 2.5 V.

Vout ≈ 2.5 V

The actual output depends on offset, output swing, loading and device limitations.

10. Differential Amplifier

A differential amplifier amplifies the voltage difference between two input signals while ideally rejecting common-mode voltage.

For a matched resistor ratio configuration:

Vout = (R2/R1)(V2 − V1)

For this equation, the resistor ratios must be matched:

R2/R1 = R4/R3

Assuming the standard differential amplifier connections and ideal op-amp behavior.

Numerical

R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 100 kΩ, V1 = 0.5 V, V2 = 0.6 V.

Vout = (100/10)(0.6 − 0.5)
Vout = 10 × 0.1 = 1 V

12. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

CMRR measures the ability of an amplifier to reject common-mode signals relative to differential signals.

CMRR = Ad/Acm
CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(Ad/Acm)

Where:

  • Ad = differential gain
  • Acm = common-mode gain

Numerical

Ad = 1000, Acm = 0.1.

CMRR = 1000/0.1 = 10000
CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(10000) = 80 dB

14. Op-Amp Gain and Bandwidth

For a simplified single-pole op-amp model:

BW ≈ GBW / Noise gain

For the inverting amplifier, noise gain is:

Noise gain = 1 + Rf/Rin

Signal gain magnitude is Rf/Rin, but the noise gain used for bandwidth and stability estimates is typically 1 + Rf/Rin.

Numerical

GBW = 1 MHz, Rf = 90 kΩ, Rin = 10 kΩ.

Noise gain = 1 + 90/10 = 10
BW ≈ 1 MHz/10 = 100 kHz

16. Slew Rate Numerical

For a sinusoidal output:

SR ≥ 2πfVp

Example

f = 20 kHz and Vp = 5 V.

SR ≥ 2π × 20000 × 5
SR ≥ 628318.5 V/s ≈ 0.628 V/µs

An op-amp with a slew rate of 0.5 V/µs may be slew-rate limited under these conditions.

  • Calculated output = 8 V
  • Practical output swing = ±5 V
Actual output cannot reach 8 V

20. Input and Output Current

For a practical op-amp, input currents are small but nonzero. Output current is supplied or sunk by the output stage.

Load current

IL = Vout/RL

Inverting amplifier input current

Under ideal assumptions, the current through Rin is approximately:

Iin ≈ Vin/Rin

This current flows through Rf toward the output because the op-amp input current is assumed zero.

Numerical

Vin = 0.5 V, Rin = 10 kΩ.

Iin ≈ 0.5/10000 = 50 µA

21. Op-Amp Power and Load Calculation

For a simple resistive load connected to an output:

Pload = Vout²/RL

Example: 4 V across 1 kΩ.

Pload = 4²/1000 = 16 mW

Load power is not the same as op-amp internal power dissipation. Internal dissipation depends on supply voltages, output current, device architecture and operating conditions.

23. Practical Laboratory

Objective

Analyze basic op-amp circuits and verify gain and output limitations.

Experiment A: Inverting Amplifier

  1. Build an inverting amplifier using an appropriate op-amp and resistors.
  2. Choose Rin = 10 kΩ and Rf = 47 kΩ.
  3. Apply a small input signal.
  4. Calculate the theoretical gain.
  5. Observe input and output waveforms.
  6. Verify phase inversion.

Experiment B: Non-Inverting Amplifier

  1. Build a non-inverting amplifier.
  2. Choose R1 = 10 kΩ and Rf = 47 kΩ.
  3. Calculate the theoretical gain.
  4. Apply a small input signal.
  5. Observe output amplitude and phase.

Experiment C: Voltage Follower

  1. Connect the output to the inverting input.
  2. Apply the input to the non-inverting input.
  3. Observe output tracking.
  4. Compare the circuit with and without a load, within the device's operating limits.

Expected Result

The output follows the calculated gain relationship while the op-amp operates within its linear region. When practical limitations are exceeded, clipping, distortion or other non-ideal behavior may occur.

Use a low-voltage laboratory setup and verify the supply and input ratings before connecting the circuit.

25. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is the gain of an inverting amplifier?

Av = −Rf/Rin.

2. What is the gain of a non-inverting amplifier?

Av = 1 + Rf/R1.

3. Why is the gain of a voltage follower equal to one?

The output is fed directly back to the inverting input, so negative feedback drives the output approximately equal to the input.

4. What is a virtual short?

With negative feedback and linear operation, the input voltages are approximately equal, although they are not physically shorted.

5. What is CMRR?

The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain.

6. What is CMRR in dB?

20 log10(Ad/Acm).

7. Why is a differential amplifier used?

To amplify the difference between two signals while ideally rejecting common-mode signals.

8. What is the noise gain of an inverting amplifier?

1 + Rf/Rin.

9. Is noise gain the same as signal gain?

No. For an inverting amplifier, signal gain magnitude is Rf/Rin, whereas noise gain is 1 + Rf/Rin.

10. What is bandwidth?

The frequency range over which the amplifier performs according to the defined response criteria.

11. What is slew rate?

The maximum rate of change of output voltage.

12. What causes clipping?

When the requested output exceeds the available output swing.

13. What is output saturation?

The output is limited near an available output boundary and cannot follow the requested linear output.

14. Why does a voltage follower not provide voltage gain?

Its feedback connection produces approximately unity voltage gain.

15. Why use a voltage follower?

To isolate a high-impedance source from a load by providing buffering.

16. What is input offset voltage?

The small differential input voltage needed to make the output zero under specified conditions.

17. What is input bias current?

The current entering or leaving an op-amp input terminal under specified operating conditions.

18. What is the difference between GBW and slew rate?

GBW is related to frequency-dependent gain; slew rate limits the maximum output voltage transition rate.

19. Why can a mathematically correct op-amp circuit fail?

Because supply voltage, input range, output swing, current, bandwidth, slew rate or stability may be inadequate.

20. Why should resistor tolerances matter in a differential amplifier?

Ratio mismatch degrades common-mode rejection and can introduce errors.

26. Numerical Practice

  1. Calculate the gain of an inverting amplifier with Rin = 5 kΩ and Rf = 50 kΩ.
  2. Calculate the output for Vin = 0.3 V using the circuit in Question 1.
  3. Calculate non-inverting gain with R1 = 2 kΩ and Rf = 18 kΩ.
  4. Calculate output voltage for a non-inverting amplifier with gain 10 and input 0.5 V.
  5. Calculate the output of a matched differential amplifier with R2/R1 = 5, V2 = 0.7 V and V1 = 0.4 V.
  6. Calculate CMRR in dB when Ad = 1000 and Acm = 0.01.
  7. Estimate bandwidth when GBW = 4 MHz and noise gain = 40.
  8. Calculate required slew rate for 10 kHz and 4 V peak.
  9. Calculate current through a 2 kΩ load at 5 V.
  10. Explain the practical limitations of a voltage follower.
Show answers
  1. Av = −50/5 = −10.
  2. Vout = −10 × 0.3 = −3 V.
  3. Av = 1 + 18/2 = 10.
  4. Vout = 10 × 0.5 = 5 V.
  5. Vout = 5 × (0.7−0.4) = 1.5 V.
  6. CMRR = 100000; CMRR = 100 dB.
  7. BW ≈ 4 MHz/40 = 100 kHz.
  8. SR ≥ 2π × 10000 × 4 ≈ 0.251 V/µs.
  9. I = 5/2000 = 2.5 mA.
  10. Output swing, output current, input range, bandwidth, slew rate, stability and offset are among the practical limitations.
