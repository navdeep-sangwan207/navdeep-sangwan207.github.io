2. Introduction to Differential Amplifiers

A differential amplifier amplifies the voltage difference between two input signals while ideally rejecting voltage signals that are common to both inputs.

It is widely used in:

  • Sensor signal conditioning
  • Instrumentation circuits
  • Industrial measurement systems
  • Audio signal processing
  • Bridge circuits
  • Biomedical signal acquisition
Vout ∝ (V2 − V1)

3. Differential Signal

The differential input voltage is the difference between the two input voltages.

Vd = V2 − V1

For example, if:

  • V1 = 2.0 V
  • V2 = 2.5 V
Vd = 2.5 − 2.0 = 0.5 V

The differential signal is 0.5 V.

4. Common-Mode Signal

A common-mode voltage is a voltage component present equally at both inputs.

Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2

For example, if:

  • V1 = 2.1 V
  • V2 = 2.3 V
Vcm = (2.1 + 2.3)/2 = 2.2 V

The common-mode voltage is 2.2 V, while the differential voltage is 0.2 V.

Differential amplifiers are designed to amplify the difference between the inputs and reduce the effect of a common voltage component.

5. Differential and Common-Mode Components

Any pair of input voltages can be represented in terms of differential and common-mode components.

V1 = Vcm − Vd/2
V2 = Vcm + Vd/2

Conceptual illustration. The actual common-mode and differential signals may vary with time and frequency.

The gain relationship depends on resistor-ratio matching. For the standard four-resistor circuit, R2/R1 must equal R4/R3 for the ideal differential-amplifier equation to apply.

8. Differential Amplifier Gain Derivation

For the standard four-resistor circuit:

R2/R1 = R4/R3 = k

The non-inverting input voltage is determined by the divider formed by R3 and R4:

V+ = V2 × R4/(R3 + R4)

Under ideal negative-feedback operation:

V− ≈ V+

Applying KCL at the inverting node:

(V1 − V−)/R1 = (V− − Vout)/R2

Rearranging:

Vout = (1 + R2/R1)V− − (R2/R1)V1

Substituting V− ≈ V+ and the divider voltage, when resistor ratios are matched:

Vout = (R2/R1)(V2 − V1)
Differential gain: Ad = R2/R1

9. Worked Example

Given:

  • R1 = 10 kΩ
  • R2 = 100 kΩ
  • R3 = 10 kΩ
  • R4 = 100 kΩ
  • V1 = 1.0 V
  • V2 = 1.2 V

Check the resistor ratios:

R2/R1 = 100k/10k = 10
R4/R3 = 100k/10k = 10

Therefore, the ratios match.

Vd = V2 − V1 = 1.2 − 1.0 = 0.2 V
Ad = 10
Vout = 10 × 0.2 = 2 V
Ideal output voltage = 2 V

10. Common-Mode Rejection

In a practical differential amplifier, the output may contain both differential and common-mode components.

Vout = AdVd + AcmVcm

Where:

  • Ad = differential gain
  • Acm = common-mode gain
  • Vd = differential input voltage
  • Vcm = common-mode input voltage

An ideal differential amplifier has:

Acm = 0

It amplifies the differential signal and rejects the common-mode signal.

11. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

CMRR describes how effectively an amplifier rejects common-mode signals compared with the differential signal it amplifies.

CMRR = |Ad/Acm|

CMRR is often expressed in decibels:

CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(|Ad/Acm|)

High CMRR

Better rejection of common-mode interference relative to differential gain.

Low CMRR

More common-mode signal can appear in the output relative to the differential signal.

12. Worked CMRR Example

Suppose:

  • Differential gain Ad = 1000
  • Common-mode gain Acm = 0.1
CMRR = 1000/0.1 = 10000

Convert to decibels:

CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(10000)
CMRR(dB) = 80 dB
CMRR = 10,000 | CMRR = 80 dB

16. Resistor Matching and CMRR

The resistor ratios in a four-resistor differential amplifier must match to achieve good common-mode rejection.

R2/R1 = R4/R3

Even if the op-amp itself has a high CMRR, mismatched external resistor ratios can significantly degrade the overall common-mode rejection.

Practical improvements

  • Use precision resistors.
  • Use matched resistor networks.
  • Consider resistor temperature-coefficient matching.
  • Use an instrumentation amplifier when high precision or high input impedance is required.
  • Keep input wiring symmetrical when practical.
  • Reduce interference through grounding, shielding and layout practices.
CMRR is frequency-dependent. Resistor mismatch, source impedance mismatch, parasitic capacitance and input common-mode range can all affect real-world performance.

17. Applications of Differential Amplifiers

Sensor Interfaces

Amplify the difference between sensor output terminals.

Bridge Circuits

Measure small voltage differences in resistive bridge systems.

Industrial Systems

Reject some common-mode interference in differential measurement.

Audio Electronics

Process differential signals and help reject interference in suitable circuits.

Instrumentation

Measure small signals in the presence of common-mode voltages.

Biomedical Interfaces

Used in signal acquisition front ends, with additional safety and noise considerations.

19. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

To demonstrate differential amplification and investigate common-mode rejection.

Components

  • Suitable op-amp
  • Matched resistors
  • Low-voltage signal sources
  • Regulated supply
  • Multimeter
  • Breadboard

Procedure

  1. Build a four-resistor differential amplifier with matched ratios.
  2. Verify resistor ratios using a multimeter.
  3. Apply two low-voltage inputs with a known difference.
  4. Measure the output voltage.
  5. Calculate differential gain.
  6. Apply an equal voltage to both inputs.
  7. Measure the common-mode output.
  8. Investigate how resistor mismatch affects common-mode rejection.

Expected Result

The amplifier should amplify the differential component while rejecting common-mode components to the extent allowed by resistor matching, op-amp CMRR and operating conditions.

Check input common-mode range, supply voltage and output swing. Never apply voltages outside the op-amp ratings.

21. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is a differential amplifier?

An amplifier that amplifies the difference between two input signals.

2. What is differential voltage?

Vd = V2 − V1.

3. What is common-mode voltage?

Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2.

4. What is CMRR?

The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain.

5. What is the CMRR equation?

CMRR = |Ad/Acm|.

6. How is CMRR expressed in dB?

CMRR(dB) = 20log10(|Ad/Acm|).

7. What is ideal common-mode gain?

Zero.

8. What is ideal CMRR?

Infinite.

9. Why are matched resistors important?

They help maintain the required ratios and improve common-mode rejection.

10. What happens if the resistor ratios do not match?

Common-mode signals may produce an unwanted output.

11. What is the gain of the matched four-resistor differential amplifier?

Ad = R2/R1, when R2/R1 = R4/R3.

12. What is the difference between differential and common-mode signals?

Differential is the difference between inputs; common-mode is the component shared by both.

13. Why is CMRR important in sensor circuits?

It helps reject unwanted common-mode interference while amplifying the desired differential signal.

14. What is an instrumentation amplifier?

A precision amplifier designed to amplify differential signals with high input impedance and high common-mode rejection.

15. Does CMRR remain constant at all frequencies?

No. CMRR generally varies with frequency and device conditions.

16. Can a differential amplifier measure any differential voltage?

No. The input common-mode voltage, differential input range, supply and output swing must remain within device limits.

17. What is common-mode interference?

An unwanted voltage component appearing similarly on both input lines.

18. How can CMRR be improved?

Use matched resistors, a suitable amplifier, balanced input paths and appropriate circuit layout.

23. Lecture Summary

  • Differential amplifiers amplify the voltage difference between two inputs.
  • Vd = V2 − V1.
  • Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2.
  • The standard four-resistor differential amplifier requires matched resistor ratios.
  • Ad = R2/R1 under matched-ratio conditions.
  • CMRR measures differential gain relative to common-mode gain.
  • CMRR(dB) = 20log10(|Ad/Acm|).
  • Resistor matching is important for practical common-mode rejection.
  • Instrumentation amplifiers are often preferred for precision sensor measurements.
