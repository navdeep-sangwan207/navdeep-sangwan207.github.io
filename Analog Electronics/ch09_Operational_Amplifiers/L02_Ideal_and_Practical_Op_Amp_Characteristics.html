  • Understand the ideal characteristics of an operational amplifier.

  • Recognize practical limitations when designing amplifier circuits.

2. Ideal Operational Amplifier

An ideal operational amplifier is a theoretical model with perfect characteristics. It is used to simplify circuit analysis and understand the behavior of real op-amp circuits.

Infinite open-loop gain

AOL → ∞

Very small differential input can produce a large output change in the ideal model.

Infinite input impedance

Rin → ∞

No input current flows into either input terminal.

Zero output impedance

Rout → 0

Output voltage is independent of load current in the ideal model.

Infinite bandwidth

BW → ∞

All signal frequencies are amplified without gain reduction.

Zero input offset voltage

VOS = 0

Equal input voltages produce zero differential output demand.

Infinite slew rate

SR → ∞

Output voltage can change instantaneously in the ideal model.

Infinite CMRR

CMRR → ∞

Common-mode signals are completely rejected.

Infinite PSRR

PSRR → ∞

Supply voltage variations do not affect the output.

Zero noise

Noise = 0

The ideal model produces no internally generated noise.

4. Open-Loop Gain and Finite Gain

Open-loop gain is the voltage gain of an op-amp without external feedback.

AOL = Vout / (V+ − V−)

For a practical op-amp, AOL is large but finite. For example, assume an illustrative open-loop gain of 100,000 V/V.

If the differential input is 100 µV:

Vout = 100,000 × 100 µV = 10 V

The output may approach its positive or negative limit depending on the supply rails and output-stage design.

Open-loop gain is frequency-dependent. A single gain value is not sufficient to describe an op-amp across its entire operating frequency range.

5. Input Impedance and Output Impedance

Input impedance

Input impedance describes the opposition presented by the input terminals to the signal source.

Ideal: Rin → ∞

High input impedance minimizes loading of the preceding circuit.

Output impedance

Output impedance describes the effective internal opposition between the output and the external load.

Ideal: Rout → 0

Low output impedance helps the amplifier drive a load while maintaining its output voltage, within its current limits.

Signal loading example

Consider a 10 kΩ source resistance connected to a 10 kΩ input resistance.

Vinput = Vsource × Rin / (Rs + Rin)

For a 1 V source, the input becomes 0.5 V. A much higher input impedance reduces this loading effect.

6. Virtual Short and Virtual Ground

In a negative-feedback op-amp circuit operating in its linear region, the differential input voltage is generally very small.

V+ ≈ V−

This is called the virtual short. It does not mean the input terminals are physically connected.

Virtual ground

If the non-inverting input is connected to ground, negative feedback may hold the inverting input at approximately 0 V.

Conditions: The virtual-short approximation requires negative feedback and linear operation. It is not generally valid in open-loop operation, positive-feedback circuits or when the output is saturated.

7. Bandwidth and Frequency Response

Bandwidth is the frequency range over which the amplifier maintains its specified gain. A common convention defines the bandwidth limit at the frequency where gain falls by 3 dB from its midband value.

Gain at cutoff = 0.707 × Midband Gain

For a simplified single-dominant-pole op-amp, the gain-bandwidth product is approximately constant over its applicable frequency range.

Closed-loop bandwidth ≈ GBW / Closed-loop gain

Example: If GBW = 1 MHz and the closed-loop gain magnitude is 10:

Bandwidth ≈ 1 MHz / 10 = 100 kHz

8. Slew Rate

Slew rate is the maximum rate of change of an op-amp output voltage.

SR = Maximum |dVout/dt|

It is commonly specified in V/µs.

For a sinusoidal output, the minimum slew rate required to reproduce the signal without slew-rate distortion is:

SRrequired = 2πfVpeak

Worked example

For a 10 kHz sine wave with a peak amplitude of 5 V:

SR = 2π × 10,000 × 5
SR ≈ 0.314 V/µs

An op-amp with a lower slew rate cannot reproduce this signal without distortion at the stated amplitude and frequency.

9. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

CMRR measures the ability of an op-amp to reject signals that appear equally at both input terminals.

CMRR = Ad / Acm

In decibels:

CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(Ad / Acm)

Here, Ad is differential gain and Acm is common-mode gain.

A higher CMRR is generally desirable in measurement systems where unwanted common-mode interference may be present.

10. Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR)

PSRR describes how effectively an op-amp rejects changes or disturbances in its power supply voltages.

Supply variations can couple into the output through the internal circuitry. A higher PSRR generally means less sensitivity to supply disturbances.

Datasheets may express power-supply rejection using different conventions, such as µV/V or dB. Check the definition and test conditions before comparing devices.

14. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

To study ideal and practical op-amp characteristics and observe the effects of finite gain, bandwidth and slew rate.

Procedure

  1. Select an op-amp IC and obtain its manufacturer datasheet.
  2. Record open-loop gain, input offset voltage, input bias current and input impedance.
  3. Record the gain-bandwidth product and slew rate.
  4. Identify input common-mode range and output voltage swing.
  5. Compare the practical values with the ideal assumptions.
  6. Use a low-voltage test circuit to observe the frequency response, following the manufacturer's recommended circuit and supply conditions.

Expected Result

The practical device will show finite gain, finite bandwidth, finite slew rate and nonzero input offset and bias current.

Do not exceed the recommended supply, input common-mode, differential input or output ratings. Use current-limited supplies and suitable test equipment.

16. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is an ideal op-amp?

A theoretical amplifier model with infinite gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance and other perfect characteristics.

2. What is open-loop gain?

The voltage gain without external feedback.

3. Why is high input impedance desirable?

It reduces loading of the signal source.

4. Why is low output impedance desirable?

It helps maintain the output voltage when driving a load, within the output current capability.

5. What is bandwidth?

The frequency range over which an amplifier meets its specified gain criterion.

6. What is gain-bandwidth product?

For a typical single-dominant-pole voltage-feedback op-amp, it is approximately constant over a suitable range of closed-loop gains.

7. What is slew rate?

The maximum rate of change of output voltage, usually in V/µs.

8. What is CMRR?

The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain.

9. What is PSRR?

A measure of the rejection of power-supply variations or disturbances.

10. What is input offset voltage?

The small differential DC voltage required between the inputs to make the output zero under specified conditions.

11. What is input bias current?

The average of the currents flowing into the two input terminals under specified conditions.

12. What is virtual short?

The approximate equality of the two input voltages in a properly operating negative-feedback linear circuit.

13. Does virtual short mean the input terminals are physically connected?

No. Their voltages are approximately equal, but they are not physically shorted.

14. When is virtual ground valid?

When negative feedback maintains the inverting input near a grounded non-inverting reference while the amplifier remains in its linear operating region.

15. Can an ideal op-amp have infinite bandwidth?

Yes, in the ideal theoretical model. Practical devices have finite bandwidth.

16. What happens if the required slew rate exceeds the device capability?

The output cannot follow the required slope and becomes slew-rate distorted.

17. What does a 3 dB bandwidth point represent?

A voltage gain magnitude approximately 0.707 times the midband gain.

18. Is the output of every op-amp rail-to-rail?

No. Output swing depends on the IC architecture, supply voltage and load.

17. Numerical Practice

  1. An op-amp has AOL = 200,000 and differential input = 50 µV. Find the ideal open-loop output before saturation.
  2. A circuit has GBW = 2 MHz and closed-loop gain magnitude = 20. Estimate its bandwidth using the single-pole approximation.
  3. Calculate the minimum slew rate for a 20 kHz sine wave with a 3 V peak amplitude.
  4. An amplifier has Ad = 100,000 and Acm = 10. Calculate CMRR in dB.
  5. A 2 V source with 5 kΩ source resistance drives a 45 kΩ input resistance. Calculate the loaded input voltage.
Show answers
  1. 10 V.
  2. Approximately 100 kHz.
  3. 2π × 20,000 × 3 = 0.377 V/µs.
  4. CMRR = 10,000; CMRR(dB) = 80 dB.
  5. 2 × 45/(5+45) = 1.8 V.

18. Lecture Summary

  • The ideal op-amp has infinite gain and input impedance, zero output impedance and infinite bandwidth.
  • Real op-amps have finite and non-ideal characteristics.
  • Bandwidth and gain-bandwidth product determine frequency performance.
  • Slew rate limits the maximum output voltage transition speed.
  • CMRR and PSRR describe rejection of common-mode and supply disturbances.
  • Virtual short and virtual ground are approximations valid under negative feedback and linear operation.
  • Always check the selected IC datasheet before circuit design.
