1. Lecture Overview

Voltage, current, resistance and power are the basic quantities used to understand and analyze electrical circuits. Every electronic component operates under specific voltage, current and power limits. Understanding these quantities is essential for circuit design, component selection, laboratory testing and troubleshooting.

Placement objective: Develop the ability to calculate voltage, current, resistance, power and energy; understand their units; and use a digital multimeter safely to measure electrical quantities.

Prerequisites

  • Lecture 1: Analog and Digital Signals
  • Lecture 2: Analog Electronic System Blocks
  • Basic algebra and arithmetic.

Learning Objectives

  • Define voltage, current, resistance and electrical power.
  • Understand their SI units and measurement symbols.
  • Apply basic electrical equations.
  • Differentiate DC and AC voltage and current.
  • Calculate electrical energy and power consumption.
  • Understand resistor power ratings and component limits.
  • Use a digital multimeter to measure voltage, current and resistance.
  • Solve practical circuit and placement numerical problems.

2. Electric Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical interactions. Charge is represented by the symbol Q and measured in coulombs (C).

  • Electrons carry negative charge.
  • Protons carry positive charge.
  • The charge of one electron has a magnitude of approximately 1.602 × 10-19 C.

Relation Between Charge and Current

Electric current is the rate at which electric charge passes through a cross-section of a conductor.

I = Q / t

Q = I × t

Where:

  • I = current in amperes (A)
  • Q = charge in coulombs (C)
  • t = time in seconds (s)

Solved Example

Question: A charge of 30 C passes through a wire in 10 seconds. Calculate the current.

I = Q / t

I = 30 / 10 = 3 A
Answer: 3 A.

3. Voltage (Electrical Potential Difference)

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. It represents the energy transferred per unit charge when charge moves between those points.

Voltage is represented by V and measured in volts (V).

V = W / Q

W = V × Q

Where:

  • V = voltage in volts
  • W = energy transferred in joules
  • Q = charge in coulombs

Understanding Voltage

A voltage difference can drive current through a suitable conducting path. For current to flow continuously in a circuit, there must be a closed path and an appropriate source of electrical energy.

Voltage Sources

  • Battery: supplies DC voltage.
  • DC power supply: provides regulated or adjustable DC voltage.
  • AC generator: produces alternating voltage.
  • Signal generator: produces test waveforms.

DC Voltage

DC voltage has a fixed polarity. Its magnitude may be constant or vary with time while maintaining the same polarity.

AC Voltage

AC voltage periodically changes magnitude and polarity. A sinusoidal AC voltage is commonly expressed as:

v(t) = Vp sin(2πft + φ)

Here Vp is peak voltage, f is frequency and φ is phase angle.

Practical example: A 5 V DC supply is commonly used in electronic circuits. An AC mains supply is an alternating voltage source and must not be connected directly to low-voltage circuits without appropriate conversion and protection.

4. Electric Current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor or circuit element.

I = Q / t

The SI unit is the ampere (A).

Conventional Current and Electron Flow

  • Conventional current: Defined as flowing from higher potential to lower potential through an external passive circuit.
  • Electron flow: Electrons move in the opposite direction in a metallic conductor.

Circuit diagrams and conventional current calculations normally use conventional current direction.

Types of Current

TypeDescriptionExample
DC currentMaintains one direction of flow.Battery-powered circuit.
AC currentPeriodically reverses direction.AC power circuit.
Transient currentChanges during switching or circuit response.Capacitor charging current.

Solved Example

A circuit carries a constant current of 250 mA for 20 seconds. Calculate the total charge transferred.

I = 250 mA = 0.25 A

Q = I × t
Q = 0.25 × 20 = 5 C
Answer: 5 coulombs.

5. Resistance

Resistance is the opposition offered by a material or component to the flow of electric current.

Resistance is represented by R and measured in ohms (Ω).

Factors Affecting Resistance

The resistance of a uniform conductor is:

R = ρL / A

Where:

  • ρ = resistivity of the material in Ω·m
  • L = conductor length in metres
  • A = cross-sectional area in square metres

Therefore:

  • Increasing length increases resistance.
  • Increasing cross-sectional area decreases resistance.
  • Material resistivity affects resistance.
  • Temperature affects resistance, depending on the material.

Resistor

A resistor is a passive electronic component designed to provide a specified resistance. It is used to limit current, divide voltage, set bias conditions and establish timing constants.

Resistor Symbols and Values

SymbolMeaning
ΩOhm
kΩKilo-ohm = 1,000 Ω
MΩMega-ohm = 1,000,000 Ω

Temperature Coefficient

A resistor's value may change with temperature. For a simple linear approximation:

Rt = R0 [1 + α(T - T0)]

Where α is the temperature coefficient, R0 is resistance at reference temperature T0, and Rt is resistance at temperature T.

6. Relationship Between Voltage, Current and Resistance

For an ohmic resistor operating under conditions where its resistance is approximately constant, voltage and current are related by Ohm's law.

V = I × R

I = V / R

R = V / I

Ohm's Law Triangle

Example 1: Calculate Current

A 12 V DC supply is connected across a 6 Ω resistor. Find the current.

I = V / R

I = 12 / 6 = 2 A
Answer: 2 A.

Example 2: Calculate Resistance

A voltage of 24 V produces a current of 3 A through a resistor. Calculate resistance.

R = V / I

R = 24 / 3 = 8 Ω
Answer: 8 Ω.

Example 3: Calculate Voltage

A current of 0.5 A flows through a 20 Ω resistor. Calculate voltage across it.

V = I × R

V = 0.5 × 20 = 10 V
Answer: 10 V.
Ohm's law in this simple form applies to ohmic behavior. Components such as diodes and transistors have nonlinear voltage-current characteristics, so a single constant resistance does not describe their complete operation.

7. Electrical Power

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted. It is represented by P and measured in watts (W).

P = W / t

P = V × I

Using Ohm's law, power in a resistor can also be calculated as:

P = I²R

P = V² / R

Power Triangle

Solved Example 1: Calculate Power

A resistor carries 2 A when connected across 12 V. Calculate power dissipation.

P = V × I

P = 12 × 2 = 24 W
Answer: 24 W.

Solved Example 2: Power Using Resistance

A 10 Ω resistor carries 2 A. Calculate power.

P = I²R

P = 2² × 10
P = 40 W
Answer: 40 W.

Solved Example 3: Calculate Current from Power

A 60 W load operates from a 12 V DC source. Calculate current.

I = P / V

I = 60 / 12 = 5 A
Answer: 5 A.

8. Electrical Energy

Electrical energy is the total amount of electrical work transferred over a period of time.

E = P × t

E = V × I × t

SI unit: joule (J).

Commercial electrical energy is commonly measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh).

Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × Time (hours)

Solved Example

A 100 W device operates continuously for 5 hours. Calculate energy consumption in kWh.

Power = 100 W = 0.1 kW

Energy = 0.1 × 5
Energy = 0.5 kWh
Answer: 0.5 kWh.

Energy in Joules

E = 100 × (5 × 3600)

E = 1,800,000 J

9. Conductance and Resistivity

Conductance

Conductance measures how easily current flows through a component. It is the reciprocal of resistance.

G = 1 / R

Its SI unit is siemens (S).

Resistivity and Conductivity

Resistivity is a material property describing its opposition to electric current. Conductivity is its reciprocal.

σ = 1 / ρ

Calculate conductance for a resistor of 500 Ω.

G = 1 / 500

11. AC and DC Power

DC Power

For a DC circuit, instantaneous power is:

P = V × I

For a constant voltage across a resistor, this is also the average power dissipated by that resistor.

AC Power in a Resistive Load

For a purely resistive AC load with sinusoidal voltage and current, voltage and current are in phase.

P = Vrms × Irms

AC Power in a General AC Circuit

For sinusoidal voltage and current:

P = Vrms × Irms × cosφ

Where:

  • P = real power in watts.
  • Vrms = RMS voltage.
  • Irms = RMS current.
  • φ = phase angle between voltage and current.
  • cosφ = power factor for the sinusoidal case.

Three Important AC Power Quantities

QuantityFormulaUnit
Real powerP = VI cosφW
Reactive powerQ = VI sinφvar
Apparent powerS = VIVA

For sinusoidal steady-state conditions:

S² = P² + Q²
The simple power-factor equations above assume sinusoidal steady-state voltage and current. With distorted waveforms, total power factor also depends on waveform distortion.

12. Resistor Power Rating and Selection

A resistor converts electrical energy into heat. Its power rating specifies the maximum power it can safely dissipate under the conditions stated in its datasheet.

Common nominal resistor power ratings include:

  • 0.125 W
  • 0.25 W
  • 0.5 W
  • 1 W
  • 2 W
  • 5 W and higher

Practical Resistor Selection Example

A resistor has 12 V across it and a resistance of 100 Ω. Calculate its power dissipation.

P = V² / R

P = 12² / 100
P = 144 / 100 = 1.44 W
Answer: The resistor dissipates 1.44 W.

A 1 W resistor is not suitable for this operating point because the calculated dissipation exceeds its nominal rating. A higher rating must be selected after considering ambient temperature, ventilation, mounting conditions and the manufacturer's derating curve. A 2 W resistor may be a starting choice, but its actual thermal suitability must still be verified.

Do not select a resistor solely because its rating is slightly above calculated power. Adequate thermal margin and datasheet derating requirements are important.

13. Measuring Voltage, Current and Resistance Using a DMM

13.1 Measuring DC Voltage

  1. Select the DC voltage mode (V⎓).
  2. Insert the black probe into COM.
  3. Insert the red probe into the voltage input terminal.
  4. Connect the probes in parallel across the component or supply.
  5. Observe the displayed reading and polarity.

13.2 Measuring AC Voltage

  1. Select AC voltage mode (V~).
  2. Verify the meter's voltage and frequency limits.
  3. Connect the probes in parallel across the measurement points.
  4. Observe the reading.

13.3 Measuring DC Current

  1. Turn off the circuit before changing connections.
  2. Move the red probe to the correct current input terminal.
  3. Select an appropriate current range.
  4. Open the circuit and insert the meter in series.
  5. Power the circuit and read the current.
  6. After measurement, return the probe to the voltage terminal.

13.4 Measuring Resistance

  1. Switch off the circuit power.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely.
  3. Isolate at least one resistor terminal when necessary.
  4. Select resistance mode (Ω).
  5. Connect the probes across the resistor.
  6. Read the measured resistance.
Critical safety rule: Never connect a multimeter configured for current measurement directly across a voltage source. This can create a short circuit and damage the meter, circuit or cause injury. Never measure resistance on an energized circuit.

ResistanceAcross the isolated/de-energized component

14. Practical Circuit Analysis

Example 1: LED Current-Limiting Resistor

A 5 V supply drives an LED through a series resistor. Assume the LED forward voltage is 2 V and the target current is 10 mA. Calculate the resistor value.

VR = 5 - 2 = 3 V

R = VR / I
R = 3 / 0.01 = 300 Ω

A standard 330 Ω resistor may be selected, giving an approximate current of:

I = 3 / 330 ≈ 9.09 mA
Answer: 300 Ω is the calculated value. A 330 Ω standard resistor gives approximately 9.1 mA under the stated assumptions.

Example 2: Current Through a Sensor Resistor

A 3.3 V supply is applied across a 1 kΩ resistor. Calculate current and resistor power.

I = 3.3 / 1000 = 0.0033 A
I = 3.3 mA

P = V² / R
P = 3.3² / 1000
P = 0.01089 W = 10.89 mW
Answer: Current = 3.3 mA; power = 10.89 mW.

Example 3: Battery Operating Time (Ideal Approximation)

A 2000 mAh battery supplies a constant current of 400 mA. Estimate the ideal operating time.

Operating time = Capacity / Current

= 2000 mAh / 400 mA
= 5 hours
Answer: 5 hours ideally.

Actual battery runtime may be lower because of discharge-rate effects, temperature, cutoff voltage, battery condition and converter efficiency.

Practical Troubleshooting Procedure

  1. Inspect the circuit visually for incorrect wiring or damaged components.
  2. Verify the supply voltage and polarity.
  3. Measure voltage at important circuit nodes.
  4. Measure current using a correctly configured series connection.
  5. Calculate power for components that may be overheating.
  6. Compare measured values with expected values.
  7. Identify and correct the source of the fault.

16. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is voltage?

Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. It is the energy transferred per unit charge and is measured in volts.

Q2. What is electric current?

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge. Its SI unit is the ampere.

Q3. What is resistance?

Resistance is the opposition offered to current flow. Its SI unit is the ohm.

Q4. State Ohm's law.

For an ohmic conductor under constant physical conditions, current is directly proportional to voltage. V = IR.

Q5. What is electrical power?

Electrical power is the rate of electrical energy transfer. For a DC circuit, P = VI.

Q6. What is the SI unit of power?

The watt (W).

Q7. What is the difference between AC and DC?

DC maintains one direction of current flow, whereas AC periodically reverses direction.

Q8. Why does a resistor become hot?

It dissipates electrical energy as heat. The power is given by P = I²R for a resistor.

Q9. How is voltage measured?

Using a voltmeter or DMM connected in parallel across the measurement points.

Q10. How is current measured?

Using an ammeter or DMM inserted in series with the circuit path.

Q11. Why should resistance not be measured in a powered circuit?

The meter's resistance-measurement circuit applies its own test signal. External circuit voltage can cause incorrect readings or damage the meter.

Q12. What is the difference between power and energy?

Power is the rate of energy transfer, while energy is the total amount transferred over time.

Q13. What is conductance?

Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance and is measured in siemens.

Q14. Why should a resistor have a suitable power rating?

Its rating must safely handle the heat generated during operation, including required thermal derating and design margin.

Q15. What happens if a resistor is connected directly across a voltage source with too low a resistance?

It may draw excessive current and dissipate excessive power, potentially causing overheating or failure.

17. Practice Problems

Numerical Questions

  1. A charge of 120 C passes through a conductor in 60 seconds. Calculate current.
  2. A 9 V supply is connected across a 3 kΩ resistor. Calculate current in mA.
  3. A 2 A current flows through a 15 Ω resistor. Calculate voltage.
  4. A 24 V supply produces a current of 0.5 A. Calculate resistance.
  5. A 12 Ω resistor carries 3 A. Calculate power.
  6. A 100 Ω resistor has 10 V across it. Calculate current and power.
  7. A 50 W lamp operates for 8 hours. Calculate energy in kWh.
  8. Calculate conductance of a 2 kΩ resistor.
  9. A 12 V supply drives an LED with 2 V forward drop at 20 mA. Calculate the series resistor.
  10. A 12 V, 24 W DC load operates continuously. Calculate its current and resistance, assuming constant resistance.

Practical Tasks

  1. Measure a battery's DC voltage using a DMM.
  2. Measure a resistor's value with the circuit switched off.
  3. Build a 5 V resistor circuit and calculate its expected current.
  4. Compare the calculated current with the measured current.
  5. Calculate resistor power and verify that its rating is sufficient.
  6. Measure the voltage drop across a resistor under load.
  7. Practice identifying the correct DMM terminals for voltage and current measurements.

18. Lecture Summary

  • Voltage is electrical potential difference.
  • Current is the rate of flow of charge.
  • Resistance opposes current flow.
  • Ohm's law relates voltage, current and resistance.
  • Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer.
  • Electrical energy depends on power and operating time.
  • Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance.
  • Resistor power ratings must be selected according to actual dissipation and thermal conditions.
  • Voltage is measured in parallel, while current is measured in series.
  • Safe DMM operation is essential for accurate measurements and equipment protection.
