1. Lecture Overview

An analog electronic system consists of interconnected functional blocks that receive an input, process or modify it, and produce an output. Each block performs a specific function such as sensing, amplification, filtering, signal conversion or control.

Placement objective: Understand the architecture of an analog electronic system, identify the function of each block, trace signal flow, and analyze practical circuits using block diagrams.

Prerequisites

  • Lecture 1: Analog and Digital Signals
  • Basic knowledge of voltage, current, resistance and electronic signals.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the meaning of a functional block in electronics.
  • Identify the major blocks of an analog electronic system.
  • Explain sensor, signal-conditioning and amplifier functions.
  • Understand filters, converters, controllers and output stages.
  • Trace signal flow through a practical system.
  • Perform basic gain, attenuation and loading calculations.
  • Identify common system-level faults.

2. What Is an Analog Electronic System?

An analog electronic system is a combination of electronic circuits that process continuously varying electrical signals. The system may measure a physical quantity, condition a signal, amplify it, filter unwanted components and drive an output device.

Examples include:

  • Temperature monitoring systems.
  • Audio amplifiers.
  • Regulated DC power supplies.
  • Sensor measurement circuits.
  • Battery monitoring circuits.
  • Industrial signal-conditioning modules.
  • Overvoltage and undervoltage protection systems.

Functional Block

A functional block represents a part of a system that performs a particular operation. It may contain one component or many interconnected components.

For example, an amplifier block may contain a transistor or an operational amplifier, resistors, capacitors and biasing components. The block diagram describes its function rather than every physical component connection.

4. Input and Sensor Block

The input block receives information from the external environment. A sensor converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal.

Common Sensors

SensorMeasured quantityTypical output behavior
ThermistorTemperatureResistance changes with temperature.
LDRLight intensityResistance changes with illumination.
PhotodiodeLightProduces a photocurrent.
MicrophoneSoundProduces an electrical audio signal.
Strain gaugeMechanical strainSmall resistance variation.

Example: Thermistor Voltage Divider

A thermistor can be connected with a fixed resistor to create a voltage divider. The output voltage changes when thermistor resistance changes.

For the circuit shown, the output is measured across the fixed resistor R:

Vout = VCC × R / (Rth + R)

Solved Example

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 5 V
  • Thermistor resistance = 10 kΩ
  • Fixed resistance = 10 kΩ
Vout = 5 × 10 / (10 + 10)

Vout = 2.5 V
Answer: The output voltage is 2.5 V.

If the thermistor resistance changes, the output voltage also changes. Whether the voltage rises or falls with temperature depends on whether the thermistor is NTC or PTC and on its position in the divider.

5. Signal Conditioning Block

Sensor outputs are not always suitable for direct connection to the next stage. They may be too small, noisy, offset, nonlinear or outside the required input range.

Signal conditioning modifies the signal to make it suitable for measurement or processing.

Common Signal-Conditioning Operations

  • Amplification of weak signals.
  • Attenuation of high-voltage signals.
  • Filtering of unwanted noise.
  • Level shifting or offset adjustment.
  • Buffering to reduce loading effects.
  • Isolation between circuit sections where required.
  • Linearization of sensor response when necessary.

Example: Voltage Attenuation

Suppose a sensor produces a maximum of 10 V, but the next stage accepts a maximum input of 5 V. A resistor divider can reduce the voltage.

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Choose R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ.

Vout = 10 × 10 / (10 + 10)

Vout = 5 V
A resistor divider is not automatically suitable for every signal. Its output depends on load impedance, resistor tolerance and input voltage. Check the maximum input rating and transient conditions of the receiving circuit.

6. Amplifier Block

An amplifier increases the magnitude of a signal. Depending on the application, it may provide voltage gain, current gain or power gain.

Types of Amplifiers by Function

Amplifier typePurpose
Voltage amplifierIncreases signal voltage.
Current amplifierProvides greater output current capability.
Power amplifierDelivers power to a load.
Buffer amplifierProvides isolation and drive capability with little or no voltage gain.
Instrumentation amplifierAmplifies small differential signals while rejecting common-mode signals.

Voltage Gain

Av = Vout / Vin

Voltage gain in decibels:

Av(dB) = 20 log10 |Vout / Vin|

Solved Example

An amplifier receives an input signal of 0.2 V and produces an output signal of 2 V. Calculate its voltage gain.

Av = 2 / 0.2 = 10

Av(dB) = 20 log10(10) = 20 dB
Answer: Voltage gain = 10, or 20 dB.

Practical Amplifier Considerations

  • Input and output voltage limits.
  • Supply voltage and current requirements.
  • Input impedance and output impedance.
  • Frequency response and bandwidth.
  • Noise and distortion.
  • Power dissipation and thermal limits.
  • Stability and feedback requirements.

7. Filter Block

A filter is a frequency-selective circuit. It allows certain frequency components to pass while reducing others.

Types of Filters

FilterFunctionExample application
Low-passPasses low frequencies and attenuates higher frequencies.Removing high-frequency noise from sensor signals.
High-passPasses high frequencies and attenuates lower frequencies.Removing DC offset from an AC signal.
Band-passPasses a selected frequency band.Audio and communication circuits.
Band-stopAttenuates a selected frequency band.Rejecting unwanted interference.

RC Low-Pass Filter

A first-order RC low-pass filter consists of a resistor in series with the input and a capacitor connected from the output node to ground. The output is taken across the capacitor.

The cutoff frequency of an ideal first-order RC low-pass filter is:

fc = 1 / (2πRC)

Solved Example

Given R = 10 kΩ and C = 0.1 µF, calculate the cutoff frequency.

R = 10,000 Ω
C = 0.1 × 10⁻⁶ F

fc = 1 / (2 × π × 10,000 × 0.1 × 10⁻⁶)

fc ≈ 159.15 Hz
Answer: The cutoff frequency is approximately 159 Hz.

At the cutoff frequency, the magnitude response of an ideal first-order filter is approximately 0.707 of its passband value, equivalent to -3 dB.

8. Converter Block: ADC and DAC

Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)

An ADC converts an analog voltage or current into a digital code. It is commonly used when a microcontroller or digital processor must read an analog sensor.

Main ADC concepts:

  • Resolution.
  • Sampling rate.
  • Input voltage range.
  • Reference voltage.
  • Quantization error.
  • Conversion accuracy and settling time.

Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC)

A DAC converts a digital code into an analog output voltage or current. It is used in waveform generation, control systems and analog output interfaces.

ADCDAC
Analog to digitalDigital to analog
Reads sensor voltagesProduces analog output signals
Used for measurement and acquisitionUsed for waveform generation and control
Embedded-system example: A microcontroller reads a temperature sensor using its ADC and may control an analog output through a DAC or PWM-based output stage with suitable filtering.

9. Output and Load Block

The output block delivers the processed signal to a device or another system. The output may represent information or perform a physical action.

Common Output Devices

  • LED indicators.
  • Buzzers and speakers.
  • Relays.
  • Motors.
  • Displays.
  • Communication interfaces.
  • Control inputs to other electronic systems.

Output Driver

A signal-processing circuit may not be capable of supplying the current required by a load. An output driver provides the necessary current or voltage capability.

For example, a microcontroller GPIO pin should not normally drive a high-current motor directly. A transistor, MOSFET or suitable driver IC is used between the GPIO and the load.

The output device must be selected according to load voltage, current, power, switching requirements and thermal limits. Inductive loads such as relays and motors also require suitable transient suppression.

10. Power Supply Block

Every electronic system needs an appropriate power source. The power-supply block provides the voltage and current required by different circuit stages.

A typical regulated power supply may contain:

  1. Transformer or AC input stage, where applicable.
  2. Rectifier.
  3. Filter capacitor.
  4. Voltage regulator.
  5. Protection and distribution circuitry.

The exact architecture depends on whether the system uses an AC mains input, a battery, a DC adapter or another power source.

Practical Design Considerations

  • Correct voltage and current rating.
  • Acceptable ripple and noise.
  • Regulator dropout voltage.
  • Power dissipation and heat sinking.
  • Overcurrent and overvoltage protection.
  • Decoupling capacitors near IC supply pins.

11. Feedback and Control Block

Some electronic systems use feedback to compare the actual output with a desired reference value. The difference between these quantities is called the error signal.

The controller adjusts the system based on the feedback signal. For example, a regulated power supply may monitor its output voltage and adjust the control element to maintain the desired value.

Open-Loop vs Closed-Loop Systems

Open-loop systemClosed-loop system
No output feedback is used for correction.Output is measured and used for correction.
Cannot automatically correct output deviations.Can correct deviations within its operating limits.
Usually simpler.Requires feedback and stability considerations.
Example: basic fixed-duty timer control.Example: regulated voltage control.

12. Complete Practical Example: Temperature Monitoring System

Consider a temperature monitoring system that measures the temperature of an industrial machine and displays the result.

System Operation

  1. The temperature sensor detects the machine temperature.
  2. The sensor generates an electrical output.
  3. The conditioning stage filters or scales the signal if required.
  4. The ADC converts the analog signal into a digital value.
  5. The microcontroller calculates the temperature.
  6. The display presents the measured temperature.
  7. If the temperature exceeds a programmed threshold, an alarm or control output can be activated.

Example: ADC Voltage Calculation

Assume a 10-bit ADC uses a 3.3 V reference and receives a voltage of 1.65 V. Using the ideal ADC code approximation:

ADC code ≈ (Vin / Vref) × (2ⁿ - 1)

ADC code ≈ (1.65 / 3.3) × 1023

ADC code ≈ 511.5
Answer: The ADC code is approximately 512.

This calculation assumes an ideal ADC and a suitable input range. The exact code and transfer behavior depend on the ADC architecture and datasheet specifications.

13. System-Level Fault Analysis

SymptomPossible faulty blockDiagnostic approach
No output from systemPower supply or output stageCheck supply rails, fuses and output connections.
Incorrect sensor readingSensor or conditioning stageVerify sensor response and conditioning gain.
Excessive noiseGrounding, filter or wiringInspect grounding and compare waveforms before and after filtering.
Amplifier output saturatedAmplifier or biasingCheck input range, supply rails, gain and load.
Wrong ADC readingADC reference or input circuitVerify reference voltage, input range and grounding.
Display not respondingDigital interface or output blockCheck communication, supply and interface signals.
Unstable regulated voltageFeedback or power stageInspect feedback components, load and stability.

Practical Troubleshooting Method

  1. Confirm the power supply voltage and ground connections.
  2. Measure the input signal at the first block.
  3. Measure the output of each block in sequence.
  4. Compare the measured value with the expected value.
  5. Identify the first block where the signal becomes incorrect.
  6. Check its components, connections and datasheet specifications.
  7. Repeat the test after correcting the fault.
Industry practice: Troubleshoot by following the signal path. Measuring at block boundaries helps isolate the faulty section without replacing components randomly.

14. Component and Datasheet Selection

When selecting components for an analog system, engineers must consider the electrical requirements of the entire signal path.

ComponentImportant datasheet parameters
SensorMeasurement range, sensitivity, accuracy, response time and supply requirements.
Operational amplifierSupply range, input offset voltage, input bias current, bandwidth, slew rate and output swing.
ResistorResistance, tolerance, power rating and temperature coefficient.
CapacitorCapacitance, voltage rating, tolerance, ESR and temperature characteristics.
ADCResolution, sampling rate, reference range, input range and accuracy.
Voltage regulatorInput range, output voltage, current limit, dropout voltage and thermal characteristics.
MOSFETDrain-source voltage, continuous current, gate threshold, RDS(on) and thermal limits.
Never select a component using only one parameter. For example, a MOSFET's gate threshold voltage does not establish that it is fully enhanced at the required load current. Check RDS(on) at the actual gate drive voltage.

15. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is an analog electronic system?

It is a system that processes continuously varying electrical signals using interconnected analog circuit blocks.

Q2. What is a functional block diagram?

It is a simplified representation of a system showing its major functional sections and the flow of signals between them.

Q3. What is signal conditioning?

Signal conditioning prepares an input signal for further processing through amplification, filtering, attenuation, buffering or other modifications.

Q4. Why is an amplifier used in a sensor system?

It increases the magnitude of a weak sensor signal so that the next stage can process it reliably.

Q5. What is the function of a filter?

A filter passes desired frequency components and attenuates unwanted frequency components.

Q6. What is the difference between ADC and DAC?

An ADC converts analog information into digital codes, whereas a DAC converts digital codes into an analog output.

Q7. Why is a buffer amplifier used?

A buffer provides isolation between stages and helps prevent loading of a signal source.

Q8. What is a closed-loop system?

A closed-loop system uses feedback from its output to influence the system's operation.

Q9. What is the purpose of a power-supply block?

It provides the required operating voltages and currents to the electronic circuit blocks.

Q10. How would you troubleshoot an analog system with no output?

First check the power supply and ground, then follow the signal path stage by stage to identify where the signal disappears or becomes incorrect.

Q11. Why should a sensor not always be connected directly to an ADC?

The sensor output may require scaling, buffering, filtering, level shifting or protection to meet the ADC input requirements.

Q12. What is loading effect?

Loading occurs when the input impedance of a connected stage affects the output voltage or operation of the preceding stage.

16. Practice Problems and Practical Tasks

Numerical Problems

  1. An amplifier receives 100 mV and produces 1.5 V. Calculate voltage gain.
  2. A 12 V sensor output must be scaled to 3 V using a divider. If R2 = 10 kΩ, calculate R1 for an ideal unloaded divider.
  3. Calculate the cutoff frequency of an RC filter with R = 4.7 kΩ and C = 0.1 µF.
  4. A 12-bit ADC uses a 5 V reference. Calculate its number of codes and nominal voltage step.
  5. A sensor produces 0.8 V, and an amplifier has a gain of 5. Calculate the output voltage, assuming ideal linear operation.

Practical Tasks

  1. Draw a block diagram of a temperature monitoring system.
  2. Identify the sensor, conditioning, processing and output stages.
  3. Build or simulate an RC low-pass filter using suitable component values.
  4. Measure its input and output signals at different frequencies.
  5. Build a voltage divider and measure the output at different input voltages.
  6. Trace the signal path in a regulated DC power supply.
  7. Prepare a troubleshooting table for a sensor system with an incorrect output.

17. Lecture Summary

  • An analog electronic system is made of interconnected functional blocks.
  • The sensor or input block detects a physical quantity.
  • Signal conditioning prepares signals for further processing.
  • Amplifiers increase voltage, current or power as required.
  • Filters control the frequency content of signals.
  • ADCs and DACs connect analog and digital domains.
  • Output drivers provide the required load-driving capability.
  • Power supplies provide operating energy to circuit stages.
  • Feedback systems use output information to correct system behavior.
  • Signal tracing is a systematic method of troubleshooting.
Placement checkpoint: You should now be able to draw and explain a basic analog system block diagram, describe the function of each stage, perform simple gain and filter calculations, and identify likely faults in a signal-processing chain.
