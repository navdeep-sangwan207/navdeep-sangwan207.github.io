Lesson 2: Analog Signals and Analog Systems

Understand analog signals, their characteristics, common waveforms and the working principles of analog electronic systems.

1. What is a Signal?

Definition: A signal is a physical quantity that varies with time or another independent variable and carries information.

In electronic systems, signals are commonly represented using voltage or current.

Examples of Signals

  • Audio signal from a microphone.
  • Voltage generated by a temperature sensor.
  • Output voltage of a light sensor.
  • Electrical signals transmitted in communication systems.
  • Voltage signals from industrial sensors.

2. What is an Analog Signal?

Definition: An analog signal is a signal whose amplitude can take continuously varying values within a specified range.

An analog signal represents information through continuously varying electrical quantities such as voltage and current.

Characteristics of Analog Signals

  • Continuous amplitude variation.
  • Can represent real-world physical quantities.
  • May vary with time.
  • Can be affected by electrical noise.
  • Can be amplified, filtered and processed.

3. What is an Analog System?

Definition: An analog system is an electronic system that processes continuous signals using analog electronic circuits.

Analog systems accept analog inputs, process them through electronic circuits and produce analog outputs.

Basic Block Diagram

Analog Input → Analog Processing Circuit → Analog Output

Examples

  • Audio amplifier systems.
  • Analog temperature measurement systems.
  • Radio receiver circuits.
  • Analog voltage regulator systems.
  • Sensor signal conditioning circuits.

4. Important Parameters of Analog Signals

1. Amplitude

Amplitude is the maximum magnitude of a signal measured relative to its reference level.

For a sine wave centered around zero, amplitude is the peak value.

2. Time Period (T)

Time period is the time required for a periodic signal to complete one full cycle.

T = Time required for one complete cycle

3. Frequency (f)

Frequency is the number of complete cycles produced by a periodic signal in one second.

f = 1 / T

Unit: Hertz (Hz).

4. Phase

Phase describes the relative position of a periodic waveform with respect to a reference waveform or time origin.

It is commonly measured in degrees or radians.

5. Offset

DC offset is the constant voltage level added to an alternating signal. It shifts the waveform above or below the zero reference.

5. Types of Analog Waveforms

1. Sine Wave

A sine wave is a periodic waveform commonly found in AC electrical systems.

It is widely used in power systems, audio circuits and communication systems.

v(t) = Vm sin(2πft + φ)

2. Square Wave

A square wave alternates between two voltage levels. It is commonly used in timing and switching applications.

3. Triangular Wave

A triangular wave consists of linear rising and falling sections.

4. Sawtooth Wave

A sawtooth waveform consists of a gradual voltage change followed by a rapid reset, or the reverse.

5. Complex Waveform

A complex waveform contains multiple frequency components and may not have a simple sinusoidal shape.

6. Analog Signal vs Digital Signal

Analog Signal Digital Signal
Amplitude varies continuously. Uses discrete amplitude levels.
Can represent a continuous range of values. Commonly uses binary logic levels.
Noise can directly alter the signal amplitude. Logic-level thresholds provide noise tolerance.
Used in analog amplifiers and sensor circuits. Used in digital processors and controllers.

7. Practical Example: Microphone and Audio Amplifier

Working Principle:

A microphone converts sound waves into a corresponding electrical signal.

This electrical signal is generally weak. An audio amplifier increases its voltage or power to drive a speaker.

The speaker converts the amplified electrical signal back into sound.

Signal Flow

Sound → Microphone → Audio Amplifier → Speaker → Sound

8. Numerical Example

Question:

An analog signal has a frequency of 50 Hz. Calculate its time period.

Given:
  • Frequency (f) = 50 Hz
Formula:
T = 1 / f
Calculation:
T = 1 / 50 = 0.02 seconds
Answer:

Time period = 20 milliseconds.

Lesson Summary

  • A signal carries information through a varying physical quantity.
  • An analog signal has continuously varying amplitude.
  • An analog system processes continuous electrical signals.
  • Amplitude, frequency, time period and phase are important signal parameters.
  • Sine, square, triangular and sawtooth are common waveforms.
  • Analog systems are used in audio, communication, instrumentation and embedded electronics.
