Lesson 4: Voltage, Current and Power

Understand the fundamental electrical quantities, their units, mathematical relationships, measurement methods and practical applications in electronic circuits.

1. Introduction to Electrical Quantities

Electrical circuits operate using electrical quantities such as voltage, current, resistance and power.

These quantities are essential for understanding, designing, testing and troubleshooting electronic circuits.

Four Important Electrical Quantities

  • Voltage (V)
  • Current (I)
  • Resistance (R)
  • Power (P)

2. Voltage

Definition: Voltage is the electric potential difference between two points in an electrical circuit.

Voltage represents the energy transferred per unit electric charge between two points.

V = W / Q

Where:

  • V = Voltage in volts (V)
  • W = Energy or work in joules (J)
  • Q = Electric charge in coulombs (C)

Unit of Voltage

The SI unit of voltage is the volt (V).

Types of Voltage

1. DC Voltage:

DC voltage has a fixed polarity. Its magnitude may be constant or vary with time.

2. AC Voltage:

AC voltage periodically changes its magnitude and polarity.

Practical Examples:
  • 1.5 V battery
  • 5 V microcontroller supply
  • 12 V DC power supply
  • 230 V AC mains supply (nominal in India)

3. Electric Current

Definition: Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor or circuit.
I = Q / t

Where:

  • I = Current in amperes (A)
  • Q = Electric charge in coulombs (C)
  • t = Time in seconds (s)

Unit of Current

The SI unit of electric current is the ampere (A).

Types of Current

1. Direct Current (DC):

Current that flows in one direction. Its magnitude may be constant or time-varying.

2. Alternating Current (AC):

Current that periodically reverses direction.

Conventional Current and Electron Flow

  • Conventional current is defined as flowing from positive to negative in the external circuit.
  • Electron flow in a metallic conductor is from negative to positive.

4. Electrical Resistance

Definition: Resistance is the opposition offered by a material or component to the flow of electric current.

Resistance is measured in ohms (Ω).

Factors Affecting Resistance

  • Length of the conductor.
  • Cross-sectional area.
  • Material of the conductor.
  • Temperature.

Resistance Formula

R = ρL / A

Where:

  • R = Resistance in ohms (Ω)
  • ρ = Resistivity of the material (Ω·m)
  • L = Length of conductor (m)
  • A = Cross-sectional area (m²)

5. Ohm's Law

Definition: Ohm's law states that, for a conductor under constant physical conditions, current is directly proportional to the voltage across it.
V = IR

Where:

  • V = Voltage (V)
  • I = Current (A)
  • R = Resistance (Ω)

Rearranged Equations

I = V / R
R = V / I
Numerical Example:

A 10 Ω resistor is connected across a 20 V DC supply. Calculate the current.

Given:

  • V = 20 V
  • R = 10 Ω

Calculation:

I = V / R = 20 / 10 = 2 A
Answer: The current flowing through the resistor is 2 A.

6. Electrical Power

Definition: Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted in a circuit.
P = W / t

Where:

  • P = Power in watts (W)
  • W = Energy in joules (J)
  • t = Time in seconds (s)

Electrical Power Formula

P = VI

Using Ohm's law, we can also write:

P = I²R
P = V² / R

Unit of Power

The SI unit of power is the watt (W).

Numerical Example:

A circuit operates at 12 V and draws 2 A. Calculate its electrical power.

P = VI = 12 × 2 = 24 W
Answer: The electrical power is 24 watts.

7. AC Power and DC Power

DC Power

For a DC circuit, power is calculated using:

P = VI

AC Power

In a single-phase AC circuit, real power for sinusoidal voltage and current is:

P = Vrms Irms cos φ

Where:

  • Vrms = RMS voltage
  • Irms = RMS current
  • cos φ = Power factor

The power factor represents the phase relationship between voltage and current for sinusoidal conditions.

8. Voltage, Current and Power Comparison

Quantity Symbol SI Unit Measuring Instrument
Voltage V Volt (V) Voltmeter
Current I Ampere (A) Ammeter
Resistance R Ohm (Ω) Ohmmeter
Power P Watt (W) Wattmeter

9. Measurement Using a Digital Multimeter

A digital multimeter (DMM) is commonly used to measure voltage, current and resistance.

Voltage Measurement

  • Select the appropriate AC or DC voltage mode.
  • Connect the probes in parallel across the component.
  • Observe the measured voltage.

Current Measurement

  • Select the appropriate current range.
  • Use the correct current input terminal.
  • Open the circuit and connect the meter in series.
  • Never connect an ammeter directly across a voltage source.

Resistance Measurement

  • Switch off circuit power.
  • Discharge capacitors safely.
  • Select resistance mode.
  • Measure across the isolated component when necessary.
Safety Note: Do not measure resistance on an energized circuit. Use properly rated test equipment and safe procedures when working with mains voltage.

Lesson Summary

  • Voltage is the potential difference between two points.
  • Current is the rate of flow of electric charge.
  • Resistance opposes current flow.
  • Ohm's law is V = IR.
  • Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer.
  • DC power is P = VI.
  • AC real power for sinusoidal conditions is P = VI cos φ using RMS values.
  • A digital multimeter measures voltage, current and resistance.
