1. Learning Objectives

This lecture consolidates the fundamentals of analog electronics and develops the ability to analyse practical circuits systematically.

  • Identify circuit nodes, branches, loops and reference ground.
  • Apply Ohm's law, KCL and KVL to practical circuits.
  • Analyse series, parallel and combination networks.
  • Calculate voltage drops, branch currents and power dissipation.
  • Understand loading effects and measurement errors.
  • Analyse basic RC and RL circuit behaviour.
  • Use a DMM and oscilloscope for circuit troubleshooting.
  • Develop a structured approach to diagnosing circuit faults.
  • Prepare for technical interviews and numerical questions.

2. Fundamentals of Analog Circuit Analysis

Analog circuit analysis involves determining voltages, currents, power and signal behaviour at different points in a circuit.

2.1 Important Circuit Terminology

TermMeaning
NodeA connection point between two or more circuit elements. All ideal points connected by uninterrupted wire belong to the same node.
BranchA path between two nodes containing a circuit element or series-connected elements.
LoopAny closed path in a circuit.
MeshA loop that does not contain another loop inside it.
GroundA selected reference potential, commonly assigned 0 V.
Reference directionAn assumed direction for current or voltage used during analysis.

2.2 Basic Circuit Diagram

Figure 1: Basic DC circuit showing a source, resistor, current direction and reference ground.

2.3 Reference Ground

Ground is a reference point for voltage measurements. If a node is selected as ground, its voltage is defined as 0 V. Other node voltages are measured relative to this point.

Circuit ground does not automatically mean earth ground. In many electronic circuits, ground is simply the common reference node.

3. Essential Circuit Laws

3.1 Ohm's Law

V = IR

For a resistor, voltage is proportional to current when its resistance is constant under the operating conditions.

3.2 Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Sum of currents entering a node = Sum of currents leaving a node

KCL is based on conservation of electric charge.

Figure 2: KCL example with entering and leaving branch currents.

5 mA + 3 mA = 4 mA + 4 mA

3.3 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop = 0

KVL is based on conservation of energy.

For a 12 V source and two series resistors with voltage drops of 4 V and 8 V:

+12 V − 4 V − 8 V = 0

3.4 Passive Sign Convention

When current enters the terminal marked positive for a component, the component absorbs power under the passive sign convention.

P = VI
  • Positive power: the element absorbs power.
  • Negative power: the element delivers power.

4. Series, Parallel and Combination Circuit Analysis

4.1 Series Circuit

In a series circuit, the same current flows through every element.

Req = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

Figure 3: Series resistor network.

4.2 Parallel Circuit

In a parallel circuit, all branches have the same voltage.

1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...

Figure 4: Parallel resistor network.

4.3 Solved Series Circuit

Three resistors of 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ are connected in series across a 12 V supply.

Req = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 kΩ
I = V/R = 12/6000 = 2 mA

Voltage drops:

  • V1 = 2 mA × 1 kΩ = 2 V
  • V2 = 2 mA × 2 kΩ = 4 V
  • V3 = 2 mA × 3 kΩ = 6 V
Check using KVL: 12 V − 2 V − 4 V − 6 V = 0.

4.4 Solved Parallel Circuit

Two resistors of 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ are connected in parallel across 12 V.

Req = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 2 kΩ
Itotal = 12/2000 = 6 mA

Branch currents:

  • I1 = 12/6000 = 2 mA
  • I2 = 12/3000 = 4 mA
Check using KCL: Itotal = 2 mA + 4 mA = 6 mA.

4.5 Series-Parallel Combination

For a mixed network, reduce the simplest series or parallel groups first. Continue until a single equivalent resistance remains.

Figure 5: One series resistor connected to a parallel resistor pair.

Example: R1 = 2 kΩ, R2 = 6 kΩ and R3 = 3 kΩ. R2 and R3 are parallel.

R23 = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 2 kΩ
Req = R1 + R23 = 2 + 2 = 4 kΩ

For a 12 V source:

Itotal = 12/4000 = 3 mA

Voltage across R1 = 3 mA × 2 kΩ = 6 V.

Voltage across the parallel network = 12 − 6 = 6 V.

  • Current through R2 = 6/6000 = 1 mA.
  • Current through R3 = 6/3000 = 2 mA.
KCL check: 1 mA + 2 mA = 3 mA.

5. Voltage Divider and Loading Effect

A voltage divider produces a fraction of the input voltage using two or more series resistors.

Figure 6: Voltage divider with output across R2.

Vout = Vin × R2/(R1 + R2)

5.1 Solved Example

Vin = 12 V, R1 = 10 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ.

Vout = 12 × 10/(10 + 10) = 6 V

5.2 Loading Effect

When a load resistor is connected across the output, it appears in parallel with R2. This changes the effective lower resistance and reduces the output voltage.

Rlower = R2 || RL = (R2 × RL)/(R2 + RL)
Vout(loaded) = Vin × Rlower/(R1 + Rlower)

Example: Vin = 12 V, R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ and RL = 10 kΩ.

Rlower = 10 kΩ || 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ
Vout = 12 × 5/(10 + 5) = 4 V

The unloaded output was 6 V, but the loaded output becomes 4 V.

Voltage dividers are suitable for reference and signal-conditioning applications when loading is controlled. For loads requiring significant current, use a buffer or an appropriately designed regulator.

6. Current Divider Rule

In a parallel resistor network, the total current divides among branches. Branch current is inversely proportional to branch resistance.

6.1 Two-Resistor Current Divider

I1 = Itotal × R2/(R1 + R2)
I2 = Itotal × R1/(R1 + R2)

Example: A total current of 6 mA enters a parallel network containing 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ.

I1 (through 2 kΩ) = 6 × 4/(2 + 4) = 4 mA
I2 (through 4 kΩ) = 6 × 2/(2 + 4) = 2 mA
The lower-resistance branch carries the higher current.

6.2 General Current Divider

For a branch with resistance Rk in a parallel network:

Ik = Itotal × (1/Rk) / Σ(1/Rj)

Here, the denominator is the total conductance of all parallel branches.

7. Power Analysis and Component Selection

7.1 Power in a Resistor

P = VI = I²R = V²/R

Power must be checked when selecting a resistor. The resistor's specified power rating must be sufficient for the actual dissipation under the intended operating conditions.

7.2 Solved Example

A 1 kΩ resistor is connected across 12 V.

P = V²/R = 12²/1000 = 0.144 W

A 0.25 W resistor may be suitable under appropriate ambient and derating conditions, but the manufacturer's specifications must be checked.

7.3 Power in Capacitors and Inductors

Ideal capacitors and inductors store and return energy rather than continuously dissipating it. Real components have losses, such as capacitor ESR and inductor winding resistance and core losses.

ComponentEnergy storedImportant rating
ResistorDoes not ideally store energyPower and working voltage
Capacitor½CV²Voltage, ripple current, ESR and temperature
Inductor½LI²Inductance, saturation current and thermal rating

8. Basic RC and RL Circuit Analysis

8.1 RC Time Constant

τ = RC

For a charging capacitor:

Vc(t) = Vs(1 − e−t/RC)

For a discharging capacitor:

Vc(t) = V0e−t/RC

8.2 RL Time Constant

τ = L/R

For a series RL circuit with a DC step input:

i(t) = (Vs/R)(1 − e−tR/L)

8.3 Practical Interpretation

  • At one time constant, a charging capacitor reaches about 63.2% of its final voltage.
  • At one time constant, a discharging capacitor retains about 36.8% of its initial voltage.
  • At about five time constants, the transient is approximately complete for many practical calculations.
  • Inductor current changes gradually rather than instantaneously.

9. AC Circuit Fundamentals

9.1 RMS Value

RMS is the effective value of an alternating voltage or current. For a sinusoidal waveform:

Vrms = Vpeak/√2
Irms = Ipeak/√2

9.2 Reactance

Xc = 1/(2πfC)
XL = 2πfL

9.3 Impedance

Impedance is the total opposition offered by a circuit to AC. It includes resistance and reactance and is measured in ohms.

Z = R + jX

Where j is the imaginary unit and X is net reactance under the chosen sign convention.

9.4 Phase Relationships

ComponentRelationship in ideal sinusoidal AC
ResistorVoltage and current are in phase.
CapacitorCurrent leads voltage by 90°.
InductorCurrent lags voltage by 90°.

CapacitanceMeasures capacitance if supportedCapacitor testing

10.2 Oscilloscope

An oscilloscope displays voltage against time. It is useful for examining:

  • Waveform shape and amplitude.
  • Frequency and time period.
  • Rise time and fall time.
  • Noise, ripple and transient behaviour.
  • Phase difference between signals.
  • Switching waveforms.

10.3 Function Generator

A function generator provides test signals such as sine, square and triangle waves. It is used to test amplifier stages, filters and frequency response.

10.4 Bench Power Supply

A regulated bench supply provides controlled DC voltage. Current limiting helps protect the circuit during initial testing.

Never measure resistance or continuity on an energized circuit. When measuring current, connect the meter in series and use the correct input terminal and range. Incorrect current-mode connections can short a supply.

11. Systematic Circuit Troubleshooting

Effective troubleshooting starts with a clear sequence rather than randomly replacing components.

Figure 7: Structured troubleshooting sequence.

11.1 Step-by-Step Method

  1. Understand the symptom: Identify what is not working and under what conditions.
  2. Inspect visually: Check burnt components, cracked solder joints, damaged tracks and incorrect polarity.
  3. Verify supply: Measure input voltage and regulated output rails.
  4. Check ground: Verify common reference and return paths.
  5. Measure important nodes: Compare actual voltages with expected values.
  6. Check passive components: Measure resistors, capacitors and inductors as appropriate.
  7. Isolate the fault: Divide the circuit into functional sections.
  8. Repair and retest: Replace or correct the faulty part, then verify operation under load.

11.2 Example: No Output from a DC Power Supply

Rectifier outputNo rectified voltageInspect rectifier connections and diodes
Filter capacitorExcessive ripple or low capacitanceCheck capacitance, ESR and polarity

12. Practical Analog Electronics Interview Questions

Q1. What is analog electronics?
It deals with electronic circuits that process continuous-valued signals such as voltage and current.
Q2. What is the difference between analog and digital signals?
An analog signal varies continuously with time and amplitude, whereas a digital signal represents information using discrete levels.
Q3. What are passive components?
Components that do not provide power gain, such as resistors, capacitors and inductors.
Q4. What is the difference between a node and a branch?
A node is an electrical connection point; a branch is a path between two nodes containing circuit elements.
Q5. State Ohm's law.
For a resistor under constant operating conditions, V = IR.
Q6. State Kirchhoff's Current Law.
The algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero. Total entering current equals total leaving current.
Q7. State Kirchhoff's Voltage Law.
The algebraic sum of all voltages around a closed loop is zero.
Q8. What is the difference between series and parallel circuits?
Series elements carry the same current; parallel branches have the same voltage across them.
Q9. What happens if one resistor opens in a series circuit?
The current path is interrupted, so current through the entire ideal series path becomes zero.
Q10. What happens if one branch opens in a parallel circuit?
Current in that branch becomes zero, but the other branches can continue operating.
Q11. What is a voltage divider?
A network of series resistors that provides a fraction of the input voltage at an intermediate node.
Q12. Why does loading affect a voltage divider?
The load is in parallel with the lower divider resistor, changing the effective resistance and output voltage.
Q13. What is the current divider rule?
It calculates the current in a parallel branch. Current divides inversely with branch resistance.
Q14. What is resistor tolerance?
The maximum permitted percentage deviation from the nominal resistance.
Q15. Why is resistor power rating important?
If dissipated power exceeds its permitted rating, the resistor can overheat, drift in value or fail.
Q16. What is capacitance?
The ratio of electric charge stored to voltage across a capacitor, C = Q/V.
Q17. What happens to a capacitor in DC steady state?
An ideal capacitor behaves as an open circuit after charging is complete.
Q18. What happens to an ideal inductor in DC steady state?
It behaves as a short circuit after the transient has settled.
Q19. What is the RC time constant?
It is the product RC and determines the rate of charging and discharging in a first-order RC circuit.
Q20. What is the RL time constant?
For a series RL circuit, it is L/R.
Q21. What is the function of a low-pass filter?
It passes lower-frequency signals and attenuates higher-frequency signals.
Q22. What is the function of a high-pass filter?
It passes higher-frequency signals and attenuates lower-frequency signals.
Q23. What is impedance?
The total opposition to AC, including resistance and reactance.
Q24. What is RMS voltage?
It is the effective value of AC voltage. For a sinusoidal waveform, Vrms = Vpeak/√2.
Q25. Why is an oscilloscope used?
It displays voltage against time and helps inspect waveform shape, amplitude, frequency, timing, ripple and transients.
Q26. What is the difference between DMM and oscilloscope?
A DMM provides numerical measurements such as voltage and resistance. An oscilloscope displays signal behaviour over time.
Q27. What is capacitor ESR?
It is the effective series resistance associated with a real capacitor. High ESR can increase ripple and heating.
Q28. Why is a flyback diode used with a relay?
It provides a current path for the relay coil when switching off, limiting the inductive voltage transient.
Q29. What is the difference between earth ground and circuit ground?
Earth ground is a safety connection to earth. Circuit ground is a reference node and may or may not be connected to earth.
Q30. How would you troubleshoot a circuit with no output?
Check the input supply, ground, protection components, signal path, component values and output load systematically. Use measurements to isolate the faulty section.

13. Numerical Interview Problems

Problem 1: Series Resistance

Three resistors of 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ are connected in series. Find equivalent resistance.

Req = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 kΩ

Problem 2: Parallel Resistance

Two resistors of 4 kΩ and 4 kΩ are connected in parallel.

Req = 4/2 = 2 kΩ

Problem 3: Current Through a Resistor

A 2.2 kΩ resistor is connected across 11 V.

I = 11/2200 = 5 mA

Problem 4: Power Dissipation

A 100 Ω resistor carries 100 mA.

P = I²R = 0.1² × 100 = 1 W

Problem 5: Voltage Divider

Vin = 15 V, R1 = 20 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ. Calculate Vout across R2.

Vout = 15 × 10/(20 + 10) = 5 V

Problem 6: Loaded Voltage Divider

For the same divider, connect a 10 kΩ load across R2.

Rlower = 10 || 10 = 5 kΩ
Vout = 15 × 5/(20 + 5) = 3 V

Problem 7: RC Time Constant

R = 100 kΩ and C = 10 µF.

τ = 100,000 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 1 s

Problem 8: RL Time Constant

L = 50 mH and R = 25 Ω.

τ = 0.05/25 = 0.002 s = 2 ms

Problem 9: Capacitive Reactance

C = 10 µF and f = 50 Hz.

Xc = 1/(2π × 50 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 318.3 Ω

Problem 10: Inductive Reactance

L = 100 mH and f = 50 Hz.

XL = 2π × 50 × 0.1 ≈ 31.42 Ω

14. Practical Lab and Simulation Exercises

Experiment 1: Verify Ohm's Law

Objective: Verify the relationship between voltage, current and resistance.

Components: 1 kΩ resistor, current-limited DC supply, DMM and breadboard.

  1. Connect the resistor to the DC supply.
  2. Apply a few safe voltage levels.
  3. Measure the voltage across the resistor.
  4. Measure current using the correct series connection.
  5. Calculate V/I for each reading.
  6. Compare the calculated resistance with the nominal value.

Experiment 2: Verify KCL and KVL

Objective: Verify current and voltage relationships in a resistor network.

Components: DC supply and suitable resistors.

  1. Build a parallel network and measure total and branch currents.
  2. Check whether total current equals the sum of branch currents.
  3. Build a series network and measure each resistor voltage drop.
  4. Check whether the sum of voltage drops equals the supply voltage.

Experiment 3: Voltage Divider Loading

Objective: Observe the effect of a load on a voltage divider.

Components: Two 10 kΩ resistors, a 10 kΩ load resistor and a 12 V current-limited supply.

  1. Build the unloaded voltage divider.
  2. Measure its output voltage.
  3. Connect the load across the lower resistor.
  4. Measure the output voltage again.
  5. Compare measured results with the calculated values of 6 V and 4 V.

Experiment 4: RC Transient Response

Objective: Observe capacitor charging and discharging.

Components: 10 kΩ resistor, 100 µF capacitor, low-voltage DC supply and oscilloscope.

  1. Build the RC circuit.
  2. Measure the capacitor voltage during charging.
  3. Record the voltage at intervals related to the time constant.
  4. Discharge the capacitor safely through the resistor.
  5. Compare the waveform with the theoretical exponential response.

Experiment 5: Circuit Troubleshooting

Objective: Identify faults introduced into a low-voltage resistor circuit.

  1. Build a working resistor network and record expected node voltages.
  2. Introduce one controlled fault at a time, such as an open resistor or incorrect resistor value.
  3. Measure circuit voltages and currents.
  4. Identify the faulty section using the measurements.
  5. Restore the correct circuit and verify normal operation.
Use only low-voltage, current-limited supplies for these exercises. Disconnect power before changing circuit connections. Discharge capacitors safely before resistance or continuity measurements.

15. Common Mistakes in Circuit Analysis

MistakeCorrect approach
Adding parallel resistances directlyUse reciprocal conductance addition or the product-over-sum formula for two resistors.
Assuming different series currentsCurrent is identical through ideal series elements with no branching.
Assuming different parallel voltagesAll ideal parallel branches share the same two nodes and voltage.
Ignoring the load on a voltage dividerInclude the load in parallel with the lower divider resistor.
Using kΩ without converting unitsMaintain consistent units during calculations.
Confusing current direction with actual directionChoose a reference direction; a negative result means the actual direction is opposite.
Measuring resistance in a live circuitSwitch off and discharge the circuit first.
Ignoring component ratingsCheck voltage, current, power, temperature and derating specifications.
Replacing components without diagnosisUse measurements to isolate the faulty section first.
