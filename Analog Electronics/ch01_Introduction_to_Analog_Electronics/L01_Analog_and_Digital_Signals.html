3. What Is an Electrical Signal?

An electrical signal is a time-varying electrical quantity, usually voltage or current, that carries information from one point to another in an electronic system.

Signals are used to represent information such as temperature, pressure, sound, speed, light intensity and communication data.

Examples of electrical signals

  • Microphone output voltage representing sound.
  • Temperature sensor output representing temperature.
  • Photodiode current representing light intensity.
  • UART transmission signals representing digital data.
  • Oscillator output used as a clock or timing reference.
Important: A signal may be represented by voltage or current. The choice depends on the electronic circuit and application.

4. Analog Signals

An analog signal is a continuous-time signal whose amplitude can take any value within a specified range.

It can represent continuously varying physical quantities. For example, the voltage produced by a temperature sensor may change gradually as the temperature changes.

Characteristics of analog signals

  • Amplitude varies continuously within its operating range.
  • Can represent continuously varying physical quantities.
  • May be affected by electrical noise and interference.
  • Requires appropriate analog signal-conditioning circuits.
  • Can be processed using amplifiers, filters and analog circuits.

Analog signal waveform

Practical examples

  • Audio signals from microphones.
  • Analog temperature and pressure sensor outputs.
  • Light sensor voltage signals.
  • Analog control voltage in power electronics.
  • Signal outputs from function generators.

5. Digital Signals

A digital signal represents information using discrete signal levels. In a typical binary digital system, two logical states are used: logic 0 and logic 1.

A physical digital signal is usually represented by voltage levels corresponding to the specified logic states.

Characteristics of digital signals

  • Uses discrete information states.
  • Binary systems represent information using 0 and 1.
  • Suitable for digital logic and microcontroller systems.
  • Can be regenerated and processed using digital circuits.
  • Requires defined voltage thresholds for logic recognition.

Digital signal waveform

Practical examples

  • Microcontroller GPIO signals.
  • UART, SPI and I2C communication signals.
  • Digital clock signals.
  • Computer and processor logic signals.
  • Digital control signals used in embedded systems.
Important interview concept: Logic HIGH does not always mean exactly 5 V, and logic LOW does not always mean exactly 0 V. Actual logic thresholds depend on the IC family and its datasheet.

7. Types of Electrical Waveforms

7.1 Sine Wave

A sine wave is a periodic waveform commonly found in AC electrical systems, signal generators and oscillator circuits.

v(t) = Vm sin(2πft + φ)

Where:

  • Vm = Peak amplitude.
  • f = Frequency in hertz.
  • t = Time in seconds.
  • φ = Phase angle.

7.2 Square Wave

A square wave alternates between two levels. It is commonly used in digital circuits, clock generation and switching systems.

7.3 Triangular Wave

A triangular waveform has linearly increasing and decreasing voltage. It is used in waveform generation, modulation and testing applications.

7.4 Pulse Wave

A pulse waveform contains a HIGH interval and a LOW interval. It is used in timing circuits, digital communication and pulse-width modulation.

8. Important Signal Parameters

8.1 Amplitude

Amplitude indicates the magnitude of a signal measured relative to a reference level.

8.2 Peak Voltage (Vp)

Peak voltage is the maximum voltage measured from the reference level to the positive or negative peak.

8.3 Peak-to-Peak Voltage (Vpp)

Peak-to-peak voltage is the difference between the maximum positive and maximum negative voltage values.

Vpp = Vmax - Vmin

For a symmetrical sine wave:

Vpp = 2Vp

8.4 Time Period (T)

The time period is the time required to complete one full cycle of a periodic waveform.

8.5 Frequency (f)

Frequency is the number of complete cycles occurring per second. Its unit is hertz (Hz).

f = 1/T
T = 1/f

8.6 RMS Voltage

RMS (Root Mean Square) voltage is the effective value of an AC voltage, defined by its equivalent heating effect in a resistive load.

For a pure sinusoidal waveform:

VRMS = Vp / √2

Approximately:

VRMS = 0.707 × Vp
This 0.707 relationship applies to a pure sinusoidal waveform. Do not apply it directly to square, triangular or arbitrary waveforms.

8.7 Duty Cycle

Duty cycle is the percentage of one complete period during which a periodic pulse signal remains HIGH.

Duty Cycle = (TON / T) × 100%

8.8 Bandwidth

Bandwidth is the range of frequencies occupied or passed by a signal or system.

Bandwidth = fH - fL

Here, fH is the upper cutoff frequency and fL is the lower cutoff frequency.

9. Solved Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Calculate Frequency

A periodic signal completes one cycle in 5 milliseconds. Calculate its frequency.

Given:

  • T = 5 ms = 0.005 s

Formula:

f = 1/T

Substitution:

f = 1/0.005 = 200 Hz
Answer: Frequency = 200 Hz.

Problem 2: Calculate Time Period

A signal has a frequency of 2 kHz. Find its time period.

Given: f = 2000 Hz

T = 1/f
T = 1/2000 = 0.0005 s
Answer: T = 0.5 ms = 500 µs.

Problem 3: Calculate Peak-to-Peak Voltage

A symmetrical sine wave has a positive peak voltage of 12 V. Calculate its peak-to-peak voltage.

Given: Vp = 12 V

Vpp = 2 × Vp
Vpp = 2 × 12 = 24 V
Answer: Peak-to-peak voltage = 24 V.

Problem 4: Calculate RMS Voltage

A sinusoidal signal has a peak voltage of 20 V. Calculate its RMS voltage.

VRMS = Vp / √2
VRMS = 20 / 1.414
VRMS ≈ 14.14 V
Answer: RMS voltage ≈ 14.14 V.

Problem 5: Calculate Duty Cycle

A digital pulse has an ON time of 4 ms and a total period of 10 ms. Calculate its duty cycle.

Duty Cycle = (4/10) × 100
Duty Cycle = 40%
Answer: Duty cycle = 40%.

Problem 6: Calculate Bandwidth

An amplifier operates from 100 Hz to 10 kHz. Calculate its bandwidth.

Given:

  • Lower cutoff frequency = 100 Hz
  • Upper cutoff frequency = 10,000 Hz
BW = 10000 - 100 = 9900 Hz
Answer: Bandwidth = 9.9 kHz.

10. Practical Signal Measurement Using an Oscilloscope

An oscilloscope is an electronic test instrument used to display voltage variations with respect to time.

Important oscilloscope controls

  • Volts/Division: Sets the vertical voltage scale.
  • Time/Division: Sets the horizontal time scale.
  • Trigger: Stabilizes the displayed waveform.
  • Probe: Connects the test instrument to the circuit.
  • Coupling: Selects AC, DC or ground display modes, depending on the oscilloscope.

Example measurement

Suppose a waveform occupies 4 vertical divisions from its positive peak to its negative peak. The oscilloscope is set to 2 V/div.

Vpp = 4 × 2 = 8 V

If one complete cycle occupies 5 horizontal divisions and the time base is 0.2 ms/div:

T = 5 × 0.2 ms = 1 ms
f = 1/0.001 = 1000 Hz = 1 kHz
Practical result: Peak-to-peak voltage = 8 V and frequency = 1 kHz.

Practical measurement procedure

  1. Identify the signal test point and circuit ground.
  2. Connect the oscilloscope probe with the correct ground reference.
  3. Select a suitable probe attenuation setting.
  4. Adjust volts/division and time/division.
  5. Stabilize the waveform using the trigger control.
  6. Measure peak-to-peak voltage and time period.
  7. Calculate frequency using the measured time period.
Safety: Never connect an earth-referenced oscilloscope ground clip to a live mains conductor or an unknown high-voltage point. Use an appropriate isolated measurement arrangement and properly rated differential probe where required.

11. Applications in Electronics and Embedded Systems

Application Signal Type Purpose
Temperature sensor Analog Represents temperature as a voltage or current.
Microphone Analog Converts sound into an electrical signal.
UART communication Digital Transfers serial binary data.
PWM control Digital pulse Controls average power delivered to a load.
ADC interface Analog input Converts an analog quantity into a digital representation.
DAC interface Analog output Produces an analog output from digital data.

Connection with embedded systems

An embedded controller frequently interacts with both analog and digital signals.

  1. A sensor produces an analog voltage.
  2. An ADC converts the voltage into a digital value.
  3. The microcontroller processes the value.
  4. A GPIO or PWM output generates a digital control signal.
  5. An external driver circuit controls the connected load.
Placement relevance: Understanding the difference between analog sensor inputs, ADC measurements, digital communication and PWM outputs is important for Embedded Systems and Electronics R&D interviews.

12. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is an electrical signal?

An electrical signal is a varying voltage or current used to represent and transfer information in an electronic system.

Q2. What is the difference between analog and digital signals?

Analog signals have continuously varying amplitude, whereas digital signals represent information using discrete levels.

Q3. Define frequency.

Frequency is the number of complete cycles per second. Its SI unit is hertz.

Q4. What is the relationship between frequency and time period?

Frequency and time period are reciprocals: f = 1/T and T = 1/f.

Q5. What is peak-to-peak voltage?

It is the difference between the maximum positive and maximum negative voltage of a waveform.

Q6. What is RMS voltage?

RMS voltage is the effective AC voltage that produces the same heating effect in a resistive load as an equivalent DC voltage.

Q7. What is the RMS value of a sine wave?

VRMS = Vp/√2, approximately 0.707 times the peak voltage.

Q8. What is duty cycle?

Duty cycle is the percentage of a signal period for which the signal remains in the active or HIGH state.

Q9. What instrument is used to observe electrical waveforms?

An oscilloscope is used to observe voltage variations with respect to time.

Q10. Why are analog signals affected by noise?

Noise can directly alter the instantaneous amplitude of an analog signal, introducing measurement or information errors.

Q11. What is bandwidth?

Bandwidth is the difference between the upper and lower frequency limits of a specified signal or system.

Q12. What is the role of an ADC?

An Analog-to-Digital Converter converts an analog input into a digital representation for processing.

Q13. Can a digital signal have intermediate voltage values?

Yes. A physical digital signal transitions between voltage levels and may temporarily pass through intermediate values. Logic interpretation depends on the receiver's specified thresholds.

Q14. What is a clock signal?

A clock signal is a periodic timing signal used to coordinate operations in digital circuits and microcontrollers.

Q15. What is the difference between a square wave and a pulse waveform?

A symmetrical square wave generally has approximately equal HIGH and LOW durations. A pulse waveform can have unequal HIGH and LOW durations.
