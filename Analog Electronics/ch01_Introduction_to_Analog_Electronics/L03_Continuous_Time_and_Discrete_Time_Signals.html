Lesson 3: Continuous-Time and Discrete-Time Signals

Understand continuous-time and discrete-time signals, their mathematical representations, differences, sampling and practical applications.

1. Introduction to Time-Based Signals

An electrical signal can be represented as a mathematical function of time.

Depending on how the signal is defined with respect to time, it can be classified as a continuous-time signal or a discrete-time signal.

This classification is important in analog electronics, digital signal processing and communication systems.

2. Continuous-Time Signal

Definition: A continuous-time signal is a signal that is defined for every instant of time within its specified operating interval.

A continuous-time signal is generally represented using x(t), where t represents continuous time.

x(t) = A sin(2πft)

Where:

  • A = Signal amplitude
  • f = Signal frequency
  • t = Time

Characteristics

  • Defined at every instant of time.
  • Can vary continuously with time.
  • Commonly used to represent physical signals.
  • Used in analog electronic circuits.

Examples

  • Microphone output voltage.
  • Analog temperature sensor voltage.
  • AC mains voltage waveform.
  • Analog audio signals.

3. Discrete-Time Signal

Definition: A discrete-time signal is a signal defined only at specific, separate time instants.

A discrete-time signal is represented using x[n], where n is an integer-valued sample index.

x[n] = A sin(2πfnTs)

Where:

  • A = Signal amplitude
  • f = Signal frequency
  • n = Sample index
  • Ts = Sampling interval

Characteristics

  • Defined only at discrete time instants.
  • Represented as a sequence of samples.
  • Can have continuously varying sample amplitudes.
  • Used in digital signal processing systems.

Examples

  • Samples of a recorded audio signal.
  • Temperature readings taken every second.
  • Digital signal processing data sequences.
  • Sensor readings collected by a microcontroller.

4. Continuous-Time vs Discrete-Time Signals

Continuous-Time Signal Discrete-Time Signal
Defined at every instant of time. Defined at specific time instants.
Represented as x(t). Represented as x[n].
Time variable is continuous. Time index is discrete.
Common in analog systems. Common in digital signal processing.
Example: Microphone voltage waveform. Example: Digitally sampled audio sequence.
Important: A discrete-time signal is not necessarily digital. Its time instants are discrete, but its amplitude values may still be continuous. Quantization is required to represent amplitudes using finite digital levels.

5. Sampling of Analog Signals

Definition: Sampling is the process of measuring a continuous-time signal at regular or specified time intervals to produce a discrete-time sequence.

Sampling is an important step in converting an analog signal into a digital representation.

Sampling Frequency

Sampling frequency is the number of samples taken per second.

fs = 1 / Ts

Where:

  • fs = Sampling frequency in Hz.
  • Ts = Sampling interval in seconds.

Nyquist Sampling Theorem

To ideally reconstruct a band-limited signal with maximum frequency fmax, its sampling frequency must be greater than twice that frequency.

fs > 2fmax

The minimum theoretical rate of twice the maximum signal frequency is called the Nyquist rate.

Example:

If the highest frequency in a signal is 5 kHz, the theoretical Nyquist rate is:

fs = 2 × 5 kHz = 10 kHz

Practical systems generally use a higher sampling rate and appropriate anti-aliasing filtering.

6. Analog-to-Digital Conversion

An Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) converts an analog input voltage into a digital numerical value.

Main Steps

  1. Sampling: Measures the analog signal at selected time instants.
  2. Quantization: Maps each sampled amplitude to one of a finite number of permitted levels.
  3. Encoding: Represents the quantized value using a binary code.
Analog Input → Sampling → Quantization → Encoding → Digital Output

Applications

  • Microcontroller-based sensor systems.
  • Digital audio recording.
  • Industrial data acquisition.
  • Temperature monitoring systems.

7. Practical Embedded Systems Example

Example: Temperature Measurement Using a Microcontroller

Consider an analog temperature sensor connected to the ADC input of a microcontroller.

  1. The temperature sensor generates an analog voltage.
  2. The ADC samples the input voltage.
  3. The sampled voltage is quantized.
  4. The ADC produces a digital value.
  5. The microcontroller calculates and displays temperature.
Temperature → Analog Sensor → ADC → Microcontroller → Display

Lesson Summary

  • Continuous-time signals are defined at every instant of time.
  • Discrete-time signals are defined at specific time instants.
  • Continuous-time signals are represented as x(t).
  • Discrete-time signals are represented as x[n].
  • Sampling converts a continuous-time signal into a discrete-time sequence.
  • Quantization maps amplitudes to finite levels.
  • ADCs convert analog signals into digital values.
