Lesson 6: Ideal and Practical Circuit Elements

Understand ideal circuit models, practical component characteristics and the differences encountered in real electronic circuits.

1. Introduction to Circuit Elements

A circuit element is a basic electrical component or source used to construct an electronic circuit.

Common circuit elements include:

  • Resistors
  • Capacitors
  • Inductors
  • Voltage sources
  • Current sources
  • Diodes and transistors

Circuit elements are often represented using ideal mathematical models for analysis. Practical components have additional characteristics and limitations.

2. Ideal and Practical Circuit Elements

Ideal Circuit Element

An ideal circuit element is a theoretical model that follows specified electrical characteristics perfectly.

Examples:

  • An ideal voltage source maintains constant voltage regardless of load current.
  • An ideal current source maintains constant current regardless of load voltage.
  • An ideal resistor has an exact resistance value.
  • An ideal capacitor has no leakage current or equivalent series resistance.

Practical Circuit Element

A practical circuit element is a real component with physical limitations such as internal resistance, tolerance, power loss, leakage and temperature dependence.

Ideal Model = Simplified Mathematical Representation

Practical Model = Ideal Model + Real-World Effects

3. Ideal and Practical Voltage Sources

Ideal Voltage Source

An ideal voltage source maintains a constant terminal voltage regardless of the current drawn by the load.

  • Internal resistance is zero.
  • Terminal voltage remains constant.
  • It is a theoretical model.
Vterminal = Vsource

Practical Voltage Source

A practical voltage source is modeled as an ideal voltage source connected in series with internal resistance.

  • Internal resistance is small but non-zero.
  • Terminal voltage decreases as load current increases.
  • Batteries and power supplies behave approximately this way.
Vterminal = E - I r

Where:

  • E = Ideal source voltage
  • I = Load current
  • r = Internal resistance

Example

A practical 12 V source has an internal resistance of 1 Ω and is connected to a 5 Ω load.

Total resistance:

R = 5 + 1 = 6 Ω

Load current:

I = 12 / 6 = 2 A

Voltage across the load:

VL = 2 × 5 = 10 V

Therefore, the load receives 10 V instead of the source's ideal 12 V.

4. Ideal and Practical Current Sources

Ideal Current Source

An ideal current source supplies constant current regardless of the voltage across its terminals.

  • Internal resistance is infinite.
  • Output current remains constant.
  • It is a theoretical model.

Practical Current Source

A practical current source is commonly modeled as an ideal current source connected in parallel with a large internal resistance.

  • Internal resistance is high but finite.
  • Output current changes slightly with load voltage.
  • Real current-source circuits approximate this behavior.
Iload = Isource - V / Rinternal

The current division between the load and internal resistance depends on their resistance values.

5. Ideal and Practical Resistors

Ideal Resistor

  • Has an exact resistance value.
  • Follows Ohm's law under all operating conditions.
  • Has no parasitic inductance or capacitance.
  • Does not change resistance with temperature.
V = IR

Practical Resistor

A practical resistor has several real-world characteristics:

  • Tolerance: Actual resistance may differ from its rated value.
  • Temperature coefficient: Resistance changes with temperature.
  • Power rating: It can safely dissipate only a specified amount of power.
  • Parasitics: Small inductive and capacitive effects may appear.

Example

A 1 kΩ resistor with ±5% tolerance can have an actual resistance between:

Minimum = 1000 - 50 = 950 Ω

Maximum = 1000 + 50 = 1050 Ω

6. Ideal and Practical Capacitors

Ideal Capacitor

  • Stores electrical energy in an electric field.
  • Has no leakage current.
  • Has zero equivalent series resistance (ESR).
  • Has no dielectric losses.
Q = CV

XC = 1 / (2πfC)

Practical Capacitor

Practical capacitors have non-ideal properties:

  • ESR: Equivalent series resistance causes power loss and heating.
  • Leakage: A small current flows through the dielectric.
  • Tolerance: Actual capacitance differs from the rated value.
  • Voltage rating: Maximum recommended operating voltage.
  • Dielectric losses: Energy is dissipated inside the dielectric material.

Capacitors are commonly used for filtering, coupling, decoupling and timing applications.

7. Ideal and Practical Inductors

Ideal Inductor

  • Stores energy in a magnetic field.
  • Has zero winding resistance.
  • Has no core losses.
  • Has no parasitic capacitance.
V = L (di/dt)

XL = 2πfL

Practical Inductor

Practical inductors have:

  • Winding resistance: Causes copper losses.
  • Core losses: Include hysteresis and eddy-current losses.
  • Saturation: Inductance may decrease when the magnetic core saturates.
  • Parasitic capacitance: Exists between winding turns.

Inductors are used in filters, power converters, transformers and electromagnetic circuits.

8. Linear and Nonlinear Circuit Elements

Linear Elements

A linear element has a voltage-current relationship that follows linear behavior under specified operating conditions.

Examples:

  • Ideal resistor
  • Ideal capacitor
  • Ideal inductor

Linear circuits obey the principles of superposition and proportionality when their elements operate in their linear regions.

Nonlinear Elements

A nonlinear element does not have a linear voltage-current relationship.

Examples:

  • PN junction diode
  • BJT transistor
  • MOSFET
  • LED

For example, a diode conducts significantly in forward bias after its conduction region is reached, and its current does not increase linearly with voltage.

9. Active and Passive Circuit Elements

Passive Elements

Passive elements store or dissipate energy but do not provide power gain.

  • Resistors
  • Capacitors
  • Inductors

Active Elements

Active devices can control current or voltage and can provide power gain when used with an appropriate external supply.

  • Transistors
  • Operational amplifiers
  • Integrated circuits

Independent and dependent voltage or current sources are also classified as active circuit elements in circuit theory.

10. Real-World Effects in Electronic Circuits

Practical components do not behave exactly like ideal mathematical models. Important real-world effects include:

  • Resistance: Causes voltage drops and power loss.
  • Capacitance: Affects charging, filtering and signal behavior.
  • Inductance: Can produce unwanted voltage spikes during switching.
  • Temperature: Changes component characteristics.
  • Tolerance: Causes variations in circuit performance.
  • Power dissipation: Produces heat in components.

Practical Embedded System Example

Consider a microcontroller power supply circuit containing a voltage regulator, resistors and capacitors.

  • The regulator has a finite output impedance and dropout voltage.
  • Resistors have tolerance and temperature variation.
  • Capacitors have ESR and leakage current.
  • Supply noise can affect the microcontroller's operation.

Understanding practical component behavior helps engineers design stable and reliable embedded systems.

12. Lesson Summary

  • Ideal circuit elements are simplified theoretical models.
  • Practical components have internal losses and physical limitations.
  • An ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance.
  • An ideal current source has infinite internal resistance.
  • Practical resistors have tolerance and power limitations.
  • Practical capacitors have ESR and leakage current.
  • Practical inductors have winding and core losses.
  • Linear and nonlinear elements have different voltage-current relationships.
  • Real-world component behavior is important in reliable circuit design.
