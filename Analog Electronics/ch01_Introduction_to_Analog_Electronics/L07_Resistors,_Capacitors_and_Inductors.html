2. Overview of Passive Components

Passive components are electronic components that do not provide power gain. They are commonly used to limit current, store energy, filter signals, couple or decouple circuits and control transient behaviour.

Figure 1: Basic circuit symbols of passive components.

ComponentMain functionEnergy behaviour
ResistorLimits current and produces voltage dropDissipates energy as heat
CapacitorStores electric-field energy and opposes changes in voltageStores and releases electric energy
InductorStores magnetic-field energy and opposes changes in currentStores and releases magnetic energy

3. Resistors

A resistor is a passive two-terminal component that opposes the flow of electric current. It is widely used for current limiting, voltage division, biasing, pull-up and pull-down networks, and signal conditioning.

3.1 Resistance and Ohm's Law

R = V/I

Where:

  • R = resistance in ohms (Ω)
  • V = voltage across the resistor in volts (V)
  • I = current through the resistor in amperes (A)

3.2 Types of Resistors

3.3 Resistor Specifications

  • Resistance value: Nominal resistance, such as 1 kΩ or 10 kΩ.
  • Tolerance: Permitted deviation from nominal resistance.
  • Power rating: Maximum permitted power dissipation under specified conditions.
  • Temperature coefficient: Change in resistance with temperature.
  • Maximum working voltage: Maximum voltage permitted across the resistor under its specified conditions.

3.4 Resistor Colour Code

3.5 Four-Band Resistor Example

Consider a resistor with colour bands:

Brown – Black – Red – Gold

  • First digit = 1
  • Second digit = 0
  • Multiplier = 100
  • Tolerance = ±5%
Resistance = 10 × 100 = 1000 Ω = 1 kΩ ±5%

3.6 Resistor Power Calculation

P = VI = I²R = V²/R

Example: A 2.2 kΩ resistor has 10 V across it.

P = 10²/2200 = 0.0455 W = 45.5 mW

A suitable standard power rating should be selected with margin, accounting for temperature and derating.

3.7 Practical Applications

  • LED current limiting.
  • Transistor base biasing.
  • Voltage divider circuits.
  • Pull-up and pull-down resistors in digital interfaces.
  • Current sensing and feedback networks.
  • RC filters and timing circuits.

4. Capacitors

A capacitor is a passive component that stores electrical energy in an electric field. It consists of two conductive plates separated by an insulating material called a dielectric.

Figure 2: Simplified capacitor construction.

4.1 Capacitance

Capacitance is the ability of a capacitor to store electric charge for a given voltage.

C = Q/V

Where C is capacitance in farads (F), Q is charge in coulombs (C), and V is voltage in volts (V).

For an ideal parallel-plate capacitor:

C = εA/d
  • ε = permittivity of the dielectric material.
  • A = overlapping plate area.
  • d = distance between the plates.

4.2 Types of Capacitors

TypeCharacteristicsApplications
CeramicNon-polarized; available in many valuesDecoupling and high-frequency circuits
Aluminium electrolyticPolarized; relatively high capacitancePower supply filtering
TantalumPolarized; compact capacitanceCompact electronic equipment

SupercapacitorVery high capacitance; low voltage per cellShort-term energy storage
Variable capacitorCapacitance can be adjustedRF tuning circuits

4.3 Capacitor Specifications

  • Capacitance value and tolerance.
  • Rated working voltage.
  • Polarity, where applicable.
  • Equivalent series resistance (ESR).
  • Leakage current.
  • Temperature range and lifetime.
  • Frequency response and ripple-current rating.
Electrolytic and tantalum capacitors are polarized. Incorrect polarity or excessive applied voltage can cause overheating, leakage or failure. Always follow the component datasheet.

4.4 Capacitor Energy Storage

E = ½CV²

Where E is stored energy in joules.

Example: A 100 µF capacitor is charged to 12 V.

E = ½ × 100 × 10−6 × 12² = 0.0072 J

Stored energy = 7.2 mJ.

4.5 Capacitors in Series

For capacitors connected in series, the reciprocal values add:

1/Ceq = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + ...

For two capacitors:

Ceq = (C1 × C2)/(C1 + C2)

4.6 Capacitors in Parallel

Ceq = C1 + C2 + ...

4.7 Solved Example

Two capacitors of 10 µF and 20 µF are connected in parallel.

Ceq = 10 + 20 = 30 µF

When connected in series:

Ceq = (10 × 20)/(10 + 20) = 6.67 µF
Remember: Capacitors combine opposite to resistors. Capacitors add in parallel and use the reciprocal formula in series.

4.8 Capacitor Applications

  • Power supply smoothing and filtering.
  • Supply decoupling and bypassing.
  • AC coupling between amplifier stages.
  • RC timing and delay circuits.
  • Oscillator and waveform-generation circuits.
  • Frequency-selective filters.
  • Energy storage and transient suppression.

5. Inductors

An inductor is a passive component that stores energy in a magnetic field when current flows through its winding. It generally consists of a coil of wire, sometimes wound around an air, ferrite or iron core.

Figure 3: Inductor coil and its magnetic field.

5.1 Inductance

Inductance is the property of a coil by which it opposes changes in current through it by inducing an electromotive force.

V = L × (di/dt)

For the passive sign convention, this equation describes the voltage drop across an ideal inductor. The induced emf opposing the current change follows Lenz's law.

Unit: Henry (H).

5.2 Types of Inductors

TypeCharacteristicsApplications
Air-coreNo magnetic coreRF and high-frequency circuits
Iron-coreHigh inductance for a given winding in suitable designsLow-frequency and power circuits
Ferrite-coreSuitable for many high-frequency applicationsSMPS and RF circuits
Power inductorDesigned for current handling and energy storageDC-DC converters
Variable inductorInductance can be adjustedTuning circuits

5.3 Inductor Specifications

  • Inductance value.
  • DC resistance (DCR).
  • Rated current and saturation current.
  • Core material and core losses.
  • Self-resonant frequency.
  • Temperature rise and operating temperature.
  • Inductance tolerance.

5.4 Energy Stored in an Inductor

E = ½LI²

Example: A 10 mH inductor carries 2 A.

E = ½ × 0.01 × 2² = 0.02 J

Stored energy = 20 mJ.

5.5 Inductors in Series

For ideal, uncoupled inductors connected in series:

Leq = L1 + L2 + ...

5.6 Inductors in Parallel

For ideal, uncoupled inductors connected in parallel:

1/Leq = 1/L1 + 1/L2 + ...
These simple combination formulas assume negligible mutual magnetic coupling. If inductors are magnetically coupled, their dot orientation and mutual inductance must be considered.

5.7 Inductor Applications

  • SMPS energy storage and filtering.
  • LC and RLC filters.
  • RF tuning circuits.
  • Chokes for reducing unwanted AC ripple or noise.
  • Electromagnetic interference suppression.
  • DC-DC buck and boost converters.

6. DC and AC Behaviour of Passive Components

6.1 Resistor Behaviour

For an ideal resistor, current and voltage are in phase in both DC and sinusoidal AC circuits.

6.2 Capacitor Behaviour

  • In DC steady state, an ideal capacitor behaves as an open circuit.
  • During charging and discharging, capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously.
  • In sinusoidal AC, current leads voltage by 90° for an ideal capacitor.
  • Capacitive reactance decreases as frequency increases.

6.3 Inductor Behaviour

  • In DC steady state, an ideal inductor behaves as a short circuit.
  • Inductor current cannot change instantaneously.
  • In sinusoidal AC, current lags voltage by 90° for an ideal inductor.
  • Inductive reactance increases as frequency increases.
ComponentIdeal DC steady stateIdeal AC phase relation
ResistorResistance remains RV and I are in phase
CapacitorOpen circuitCurrent leads voltage by 90°
InductorShort circuitCurrent lags voltage by 90°
These are ideal steady-state descriptions. Real capacitors have leakage and ESR; real inductors have winding resistance and core losses.

7. Capacitive and Inductive Reactance

Reactance is the opposition offered by a capacitor or inductor to alternating current. It is measured in ohms.

7.1 Capacitive Reactance

XC = 1/(2πfC)

Where f is frequency in hertz and C is capacitance in farads.

7.2 Inductive Reactance

XL = 2πfL

Where L is inductance in henries.

7.3 Solved Example: Capacitive Reactance

Calculate the capacitive reactance of a 10 µF capacitor at 50 Hz.

Xc = 1/(2 × π × 50 × 10 × 10−6) ≈ 318.3 Ω

7.4 Solved Example: Inductive Reactance

Calculate the inductive reactance of a 100 mH inductor at 50 Hz.

XL = 2 × π × 50 × 0.1 ≈ 31.42 Ω

7.5 Frequency Dependence

Frequency increasesEffect
Capacitive reactanceDecreases
Inductive reactanceIncreases

8. RC Circuits and Time Constant

An RC circuit contains a resistor and capacitor. It is used for timing, filtering, coupling, signal shaping and transient control.

Figure 4: Series RC charging circuit.

8.1 RC Time Constant

τ = R × C

Where τ is the time constant in seconds, R is resistance in ohms and C is capacitance in farads.

8.2 Capacitor Charging Equation

VC(t) = Vs(1 − e−t/RC)

The charging current is:

i(t) = (Vs/R)e−t/RC

8.3 Capacitor Discharging Equation

VC(t) = V0e−t/RC

8.4 Time Constant Interpretation

Elapsed timeCharging voltageDischarging voltage remaining
1τ63.2% of final value36.8% of initial value
2τ86.5%13.5%
3τ95.0%5.0%
4τ98.2%1.8%
5τ99.3%0.7%

Figure 5: Conceptual RC charging and discharging curves. Curves are illustrative, not measurement plots.

8.5 Solved Example

A 10 kΩ resistor is connected in series with a 100 µF capacitor. Calculate the time constant.

τ = 10,000 × 100 × 10−6 = 1 second

Approximate charging time to reach 99% of the final voltage is about 5τ = 5 seconds.

9. RL Circuits and Time Constant

An RL circuit contains a resistor and inductor. It is used in filtering, switching circuits, transient suppression and power electronics.

Figure 6: Series RL circuit.

9.1 RL Time Constant

τ = L/R

9.2 Inductor Current During Switching ON

i(t) = (Vs/R)(1 − e−tR/L)

9.3 Inductor Current During Switching OFF

i(t) = I0e−tR/L

9.4 Solved Example

A 100 mH inductor is connected in series with a 50 Ω resistor. Find the RL time constant.

τ = L/R = 0.1/50 = 0.002 s = 2 ms

After approximately 5τ = 10 ms, the current reaches about 99.3% of its final value during an ideal step response.

9.5 Practical Inductor Switching

When current through an inductor is interrupted, the inductor generates a voltage that opposes the sudden change in current. This can create a high-voltage transient.

  • Relay coils and solenoids can generate back EMF during switching.
  • A suitable flyback diode is commonly connected across a DC relay coil.
  • Other applications may use TVS diodes, snubbers or clamps depending on switching requirements.
A flyback diode must be connected with correct polarity: reverse-biased during normal coil operation. It is not suitable for every high-speed or AC switching application.

10. RC and RL Frequency Applications

10.1 RC Low-Pass Filter

An RC low-pass filter passes lower-frequency signals and attenuates higher-frequency signals.

Figure 7: RC low-pass filter. Output is taken across the capacitor.

10.2 RC High-Pass Filter

An RC high-pass filter passes higher-frequency signals and attenuates lower-frequency signals.

Figure 8: RC high-pass filter. Output is taken across the resistor.

10.3 RC Cutoff Frequency

fc = 1/(2πRC)

At the cutoff frequency of a first-order RC filter, the output magnitude is approximately 0.707 of the passband value, corresponding to −3 dB.

10.4 Solved Example

Calculate the cutoff frequency of an RC filter with R = 10 kΩ and C = 0.1 µF.

fc = 1/(2 × π × 10,000 × 0.1 × 10−6) ≈ 159.15 Hz

10.5 LC Filters

Inductors and capacitors are often combined to form LC filters. These networks are commonly used in power supplies and RF circuits.

f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))

This is the ideal resonance frequency of an LC network.

11. Practical Component Testing

11.1 Resistor Testing Using DMM

  1. Switch off the circuit supply.
  2. Discharge any stored energy in the circuit.
  3. Disconnect at least one resistor terminal if parallel paths may affect the reading.
  4. Select resistance mode.
  5. Place probes across the resistor.
  6. Compare the reading with its nominal value and tolerance.

11.2 Capacitor Testing

  1. Disconnect the supply.
  2. Discharge the capacitor safely using a suitable resistor and verify the voltage is near zero.
  3. Isolate at least one terminal when necessary.
  4. Use capacitance mode or an LCR meter if available.
  5. Compare the measured capacitance with its specified tolerance.
  6. For power-supply capacitors, ESR testing may be required.

11.3 Inductor Testing

  1. Disconnect the circuit supply.
  2. Isolate the inductor where necessary.
  3. Measure winding resistance using DMM resistance mode.
  4. Use an LCR meter to measure inductance.
  5. Compare inductance and DCR with the component specification.
A normal DMM resistance reading alone cannot confirm that a capacitor or inductor is fully healthy. Capacitor ESR, leakage and inductor inductance or saturation characteristics may require dedicated test equipment.

13. Placement-Oriented Practical Scenarios

Scenario 1: LED Does Not Glow

Possible causes:

  • LED polarity is reversed.
  • Series resistor has an incorrect value or is open.
  • Supply voltage is absent.
  • LED is damaged.
  • Loose breadboard or solder connection.

Approach: Check supply voltage, LED polarity, resistor value and continuity. Measure the voltage across the LED while operating under safe current-limited conditions.

Scenario 2: Power Supply Output Has Excessive Ripple

Possible causes:

  • Filtering capacitor has reduced capacitance.
  • Capacitor ESR has increased.
  • Capacitor polarity is incorrect.
  • Load current exceeds the supply design limit.

Approach: Inspect the capacitor, measure ripple using an oscilloscope, and test capacitance and ESR with suitable equipment.

Scenario 3: Relay Driver Damages the Switching Transistor

Possible cause: Inductive voltage transient generated when the relay coil is switched off.

Approach: Verify the flyback protection circuit, diode polarity and switching requirements. Select the clamp method according to the required release time and voltage limits.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a passive component?
A component that does not provide power gain. Examples include resistors, capacitors and inductors.
Q2. What is the main function of a resistor?
It limits current, produces voltage drops and helps establish operating points in electronic circuits.
Q3. What is resistor tolerance?
It is the permitted percentage variation of actual resistance from its nominal value.
Q4. What is the difference between a resistor and a potentiometer?
A fixed resistor has a fixed resistance value, whereas a potentiometer has an adjustable wiper and is commonly used as a variable voltage divider.
Q5. What is capacitance?
Capacitance is the ratio of stored charge to voltage across a capacitor, expressed as C = Q/V.
Q6. Why is a capacitor used in a power supply?
It smooths rectified output by storing and releasing charge, reducing voltage ripple.
Q7. Why does a capacitor block DC in steady state?
Once fully charged under ideal DC steady-state conditions, no continuous current flows through its dielectric.
Q8. What is ESR?
Equivalent series resistance is the effective resistive component of a capacitor's non-ideal behaviour. High ESR can cause heating and poor filtering.
Q9. Why is an electrolytic capacitor polarized?
Its internal construction requires correct polarity for normal operation. Reverse voltage can damage it.
Q10. What is an inductor?
An inductor is a component that stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes changes in current.
Q11. Why does an inductor oppose a sudden change in current?
A changing current produces a changing magnetic flux and an induced voltage that opposes the change according to Lenz's law.
Q12. What happens to an ideal capacitor at DC steady state?
It behaves as an open circuit.
Q13. What happens to an ideal inductor at DC steady state?
It behaves as a short circuit after transient conditions have settled.
Q14. What is the RC time constant?
It is the product of resistance and capacitance, τ = RC. It determines the rate of capacitor charging and discharging.
Q15. What is the RL time constant?
It is the ratio of inductance to resistance, τ = L/R, for a series RL circuit.
Q16. What is capacitive reactance?
It is the opposition offered by a capacitor to AC, calculated as Xc = 1/(2πfC).
Q17. What is inductive reactance?
It is the opposition offered by an inductor to AC, calculated as XL = 2πfL.
Q18. What is the difference between capacitance and inductance?
Capacitance relates to electric-field energy storage and opposes rapid voltage changes. Inductance relates to magnetic-field energy storage and opposes rapid current changes.
Q19. Why is a flyback diode connected across a relay coil?
It provides a path for coil current when the switching transistor turns off, limiting the inductive voltage transient.
Q20. What is the purpose of an RC low-pass filter?
It passes low-frequency signals and attenuates higher-frequency components.
Q21. What is the purpose of an RC high-pass filter?
It passes high-frequency signals and attenuates lower-frequency components.
Q22. What is the −3 dB cutoff point?
For a first-order filter, it is the frequency at which output magnitude falls to approximately 70.7% of its passband value.
Q23. What is the difference between DMM and LCR meter testing?
A DMM commonly measures resistance, voltage and current, and may offer basic capacitance measurement. An LCR meter measures inductance, capacitance and resistance using specified test conditions and frequencies.
Q24. Why should a capacitor be discharged before testing?
It may retain electrical energy after power is removed. Discharging it safely reduces shock, equipment-damage and measurement risks.

15. Practice Problems

Problem 1: A 4.7 kΩ resistor has 9.4 V across it. Calculate the current.

Answer: I = 9.4/4700 = 2 mA.

Problem 2: Calculate the power dissipated by a 1 kΩ resistor carrying 20 mA.

Answer: P = I²R = (0.02)² × 1000 = 0.4 W.

Problem 3: A 100 µF capacitor is connected in parallel with a 220 µF capacitor. Find equivalent capacitance.

Answer: Ceq = 320 µF.

Problem 4: Two capacitors of 10 µF and 30 µF are connected in series. Find equivalent capacitance.

Answer: Ceq = (10 × 30)/(10 + 30) = 7.5 µF.

Problem 5: Calculate the energy stored in a 470 µF capacitor charged to 10 V.

Answer: E = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 = 0.0235 J.

Problem 6: Find the time constant of a 4.7 kΩ resistor and 100 µF capacitor.

Answer: τ = 4700 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.47 s.

Problem 7: Find the time constant of a 200 mH inductor in series with a 100 Ω resistor.

Answer: τ = 0.2/100 = 0.002 s = 2 ms.

Problem 8: Calculate capacitive reactance for 1 µF at 1 kHz.

Answer: Xc = 1/(2π × 1000 × 1 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 159.15 Ω.

Problem 9: Calculate inductive reactance for 50 mH at 1 kHz.

Answer: XL = 2π × 1000 × 0.05 ≈ 314.16 Ω.

Problem 10: Calculate the cutoff frequency of a circuit with R = 4.7 kΩ and C = 10 nF.

Answer: fc = 1/(2π × 4700 × 10 × 10⁻⁹) ≈ 3386 Hz.

Problem 11: Calculate the energy stored in a 50 mH inductor carrying 3 A.

Answer: E = ½ × 0.05 × 3² = 0.225 J.

Problem 12: A capacitor is charged to 10 V. What percentage of the final voltage is reached after one time constant?

Answer: Approximately 63.2%, or 6.32 V.

16. Practical Lab Exercises

Experiment 1: Resistor Identification and Measurement

Objective: Identify resistor values using colour codes and verify them with a DMM.

Components: Assorted resistors, DMM and resistor colour-code chart.

  1. Identify the nominal resistance using the colour bands.
  2. Calculate the permitted tolerance range.
  3. Measure the resistance using the DMM.
  4. Check whether the measured value is within tolerance.
  5. Record the results.

Experiment 2: Capacitor Charging and Discharging

Objective: Observe the RC transient response.

Components: 5 V current-limited DC supply, 10 kΩ resistor, 100 µF capacitor and DMM or oscilloscope.

  1. Connect the resistor and capacitor in a series RC circuit.
  2. Apply the supply and observe the capacitor voltage.
  3. Record the voltage at regular time intervals.
  4. Disconnect the supply and discharge the capacitor safely through the resistor.
  5. Observe the discharging response.
  6. Compare the measured time constant with the calculated value of 1 second.

Experiment 3: RC Low-Pass Filter

Objective: Study the frequency response of an RC low-pass filter.

Components: 10 kΩ resistor, 0.1 µF capacitor, function generator and oscilloscope.

  1. Construct the RC low-pass circuit.
  2. Calculate its cutoff frequency.
  3. Apply a sine wave at frequencies below, near and above cutoff.
  4. Measure input and output amplitudes.
  5. Observe attenuation as frequency increases.

Experiment 4: Inductor and Flyback Protection

Objective: Understand the voltage transient produced by switching an inductive load.

Components: Low-voltage DC relay or inductor, suitable switching transistor, flyback diode and current-limited supply.

  1. Build a safe low-voltage inductive switching circuit.
  2. Observe the switching transient using appropriate measurement equipment.
  3. Switch off the inductive load and observe the transient.
  4. Connect the correctly rated flyback diode across the DC coil.
  5. Repeat the observation and compare the results.
Use a current-limited low-voltage supply and correctly rated components. Do not connect experimental circuits directly to mains voltage.
