Lesson 7: Basic Electronic Circuit Terminology

Learn the essential electrical and electronic terms, circuit quantities, network concepts and measurement terminology used in analog circuit analysis.

1. Introduction

Electronic circuits are described using standard electrical terminology. Understanding these terms is essential for analyzing circuits, reading schematics, troubleshooting hardware and preparing for technical interviews.

Important terms include:

  • Node
  • Branch
  • Loop
  • Mesh
  • Open circuit
  • Short circuit
  • Ground
  • Polarity
  • Input and output

2. Node

A node is a point or continuous conducting region in an electrical circuit where two or more circuit terminals are electrically connected.

All points belonging to the same ideal node have the same electrical potential.

Types of Nodes

  • Simple node: A connection between two circuit elements.
  • Essential node: A node where three or more branches meet.

Example

In a resistor network, the common connection point between a resistor, capacitor and transistor terminal forms a node.

3. Branch

A branch is a path between two nodes containing a circuit element or a series combination of elements without intermediate junctions.

Examples:

  • A resistor connected between two nodes.
  • A voltage source connected between two nodes.
  • A series combination of components forming one branch.

Each branch carries a current, with its direction defined using a chosen reference direction.

4. Loop and Mesh

Loop

A loop is any closed path in an electrical circuit that starts and ends at the same node without passing through any node more than once.

Mesh

A mesh is a loop that does not contain another loop inside it. It is the smallest independent closed path in a planar circuit.

Every mesh is a loop, but every loop is not necessarily a mesh.

These concepts are important when applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law and mesh-current analysis.

5. Open Circuit

An open circuit is a condition in which the conducting path is interrupted, preventing current from flowing through that path.

  • Ideally, open-circuit resistance is infinite.
  • Current through the open path is zero.
  • A voltage may still exist across the open terminals.
I = 0 A

Common Causes

  • Broken wire
  • Disconnected terminal
  • Damaged PCB track
  • Open fuse
  • Faulty solder joint

6. Short Circuit

A short circuit occurs when two points at different electrical potentials are connected through a path having very low resistance.

  • Ideally, short-circuit resistance is zero.
  • Current can become very high depending on the source and circuit impedance.
  • It may damage components, tracks and power supplies.
Rshort ≈ 0 Ω

Common Causes

  • Damaged insulation
  • Solder bridges on a PCB
  • Incorrect wiring
  • Failed electronic components

Fuses, current limiting circuits and protection devices help protect circuits from short-circuit faults.

7. Ground and Reference Voltage

Ground is a reference point used to define voltage measurements in an electrical circuit.

A circuit ground is commonly assigned a potential of 0 V for analysis. It does not necessarily mean that the point is connected to the physical Earth.

Types of Ground

  • Signal ground: Reference point for analog or digital signals.
  • Power ground: Return path for power supply currents.
  • Chassis ground: Connection to the equipment's conductive enclosure.
  • Earth ground: Physical connection to the Earth through a grounding system.

Embedded System Example

A microcontroller operating at 3.3 V uses its ground terminal as the reference for GPIO, ADC and communication signals.

When connecting an external sensor, a common reference ground is generally required for reliable voltage measurements and signal communication.

8. Polarity

Polarity identifies the positive and negative terminals or voltage reference directions of an electrical component or circuit.

Positive and Negative Polarity

  • Voltage is measured between two points.
  • The selected positive terminal determines the voltage reference.
  • Reversing the reference direction changes the voltage sign.

Polarity-Sensitive Components

  • Electrolytic capacitors
  • Diodes
  • LEDs
  • Transistors
  • Many integrated circuits and power devices

Incorrect polarity can cause malfunction or permanent component damage.

9. Input and Output

Input

Input is the signal or electrical quantity supplied to a circuit or electronic system.

Output

Output is the resulting signal or electrical quantity produced by the circuit.

Example: Transistor Amplifier

  • Input: Small AC signal applied to the base of a BJT amplifier.
  • Output: Amplified AC signal obtained at the collector.

The amplifier uses energy from its DC power supply to produce an output signal with greater amplitude.

10. Important Circuit Parameters

Resistance (R)

Resistance is the opposition offered by a material or component to electric current.

R = V / I

Capacitance (C)

Capacitance is the ability of a component to store electric charge per unit voltage.

C = Q / V

Inductance (L)

Inductance is the property of a circuit element that opposes changes in current by inducing voltage.

V = L (di/dt)

Impedance (Z)

Impedance is the total opposition offered by a circuit to alternating current, including resistance and reactance.

Z = R + jX

Conductance (G)

Conductance represents the ability of a component to conduct electric current.

G = 1 / R

11. Series and Parallel Connections

Series Connection

Components are connected in series when they share a single current path.

  • The same current flows through all series elements.
  • Total resistance is the sum of individual resistances.
  • Voltage is divided across the components.
RTotal = R1 + R2 + R3 + ...

Parallel Connection

Components are connected in parallel when they share the same two electrical nodes.

  • The voltage across each parallel branch is the same.
  • Total current is the sum of branch currents.
1 / RTotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + 1/R3 + ...

Example

Two resistors of 10 Ω and 20 Ω are connected in parallel.

RTotal = (10 × 20) / (10 + 20)

RTotal = 200 / 30

RTotal = 6.67 Ω

13. Lesson Summary

  • A node is an electrically common connection point.
  • A branch connects two nodes through circuit elements.
  • A loop is a closed path in a circuit.
  • A mesh is a loop containing no other loops inside it.
  • An open circuit interrupts the current path.
  • A short circuit provides a very low-resistance path.
  • Ground provides a voltage reference.
  • Polarity identifies voltage or terminal orientation.
  • Input and output describe signal flow through a circuit.
  • Resistance, capacitance, inductance and impedance are important circuit parameters.
