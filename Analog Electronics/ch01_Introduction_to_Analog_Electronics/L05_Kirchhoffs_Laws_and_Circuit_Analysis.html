1. Lecture Overview

Electrical circuits often contain multiple branches, voltage sources, current sources and interconnected components. Ohm's law is essential for circuit calculations, but complex networks require additional laws to determine unknown branch currents and node voltages.

Kirchhoff's laws provide the foundation for analysing these networks. They are widely used in electronics design, PCB troubleshooting, power supplies, amplifier circuits and electrical testing.

4. Basic Circuit Terminology

4.1 Node

A node is a connection point where two or more circuit elements meet. All points connected by ideal wire without an intervening component belong to the same electrical node and have the same potential.

4.2 Branch

A branch is a path between two nodes containing a circuit element or a series combination of elements carrying the same branch current.

4.3 Loop

A loop is any closed path in a circuit that starts and ends at the same node without repeating a node along the path, apart from the starting and ending node.

4.4 Mesh

A mesh is a loop that does not contain another loop inside it. Mesh analysis is applicable to planar circuits.

4.5 Reference Node or Ground

A reference node is selected as the zero-volt reference for measuring other node voltages. It is commonly represented by the ground symbol.

A two-mesh planar circuit. The marked connection points are nodes; vertical paths between the upper and lower nodes are branches. Each inner closed region is a mesh.
Important distinction: Every mesh is a loop, but every loop is not necessarily a mesh.

5. Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

5.1 Definition

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the algebraic sum of currents at any electrical node is zero.

In simple terms:

Total current entering a node = Total current leaving a node

Mathematically:

Σ I = 0

5.2 Physical Principle

KCL is based on the conservation of electric charge. Under normal lumped-circuit assumptions, charge does not continuously accumulate at an ideal circuit node. Therefore, the total current entering the node must equal the total current leaving it.

5.3 KCL Circuit Example

I₁ and I₂ enter node A; I₃ and I₄ leave node A. All four current directions are indicated by arrowheads.

5.4 Solved Example – Finding an Unknown Current

Given: Two currents of 5 mA and 3 mA enter a node. One current of 4 mA leaves the node. Find the other outgoing current.

Step 1: Apply KCL.
Entering currents = Leaving currents
Step 2: Substitute known values.
5 + 3 = 4 + I₄
Step 3: Solve.
8 = 4 + I₄
I₄ = 8 - 4
I₄ = 4 mA
Answer: The unknown outgoing current is 4 mA.

5.5 KCL with an Assumed Direction

In circuit analysis, the actual current direction may not be known. Assume a direction and use algebraic signs consistently.

For example, suppose currents entering a node are 2 mA and 1 mA, while an assumed outgoing current is I.

2 + 1 - I = 0
I = 3 mA

If the calculated current is negative, the actual direction is opposite to the assumed direction.

6. Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

6.1 Definition

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and voltage drops around any closed loop is zero.

Σ V = 0

In simple terms:

Total voltage rises = Total voltage drops

6.2 Physical Principle

KVL follows from conservation of energy. When a charge completes a closed path, its net energy change is zero. The energy gained from voltage sources must equal the energy lost in circuit elements, under the lumped-circuit assumptions used for ordinary circuit analysis.

6.3 KVL Sign Convention

Movement across an elementSign in the KVL equation
From negative to positive terminalPositive voltage rise (+V)
From positive to negative terminalNegative voltage drop (-V)
Across a resistor in the direction of assumed currentVoltage drop (-IR)
Across a resistor opposite to assumed currentVoltage rise (+IR)
Closed loop with a 12 V source and two series resistors. The current is clockwise: 2 mA.

6.4 Solved Example – Applying KVL

Given: A 12 V source is connected in series with two resistors: R₁ = 2 kΩ and R₂ = 4 kΩ. Find the loop current and voltage drops.

Step 1: Calculate total resistance.
RT = R₁ + R₂
RT = 2 kΩ + 4 kΩ = 6 kΩ
Step 2: Calculate current using Ohm's law.
I = V / RT
I = 12 / 6000
I = 2 mA
Step 3: Calculate voltage drops.
V₁ = I × R₁ = 2 mA × 2 kΩ = 4 V
V₂ = I × R₂ = 2 mA × 4 kΩ = 8 V
Step 4: Verify using KVL.
+12 - 4 - 8 = 0
Answer: Loop current = 2 mA, voltage across R₁ = 4 V, voltage across R₂ = 8 V.

6.5 KVL with Multiple Sources

When a loop contains multiple voltage sources, their polarities must be considered. Sources aiding the chosen traversal direction contribute voltage rises; sources opposing it contribute voltage drops.

For example, two opposing sources of 12 V and 5 V are connected in a loop with a 7 kΩ resistor.

Net voltage = 12 - 5 = 7 V
I = 7 V / 7 kΩ = 1 mA

The current direction is determined by the larger source, assuming the stated source polarities and an ideal series circuit.

7. Relationship Between KCL, KVL and Ohm's Law

These three principles work together to solve electrical networks.

PrincipleWhat it describesTypical application
Ohm's lawRelationship between voltage, current and resistanceCalculate current through a resistor
KCLCurrent balance at a nodeFind unknown branch currents
KVLVoltage balance around a closed loopFind unknown voltage drops and loop currents
Practical approach: Use KCL to write node equations, KVL to write loop equations, and Ohm's law to relate branch voltage and current.

8. Nodal Voltage Analysis

8.1 What Is Nodal Analysis?

Nodal analysis is a systematic circuit-solving method that uses KCL to determine unknown node voltages relative to a selected reference node.

8.2 General Procedure

  1. Identify all circuit nodes.
  2. Select one node as the reference ground (0 V).
  3. Assign voltage variables to the remaining nodes.
  4. Apply KCL at each unknown-voltage node.
  5. Express each branch current using Ohm's law.
  6. Solve the simultaneous equations.
  7. Calculate required branch currents and verify the result.

8.3 Nodal Analysis Example

Consider a 12 V source connected to node A through a 2 kΩ resistor. Node A is connected to ground through 3 kΩ and 6 kΩ resistors. Find the voltage at node A and the branch currents.

The 12 V source has its positive terminal connected to the 2 kΩ resistor. Node A connects to the 3 kΩ and 6 kΩ resistors, both returning to the common ground.
Step 1: Identify the reference node.

The bottom connection is ground. Its voltage is 0 V. The source fixes the voltage on the left side of the 2 kΩ resistor at +12 V, with the polarity shown.

Step 2: Assign the unknown node voltage.
Vₐ = Unknown node voltage
Step 3: Apply KCL at node A.

Assume all three branch currents are defined as leaving node A.

(Vₐ - 12)/2k + Vₐ/3k + Vₐ/6k = 0
Step 4: Multiply the equation by 6 kΩ.
3(Vₐ - 12) + 2Vₐ + Vₐ = 0
Step 5: Expand and simplify.
3Vₐ - 36 + 2Vₐ + Vₐ = 0
6Vₐ = 36
Vₐ = 6 V
Step 6: Calculate branch currents.
I through 2 kΩ = (12 - 6)/2k = 3 mA
I through 3 kΩ = 6/3k = 2 mA
I through 6 kΩ = 6/6k = 1 mA
Step 7: Verify KCL.
Current entering node A = 3 mA
Current leaving node A = 2 mA + 1 mA = 3 mA
Final result:
  • Node voltage Vₐ = 6 V
  • Current through 2 kΩ = 3 mA towards node A
  • Current through 3 kΩ = 2 mA towards ground
  • Current through 6 kΩ = 1 mA towards ground

8.4 Nodal Analysis with a Current Source

Current sources can be handled directly using KCL. For example, a 2 mA current source injects current into a node connected to ground through 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ resistors.

2 mA = V/2k + V/4k
2 mA = 0.5V mS + 0.25V mS
2 = 0.75V
V = 2.667 V (approximately)

The node voltage is approximately 2.67 V. The current divides between the two resistors.

8.5 Supernode – Introduction

A supernode occurs when an ideal voltage source connects two non-reference nodes. Instead of writing separate KCL equations at the two nodes, treat both nodes and the voltage source between them as one extended region.

  1. Identify the two nodes connected by the voltage source.
  2. Form one KCL equation around the outer boundary of the supernode.
  3. Write an additional voltage constraint from the source polarity.
  4. Solve the equations together.
If V₁ is 5 V above V₂:
V₁ - V₂ = 5 V

This voltage constraint is used together with the supernode KCL equation.

9. Mesh Current Analysis

9.1 What Is Mesh Analysis?

Mesh analysis is a systematic method for finding unknown currents in a planar circuit by applying KVL around its independent meshes.

Each mesh is assigned an assumed current, commonly in the clockwise direction. The resulting equations are solved simultaneously.

9.2 General Procedure

  1. Identify all independent meshes.
  2. Assign a mesh current to each mesh.
  3. Choose a consistent direction, usually clockwise.
  4. Apply KVL around every mesh.
  5. For a shared resistor, express its voltage using the difference between the two mesh currents.
  6. Solve the simultaneous equations.
  7. Calculate individual branch currents and voltage drops.

9.3 Mesh Analysis Example – Two Meshes

Consider a two-mesh circuit with a 10 V source in the left mesh, a 5 V source in the right mesh, a 2 kΩ resistor in each outer branch and a shared 2 kΩ resistor. Both sources aid their respective clockwise mesh currents.

Two adjacent meshes. The shared 2 kΩ resistor is the central vertical branch. Both sources have positive terminals at the top and aid their respective clockwise mesh currents.
Sign convention: The equations below use clockwise mesh currents. In the shared resistor, the two mesh currents flow in opposite directions. Therefore, its branch current is the difference between the mesh currents.
Step 1: Assign mesh currents.
Left mesh current = I₁
Right mesh current = I₂
Step 2: Apply KVL to the left mesh.

The left mesh contains its 2 kΩ resistor and the shared 2 kΩ resistor.

10 - 2kI₁ - 2k(I₁ - I₂) = 0
4kI₁ - 2kI₂ = 10
Step 3: Apply KVL to the right mesh.
5 - 2kI₂ - 2k(I₂ - I₁) = 0
-2kI₁ + 4kI₂ = 5
Step 4: Simplify using kΩ and mA.
4I₁ - 2I₂ = 10
-2I₁ + 4I₂ = 5
Step 5: Solve the equations.

Multiply the second equation by 2:

-4I₁ + 8I₂ = 10

Add it to the first equation:

6I₂ = 20
I₂ = 3.333 mA

Substitute into the first equation:

4I₁ - 2(3.333) = 10
4I₁ = 16.666
I₁ = 4.167 mA
Step 6: Calculate shared branch current.
Ishared = I₁ - I₂
= 4.167 - 3.333
= 0.833 mA (approximately)
Final result:
  • Left mesh current I₁ ≈ 4.167 mA.
  • Right mesh current I₂ ≈ 3.333 mA.
  • Shared resistor current ≈ 0.833 mA in the left mesh direction.

9.4 Supermesh – Introduction

A supermesh is formed when an ideal current source is present in a branch shared by two adjacent meshes. The current source branch is excluded from the outer KVL path, and a larger loop is formed around the two meshes.

  1. Identify the two meshes sharing the current source.
  2. Write one KVL equation around the outer perimeter of the combined meshes.
  3. Write the current-source constraint relating the mesh currents.
  4. Solve the two equations.
For a shared source directed in the I₁ direction:
I₁ - I₂ = Isource

The sign depends on the current-source arrow and the assumed mesh-current directions.

Interview tip: Choose the method that produces fewer unknown variables and simpler equations. Both methods should produce consistent physical results.

11. Circuit Verification Using Power Balance

After solving a circuit, verify whether the total power supplied by the sources equals the total power absorbed by the circuit elements.

Σ P = 0

Using the passive sign convention:

  • Positive power indicates that an element absorbs power.
  • Negative power indicates that an element delivers power.

Example – Power Verification

A 12 V source supplies a 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ series circuit. The current is 2 mA.

Source power delivered = 12 × 0.002 = 24 mW
Power in 2 kΩ = I²R = (0.002)² × 2000 = 8 mW
Power in 4 kΩ = I²R = (0.002)² × 4000 = 16 mW
Total absorbed power = 8 + 16 = 24 mW
Verification: Source power delivered = total resistor power absorbed = 24 mW.

12. Practical Applications in Electronics

12.1 Current Distribution in Parallel Branches

KCL is used to determine how current divides between parallel loads. This is useful when analysing sensor interfaces, LED branches and parallel resistor networks.

12.2 Voltage Drop in Power Supply Circuits

KVL helps calculate voltage drops across series components such as rectifier diodes, current-limiting resistors, protection resistors and wiring resistance.

12.3 Transistor Bias Networks

Nodal analysis is used to determine base, emitter and collector voltages in transistor bias circuits. These values help establish the transistor's operating point.

12.4 Op-Amp Circuits

KCL is frequently applied at an operational amplifier's input summing node. For an ideal op-amp operating with negative feedback, input currents are approximately zero, allowing the resistor currents to be related using KCL.

12.5 Embedded Hardware and Sensor Circuits

In embedded hardware, nodal analysis helps calculate voltage-divider outputs, ADC input voltages and current paths through interface circuits. KCL helps verify current distribution and KVL helps check supply and ground paths.

13. Practical Measurement and Troubleshooting

13.1 Measuring Node Voltage

  1. Set the digital multimeter to DC voltage mode.
  2. Connect the black probe to circuit ground.
  3. Connect the red probe to the node being tested.
  4. Compare the measured voltage with the calculated node voltage.
  5. Investigate differences caused by incorrect component values, loading, faulty connections or source variation.

13.2 Measuring Branch Current

  1. Turn off the power before changing the circuit connection.
  2. Set the multimeter to the correct current range and input terminal.
  3. Open the branch and connect the meter in series.
  4. Power the circuit and measure the branch current.
  5. Compare the reading with the calculated current.
  6. Switch off the circuit before removing the meter.
Meter safety: Never connect a multimeter in current mode directly across a voltage source. This can create a short circuit and damage the meter or circuit. Always check the meter's current input and fuse rating.

13.3 Troubleshooting Table

Observed problemPossible causeRecommended check
Measured node voltage is lower than expectedExcessive load, wrong resistor value or weak supplyMeasure the supply and verify resistor values
Branch current is zeroOpen connection, disconnected component or incorrect meter connectionCheck continuity and series connection
Unexpected high currentShort circuit, wrong resistor value or wiring errorPower off and inspect resistance between nodes
KCL does not balance in measurementsMeasurement error, meter burden voltage or unmeasured branchCheck all branches and measurement setup
KVL does not balance in measurementsWrong polarity, measurement reference error or changing source voltageMeasure around the same closed loop with consistent polarity
Calculated and measured voltage differComponent tolerance or circuit loadingCheck actual component values and connected loads

13.4 Practical Example – Voltage Divider Verification

A 10 V DC supply is connected across two series resistors: R₁ = 1 kΩ and R₂ = 4 kΩ. The output is measured across R₂.

I = 10 V / (1 kΩ + 4 kΩ)
I = 2 mA
VR1 = 2 mA × 1 kΩ = 2 V
Vout = VR2 = 2 mA × 4 kΩ = 8 V
KVL: 10 - 2 - 8 = 0

When tested with a multimeter, the output should be approximately 8 V if the supply is 10 V and the output is not significantly loaded.

14. Common Mistakes in Circuit Analysis

  • Mixing up current entering and leaving a node when applying KCL.
  • Changing sign conventions in the middle of a KVL equation.
  • Forgetting that a shared resistor carries the difference between adjacent mesh currents.
  • Using an incorrect reference node in nodal analysis.
  • Confusing a loop with a mesh.
  • Forgetting to convert kΩ, Ω, mA and A consistently.
  • Assuming a negative calculated current means the calculation is wrong. It actually indicates the opposite direction from the assumed direction.
  • Ignoring the effect of a connected measuring instrument or load.

15. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. State Kirchhoff's Current Law.

Answer: KCL states that the algebraic sum of currents at any node is zero. Total current entering a node equals total current leaving it.

Q2. State Kirchhoff's Voltage Law.

Answer: KVL states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and drops around a closed loop is zero.

Q3. What is the physical basis of KCL?

Answer: Conservation of electric charge.

Q4. What is the physical basis of KVL?

Answer: Conservation of energy.

Q5. What is a node?

Answer: A node is a connection point between circuit elements. All points connected by ideal wire without an intervening component have the same potential.

Q6. Differentiate between a loop and a mesh.

Answer: A loop is any closed path. A mesh is a loop that contains no other loop within it.

Q7. What is nodal analysis?

Answer: Nodal analysis is a method of solving circuits by applying KCL at nodes and determining unknown node voltages relative to a reference node.

Q8. What is mesh analysis?

Answer: Mesh analysis is a method of solving planar circuits by applying KVL around independent meshes to determine mesh currents.

Q9. What happens if a calculated current is negative?

Answer: It means the actual current direction is opposite to the assumed direction.

Q10. What is a reference node?

Answer: A selected node assigned zero volts, relative to which all other node voltages are measured.

Q11. What is a supernode?

Answer: A supernode is formed when an ideal voltage source connects two non-reference nodes. The combined region is analysed using KCL and a voltage constraint.

Q12. What is a supermesh?

Answer: A supermesh is formed when a current source lies in the branch shared by two adjacent meshes. KVL is applied around the outer perimeter, along with a current constraint.

Q13. Can KCL be applied to a node connected to several resistors?

Answer: Yes. Each branch current can be expressed using Ohm's law in terms of the node voltage and the voltage at the other end of the resistor.

Q14. Which method is more suitable for a circuit with many current sources?

Answer: Nodal analysis is often more convenient because current sources can be included directly in KCL equations.

Q15. Which method is more suitable for a planar circuit with fewer meshes?

Answer: Mesh analysis is often more convenient because it requires fewer unknown mesh currents.

Q16. How do you verify a solved circuit?

Answer: Check that KCL balances at the nodes, KVL balances around the loops and total supplied power equals total absorbed power.

Q17. Does KVL apply to every circuit?

Answer: KVL is directly used in lumped-circuit analysis. In circuits with significant time-varying magnetic flux linking a loop, induced electromotive force must also be considered.

Q18. Why is nodal analysis useful in electronics?

Answer: It helps determine operating voltages at transistor terminals, op-amp nodes, voltage dividers, sensor interfaces and power supply circuits.

16. Numerical Practice Problems

Problem 1 – KCL

At a node, currents of 7 mA and 2 mA enter. A current of 5 mA leaves. Calculate the other outgoing current.

Answer: 7 + 2 = 5 + I, so I = 4 mA.

Problem 2 – KVL

A 15 V source is connected to two series resistors of 3 kΩ and 2 kΩ. Calculate the circuit current and voltage across each resistor.

Answer: Total resistance = 5 kΩ; current = 3 mA; voltage drops = 9 V and 6 V.

Problem 3 – Nodal Analysis

A node is connected to a 10 V source through 2 kΩ and to ground through 3 kΩ. Calculate the node voltage.

Answer: (V - 10)/2k + V/3k = 0. Multiplying by 6 kΩ gives 3V - 30 + 2V = 0. Therefore V = 6 V.

Problem 4 – Current Division

A total current of 6 mA enters two parallel resistors of 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ. Calculate the current through each resistor.

Answer: Equivalent resistance = 1.333 kΩ; node voltage = 8 V. Current through 2 kΩ = 4 mA; current through 4 kΩ = 2 mA.

Problem 5 – KVL with Opposing Sources

Two opposing sources of 18 V and 6 V are connected in series with a 4 kΩ resistor. Calculate the current.

Answer: Net voltage = 12 V; current = 12/4k = 3 mA, in the direction of the larger source.

Problem 6 – Power Verification

A 20 V supply drives a 5 kΩ resistor. Calculate the current and power absorbed by the resistor.

Answer: I = 4 mA; P = VI = 80 mW.

17. Practical Interview and Lab Tasks

  1. Build a two-resistor series circuit and verify KVL using a digital multimeter.
  2. Build a parallel resistor network and verify KCL by measuring each branch current.
  3. Construct a voltage divider and compare calculated and measured node voltage.
  4. Solve a two-node circuit using nodal analysis and verify it using simulation.
  5. Solve a two-mesh circuit using mesh analysis and compare branch currents.
  6. Introduce a wrong resistor value into a test circuit and identify the fault using voltage measurements.
