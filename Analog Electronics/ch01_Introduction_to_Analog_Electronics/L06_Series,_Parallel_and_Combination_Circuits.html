1. Lecture Overview

Resistors are connected in series, parallel or a combination of both to control current, divide voltage, set operating points and establish required resistance values in electronic circuits.

Understanding resistor networks is essential for analysing voltage dividers, transistor bias circuits, sensor interfaces, power supply circuits and current-limiting networks. In practical electronics work, engineers frequently simplify complex resistor networks into equivalent resistance values before calculating circuit currents and voltages.

4. Series Resistor Circuits

4.1 What Is a Series Circuit?

A series circuit is a circuit in which components are connected end-to-end, forming a single current path. The same current flows through every component because there is no alternative path for the current.

4.2 Characteristics of Series Circuits

  • The same current flows through all resistors.
  • The total resistance is the sum of individual resistances.
  • The supply voltage is divided across the resistors.
  • The total voltage equals the sum of individual voltage drops.
  • If one component becomes open-circuit, the entire series path is interrupted.

4.3 Series Circuit Diagram

Three resistors connected in series. The same current passes through R₁, R₂ and R₃.

4.4 Equivalent Resistance in Series

The equivalent resistance of series-connected resistors is equal to the sum of all individual resistances.

RT = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ... + Rn

4.5 Solved Example – Series Resistance

Given: Three resistors of 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ are connected in series across a 12 V supply. Calculate equivalent resistance and circuit current.

Step 1: Calculate equivalent resistance.
RT = R₁ + R₂ + R₃
RT = 1 kΩ + 2 kΩ + 3 kΩ
RT = 6 kΩ
Step 2: Calculate total current.
I = V / RT
I = 12 / 6000
I = 2 mA
Step 3: Calculate individual voltage drops.
V₁ = 2 mA × 1 kΩ = 2 V
V₂ = 2 mA × 2 kΩ = 4 V
V₃ = 2 mA × 3 kΩ = 6 V
Step 4: Verify using KVL.
V = V₁ + V₂ + V₃
12 = 2 + 4 + 6
12 = 12 V
Final result:
  • Equivalent resistance = 6 kΩ.
  • Total current = 2 mA.
  • Voltage drops = 2 V, 4 V and 6 V.

5. Parallel Resistor Circuits

5.1 What Is a Parallel Circuit?

A parallel circuit is a circuit in which components are connected across the same two electrical nodes. Each resistor has the same voltage across it, while the total current divides among the available branches.

5.2 Characteristics of Parallel Circuits

  • The voltage across every parallel resistor is the same.
  • The total current is the sum of the branch currents.
  • The equivalent resistance is lower than the smallest individual resistance for positive finite resistors.
  • Adding another parallel resistor decreases the equivalent resistance.
  • If one branch becomes open-circuit, current can still flow through the remaining branches.

5.3 Parallel Circuit Diagram

Three resistors connected across the same two nodes. Each resistor has the same voltage across it.

5.4 Equivalent Resistance in Parallel

The reciprocal of the equivalent resistance is equal to the sum of the reciprocals of the individual resistances.

1/RT = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ... + 1/Rn

5.5 Two Resistors in Parallel

For two parallel resistors, the equivalent resistance can be calculated directly using the product-over-sum formula.

RT = (R₁ × R₂) / (R₁ + R₂)

5.6 Solved Example – Two Parallel Resistors

Given: Two resistors of 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ are connected in parallel across a 12 V supply. Calculate equivalent resistance and total current.

Step 1: Calculate equivalent resistance.
RT = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) kΩ
RT = 18/9 kΩ
RT = 2 kΩ
Step 2: Calculate total current.
IT = V/RT
IT = 12/2k
IT = 6 mA
Step 3: Calculate branch currents.
I₁ = 12/6k = 2 mA
I₂ = 12/3k = 4 mA
Step 4: Verify using KCL.
IT = I₁ + I₂
6 = 2 + 4
6 mA = 6 mA
Final result:
  • Equivalent resistance = 2 kΩ.
  • Total current = 6 mA.
  • Current through 6 kΩ = 2 mA.
  • Current through 3 kΩ = 4 mA.

5.7 Parallel Resistance – Three Equal Resistors

When n equal resistors, each having resistance R, are connected in parallel:

RT = R/n

For example, three 9 kΩ resistors connected in parallel have:

RT = 9/3 = 3 kΩ

5.8 Important Property of Parallel Resistance

For two or more positive, finite resistors connected in parallel, the equivalent resistance is always less than the smallest individual resistance. This is a useful quick check during circuit calculations.

6. Voltage Division Rule

6.1 What Is Voltage Division?

The voltage division rule is used to calculate the voltage across an individual resistor in a series network. The supply voltage is divided among the resistors in proportion to their resistance values.

6.2 Voltage Divider Formula

For a series circuit containing n resistors, the voltage across resistor Rx is:

Vx = VT × Rx/RT

For two resistors:

VR1 = Vin × R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
VR2 = Vin × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)

6.3 Voltage Divider Circuit

Two-resistor voltage divider. The output is measured across R₂.

6.4 Solved Example – Voltage Divider

Given: A 12 V supply is connected across two series resistors: R₁ = 2 kΩ and R₂ = 4 kΩ. Calculate the voltage across each resistor.

Step 1: Calculate total resistance.
RT = 2 + 4 = 6 kΩ
Step 2: Calculate voltage across R₁.
V₁ = 12 × 2/6
V₁ = 4 V
Step 3: Calculate voltage across R₂.
V₂ = 12 × 4/6
V₂ = 8 V
Step 4: Verify using KVL.
12 = 4 + 8 V
Answer: Voltage across R₁ is 4 V and voltage across R₂ is 8 V.

6.5 Loaded Voltage Divider

A voltage divider is called loaded when an external load resistor is connected across its output. The load resistor becomes parallel with the lower divider resistor and changes the output voltage.

Consider a divider with:

  • Input voltage = 12 V
  • R₁ = 2 kΩ
  • R₂ = 4 kΩ
  • Load resistor RL = 4 kΩ connected across R₂
Step 1: Calculate the parallel combination of R₂ and RL.
Rparallel = (4 × 4)/(4 + 4)
Rparallel = 2 kΩ
Step 2: Calculate total resistance.
RT = 2 + 2 = 4 kΩ
Step 3: Calculate output voltage.
Vout = 12 × 2/4
Vout = 6 V

Without the load, the output voltage was 8 V. After connecting the load, it becomes 6 V.

Important: The basic voltage divider formula assumes that the output is unloaded or that the load impedance is sufficiently high. When a load is connected, calculate the parallel combination before applying the voltage divider rule.

6.6 Practical Applications of Voltage Dividers

  • Generating a reference voltage.
  • Scaling sensor output voltage.
  • Reducing a signal voltage before an ADC input.
  • Setting transistor bias voltages.
  • Reading potentiometer positions in electronic systems.

7. Current Division Rule

7.1 What Is Current Division?

The current division rule is used to calculate the current flowing through an individual branch of a parallel resistor network. The total current divides among parallel branches according to their resistance values.

A lower-resistance branch carries more current, while a higher-resistance branch carries less current.

7.2 Current Divider Formula for Two Resistors

For two resistors R₁ and R₂ connected in parallel, with total current IT entering the parallel combination:

I₁ = IT × R₂/(R₁ + R₂)
I₂ = IT × R₁/(R₁ + R₂)

Notice that the current through one resistor is proportional to the resistance of the other parallel resistor.

7.3 Current Divider Circuit

Total current divides between two parallel resistor branches.

7.4 Solved Example – Current Division

Given: A total current of 9 mA enters two parallel resistors of 3 kΩ and 6 kΩ. Calculate the current through each resistor.

Step 1: Calculate current through 3 kΩ.
I₁ = 9 × 6/(3 + 6)
I₁ = 9 × 6/9
I₁ = 6 mA
Step 2: Calculate current through 6 kΩ.
I₂ = 9 × 3/(3 + 6)
I₂ = 9 × 3/9
I₂ = 3 mA
Step 3: Verify using KCL.
IT = I₁ + I₂ = 6 + 3 = 9 mA
Answer:
  • Current through 3 kΩ = 6 mA.
  • Current through 6 kΩ = 3 mA.

7.5 Current Division for Multiple Parallel Resistors

For n parallel resistors, the current through resistor Rx can be calculated using conductance:

Ix = IT × (1/Rx) / [Σ(1/R)]

Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance:

G = 1/R

Its SI unit is siemens (S).

8. Series-Parallel Combination Circuits

8.1 What Is a Combination Circuit?

A combination circuit contains both series and parallel connections. Such networks are common in practical electronic circuits because components are connected in different configurations to achieve required resistance and current characteristics.

8.2 Method of Circuit Reduction

  1. Identify groups of resistors that are clearly in series or parallel.
  2. Calculate the equivalent resistance of one group.
  3. Replace that group with its equivalent resistor.
  4. Repeat the process until the complete network is reduced to one equivalent resistance.
  5. Calculate the total supply current using Ohm's law.
  6. Work backwards through the original network to find individual branch currents and voltages.
  7. Verify the results using KCL, KVL and power calculations.

8.3 Combination Circuit Example

Consider a circuit containing a 2 kΩ resistor in series with a parallel combination of 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ resistors. The supply voltage is 12 V.

A 2 kΩ resistor in series with a parallel combination of 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ.
Step 1: Identify the parallel resistors.

6 kΩ and 3 kΩ are connected across the same two nodes.

Step 2: Calculate their equivalent resistance.
RP = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3)
RP = 2 kΩ
Step 3: Calculate total resistance.

The 2 kΩ resistor is in series with the equivalent 2 kΩ parallel section.

RT = 2 + 2
RT = 4 kΩ
Step 4: Calculate total supply current.
IT = 12/4k
IT = 3 mA
Step 5: Calculate voltage across the series resistor.
V₁ = 3 mA × 2 kΩ
V₁ = 6 V
Step 6: Calculate voltage across the parallel section.
VP = 12 - 6
VP = 6 V
Step 7: Calculate individual branch currents.
I₁ = 6/6k = 1 mA
I₂ = 6/3k = 2 mA
Step 8: Verify using KCL.
IT = I₁ + I₂
3 = 1 + 2 mA
Final result:
  • Total equivalent resistance = 4 kΩ.
  • Total supply current = 3 mA.
  • Voltage across the series 2 kΩ resistor = 6 V.
  • Voltage across each parallel branch = 6 V.
  • Current through 6 kΩ = 1 mA.
  • Current through 3 kΩ = 2 mA.

9. Advanced Combination Circuit Analysis

9.1 A Network with Two Parallel Groups in Series

Consider two parallel groups connected in series across a 20 V supply:

  • First parallel group: 4 kΩ and 4 kΩ.
  • Second parallel group: 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ.
Step 1: Reduce the first parallel group.
R₁ = (4 × 4)/(4 + 4) = 2 kΩ
Step 2: Reduce the second parallel group.
R₂ = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 2 kΩ
Step 3: Calculate total resistance.
RT = 2 + 2 = 4 kΩ
Step 4: Calculate total current.
IT = 20/4k = 5 mA
Step 5: Calculate voltage across each group.
V₁ = 5 mA × 2 kΩ = 10 V
V₂ = 5 mA × 2 kΩ = 10 V
Step 6: Calculate individual branch currents.
First group: 10/4k = 2.5 mA in each branch
Second group: 10/6k = 1.667 mA
Second group: 10/3k = 3.333 mA
Verification:

First parallel group: 2.5 + 2.5 = 5 mA.

Second parallel group: 1.667 + 3.333 = 5 mA.

9.2 When Series-Parallel Reduction Is Not Sufficient

Not every resistor network can be reduced by simple series-parallel combinations. If the circuit contains bridge connections or multiple interconnected branches, nodal analysis or mesh analysis may be required.

For such circuits, use the methods covered in Lecture 5 – Kirchhoff's Laws and Circuit Analysis.

Interview point: Before combining two resistors, check their connection nodes. Two resistors are in series only when their common node has no other current path. Two resistors are in parallel only when both ends connect to the same two nodes.

10. Open Circuit and Short Circuit in Resistor Networks

10.1 Open Circuit

An open circuit is a broken or interrupted current path. Its ideal resistance is infinite, and no current flows through the open path.

Ropen → ∞
I = 0 A

Examples include a broken wire, disconnected resistor lead, open fuse or damaged PCB track.

10.2 Short Circuit

A short circuit is a connection between two points through a path with ideally zero resistance. The voltage across an ideal short circuit is zero.

Rshort = 0 Ω
V = 0 V

In a real circuit, a short circuit may cause excessive current limited only by source impedance, wiring resistance and protective components.

10.3 Comparison

ParameterOpen CircuitShort Circuit
Ideal resistanceInfiniteZero
Current through the fault pathZeroLimited by the rest of the circuit
Voltage across the faultMay be non-zeroZero ideally
Typical causeBroken connectionUnintended conductive connection
Practical effectInterrupted operationExcessive current or malfunction

11. Power in Series and Parallel Resistors

11.1 Power Formulas

The power dissipated by a resistor can be calculated using any of the following formulas:

P = VI
P = I²R
P = V²/R

11.2 Power Distribution in Series Circuits

In a series circuit, the same current flows through all resistors. Therefore, power dissipation is proportional to resistance.

P = I²R

A higher-resistance resistor dissipates more power when the same current flows through all series resistors.

11.3 Power Distribution in Parallel Circuits

In a parallel circuit, the same voltage is present across every resistor. Therefore, power dissipation is inversely proportional to resistance.

P = V²/R

A lower-resistance resistor dissipates more power when all parallel resistors have the same voltage across them.

11.4 Solved Example – Resistor Power Rating

A 1 kΩ resistor is connected across a 12 V supply. Calculate the power dissipated and select a suitable resistor rating.

P = V²/R
P = 12²/1000
P = 0.144 W = 144 mW

A resistor rated at 0.25 W has a nominal rating above the calculated dissipation. In practical designs, engineers also consider ambient temperature, enclosure conditions, derating and reliability. A 0.5 W resistor may be selected where additional thermal margin is required.

12. Practical Applications in Electronics

12.1 Voltage Divider for Sensor Interfaces

Two resistors can scale a sensor voltage to a suitable level for an ADC input. The output voltage depends on the resistor ratio and the load connected to the divider.

12.2 Current-Limiting Resistors for LEDs

A series resistor limits current through an LED. Its value is selected using the supply voltage, LED forward voltage and desired current.

R = (Vsupply - VF)/ILED

12.3 Pull-Up and Pull-Down Resistors

Pull-up and pull-down resistors establish a defined logic level at digital input pins when a switch or open-drain output is not actively driving the line.

12.4 Transistor Bias Networks

Resistor networks are used to establish base voltage and emitter current in transistor bias circuits. Voltage-divider bias is a common configuration.

12.5 Current Sensing

A low-value resistor, called a shunt resistor, is connected in series with a load. The voltage drop across it is measured to estimate current.

I = Vshunt/Rshunt

12.6 Embedded System ADC Input Scaling

A resistor divider can reduce an external voltage to a range suitable for a microcontroller ADC. The divider output impedance and ADC input characteristics must be considered to avoid measurement errors.

13. Practical Measurement and Troubleshooting

13.1 Measuring Resistance

  1. Switch off the circuit power.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely before measuring.
  3. Set the digital multimeter to resistance mode.
  4. Place the probes across the resistor.
  5. Compare the measured value with the resistor's nominal value and tolerance.
Important: For an accurate in-circuit resistance measurement, isolate at least one resistor terminal when parallel paths can affect the reading. Never measure resistance on a powered circuit.

13.2 Measuring Voltage in a Series Circuit

  1. Set the DMM to the correct DC voltage range.
  2. Connect the black probe to the circuit reference point.
  3. Connect the red probe across the component being measured.
  4. Record the voltage drop across each resistor.
  5. Verify that the sum of the drops equals the supply voltage.

13.3 Measuring Current in a Parallel Circuit

  1. Switch off the supply before changing the meter connection.
  2. Set the meter to the correct current range and input terminal.
  3. Open the branch being tested.
  4. Connect the meter in series with that branch.
  5. Power the circuit and record the branch current.
  6. Repeat for the remaining branches if necessary.
  7. Verify that the sum of branch currents equals the total current.
Meter safety: Never connect a multimeter in current mode directly across a voltage source. Always connect it in series with the branch being measured. Check the current input terminal and fuse rating before taking measurements.

13.5 Practical Example – LED Current-Limiting Resistor

A red LED with a forward voltage of 2 V is connected to a 5 V supply. The required LED current is 10 mA. Calculate the series resistor.

Step 1: Calculate voltage across the resistor.
VR = 5 - 2 = 3 V
Step 2: Calculate resistance.
R = VR/I
R = 3/0.01
R = 300 Ω
Step 3: Calculate resistor power.
P = I²R
P = (0.01)² × 300
P = 0.03 W = 30 mW

A standard 330 Ω resistor can be selected to keep the current slightly below the calculated 10 mA, assuming the LED forward voltage is approximately 2 V.

I = (5 - 2)/330
I ≈ 9.09 mA

14. Common Mistakes in Resistor Network Analysis

  • Assuming that resistors are in series merely because they are drawn one after another. Check whether their common node has another branch.
  • Assuming that resistors are parallel because they appear side by side. Verify that both ends connect to the same two nodes.
  • Adding parallel resistances directly instead of adding their reciprocals.
  • Using the wrong resistor in the current divider formula.
  • Applying the unloaded voltage divider formula when an output load is connected.
  • Ignoring the effect of resistor tolerance.
  • Mixing Ω and kΩ or A and mA during calculations.
  • Ignoring resistor power ratings.
  • Measuring resistance while the circuit is powered.
  • Connecting an ammeter directly across a voltage source.

15. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a series circuit?

Answer: A series circuit has a single current path, so the same current flows through all connected components.

Q2. What is a parallel circuit?

Answer: A parallel circuit has components connected across the same two nodes, so the voltage across each component is the same.

Q3. What is the formula for equivalent resistance in series?

Answer: RT = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ... + Rn.

Q4. What is the formula for equivalent resistance in parallel?

Answer: 1/RT = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ... + 1/Rn.

Q5. What is the equivalent resistance of two parallel resistors?

Answer: RT = (R₁ × R₂)/(R₁ + R₂).

Q6. Which circuit has the same current through all resistors?

Answer: A series circuit.

Q7. Which circuit has the same voltage across all resistors?

Answer: A parallel circuit.

Q8. What happens to equivalent resistance when another resistor is added in series?

Answer: The total resistance increases.

Q9. What happens to equivalent resistance when another resistor is added in parallel?

Answer: The equivalent resistance decreases.

Q10. State the voltage division rule.

Answer: In a series circuit, voltage across a resistor is proportional to its resistance. Vx = VT × Rx/RT.

Q11. State the current division rule for two parallel resistors.

Answer: I₁ = IT × R₂/(R₁ + R₂), and I₂ = IT × R₁/(R₁ + R₂).

Q12. Why does a lower resistance branch carry more current in parallel?

Answer: Every branch has the same voltage. From Ohm's law, I = V/R, so a lower resistance produces a higher current.

Q13. What is a voltage divider?

Answer: A voltage divider is a resistor network, usually made from series resistors, that produces an output voltage lower than the input voltage.

Q14. What happens when a load is connected to a voltage divider?

Answer: The load is in parallel with the lower divider resistor and usually reduces the output voltage.

Q15. What is a current divider?

Answer: A current divider is a parallel network in which the input current divides among the branches according to their resistances.

Q16. Can two resistors of equal value be connected in parallel?

Answer: Yes. Their equivalent resistance is half the resistance of either resistor.

Q17. What is an open circuit?

Answer: An open circuit is an interrupted path with ideally infinite resistance and zero current through that path.

Q18. What is a short circuit?

Answer: A short circuit is an unintended or direct low-resistance connection between two points. Ideally, the voltage across the short is zero.

Q19. How do you identify whether two resistors are in series?

Answer: They must share a common node with no other branch connected at that node, so the same current passes through both.

Q20. How do you identify whether two resistors are in parallel?

Answer: Both terminals of one resistor must connect to the same two nodes as the other resistor.

Q21. What is the SI unit of conductance?

Answer: Siemens (S). Conductance is the reciprocal of resistance.

Q22. Why is resistor power rating important?

Answer: A resistor must safely dissipate the electrical power converted into heat. Exceeding its rating can cause overheating, value drift or component failure.

Q23. How is a shunt resistor used for current measurement?

Answer: A low-value resistor is connected in series with the load. The voltage drop across it is measured, and current is calculated using I = V/R.

Q24. What should be checked before measuring resistance with a DMM?

Answer: Switch off the circuit power, discharge capacitors safely and isolate a resistor terminal if parallel paths can affect the reading.

16. Numerical Practice Problems

Problem 1 – Series Resistance

Three resistors of 2 kΩ, 3 kΩ and 5 kΩ are connected in series across a 20 V supply. Calculate equivalent resistance and total current.

Answer: RT = 10 kΩ; I = 20/10k = 2 mA.

Problem 2 – Parallel Resistance

Two resistors of 4 kΩ and 12 kΩ are connected in parallel. Calculate equivalent resistance.

Answer: RT = (4 × 12)/(4 + 12) = 3 kΩ.

Problem 3 – Voltage Division

A 15 V supply is connected across 2 kΩ and 3 kΩ resistors in series. Calculate the voltage across each resistor.

Answer: Total resistance = 5 kΩ; current = 3 mA; voltage drops = 6 V and 9 V.

Problem 4 – Current Division

A total current of 12 mA enters two parallel resistors of 2 kΩ and 4 kΩ. Calculate the current through each resistor.

Answer: Current through 2 kΩ = 8 mA; current through 4 kΩ = 4 mA.

Problem 5 – Combination Circuit

A 3 kΩ resistor is connected in series with a parallel combination of 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ. The supply voltage is 12 V. Calculate total resistance and supply current.

Answer: Parallel equivalent = 2 kΩ; total resistance = 5 kΩ; supply current = 2.4 mA.

Problem 6 – Loaded Voltage Divider

A 10 V supply is connected to R₁ = 1 kΩ and R₂ = 1 kΩ in series. A 1 kΩ load is connected across R₂. Calculate the loaded output voltage.

Answer: R₂ parallel RL = 0.5 kΩ; total resistance = 1.5 kΩ; output voltage = 10 × 0.5/1.5 = 3.33 V approximately.

Problem 7 – Resistor Power

A 2 kΩ resistor is connected across a 10 V supply. Calculate current and power.

Answer: I = 5 mA; P = 50 mW.

Problem 8 – LED Current Limiting

A 9 V supply is connected to an LED with a forward voltage of 2 V. The required current is 20 mA. Calculate the resistor value and power dissipation.

Answer: R = (9 - 2)/0.02 = 350 Ω; P = I²R = 0.14 W. Select a suitable standard resistor value and power rating with adequate margin.

17. Practical Interview and Lab Tasks

  1. Connect three resistors in series and measure the total resistance using a DMM.
  2. Connect three resistors in parallel and compare measured equivalent resistance with the calculated value.
  3. Build a voltage divider and measure the output voltage across the lower resistor.
  4. Connect a load resistor across a voltage divider and observe the change in output voltage.
  5. Build a two-branch parallel circuit and verify current division.
  6. Construct a series-parallel resistor network and calculate its total resistance.
  7. Calculate the current-limiting resistor for an LED and verify the LED current.
  8. Simulate a resistor network in LTspice or Proteus and compare simulated and calculated values.
  9. Introduce an open connection in one branch and observe the effect on the circuit.
  10. Calculate resistor power dissipation and select a suitable power rating.

Recommended Tools

  • Digital multimeter (DMM)
  • Regulated DC power supply
  • Resistor assortment and breadboard
  • LEDs and connecting wires
  • LTspice or Proteus circuit simulation software
