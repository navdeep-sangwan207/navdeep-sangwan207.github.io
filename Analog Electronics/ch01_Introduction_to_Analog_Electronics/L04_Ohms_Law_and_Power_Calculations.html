1. Lecture Overview

Ohm's law is one of the most frequently used relationships in electrical and electronic circuit analysis. It connects voltage, current and resistance. Combined with electrical power equations, it helps engineers calculate circuit operating conditions, select components and identify faults.

Placement objective: Analyze practical resistor circuits, calculate branch currents and voltage drops, determine power dissipation, and identify whether components are operating within their ratings.

2. Ohm's Law

Ohm's law states that, for an ohmic conductor under constant physical conditions such as temperature, current is directly proportional to the voltage applied across it.

V = I × R

I = V / R

R = V / I

Where:

  • V = voltage in volts (V)
  • I = current in amperes (A)
  • R = resistance in ohms (Ω)

V-I Relationship

For a fixed resistance, increasing applied voltage increases current in direct proportion. The resistance determines the slope of the voltage-current relationship.

Example 1: Current Calculation

A 10 Ω resistor is connected across a 20 V supply.

I = V / R = 20 / 10 = 2 A
Answer: Current = 2 A.

Example 2: Voltage Calculation

A 250 Ω resistor carries 40 mA.

I = 40 mA = 0.04 A
V = I × R = 0.04 × 250 = 10 V
Answer: Voltage = 10 V.

Example 3: Resistance Calculation

A voltage of 5 V produces a current of 25 mA.

R = V / I = 5 / 0.025 = 200 Ω
Answer: Resistance = 200 Ω.

3. Linear and Nonlinear V-I Characteristics

Linear Components

An ideal fixed resistor is an example of a linear component when its temperature and other physical conditions remain constant. Its voltage-current graph is a straight line through the origin.

Nonlinear Components

A nonlinear component does not maintain a constant ratio of voltage to current over its complete operating range.

ComponentV-I behaviorExample
Fixed resistorApproximately linearCurrent limiting
DiodeNonlinearRectification

For nonlinear components, the ratio V/I at a particular operating point is called static resistance. The small-signal or dynamic resistance is the local slope dV/dI around that operating point.

4. Series Resistor Circuits

In a series circuit, components are connected end-to-end and carry the same current.

Characteristics

  • Current is the same through every series element.
  • Total voltage equals the sum of individual voltage drops.
  • Equivalent resistance is the sum of all resistances.
  • An open circuit in one element interrupts the entire current path.
RT = R1 + R2 + R3 + ... + Rn

Series Circuit Diagram

Solved Example

Three resistors of 10 Ω, 20 Ω and 30 Ω are connected in series across a 12 V supply. Calculate total resistance, circuit current and voltage drops.

RT = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60 Ω

I = V / RT = 12 / 60 = 0.2 A

V1 = I × R1 = 0.2 × 10 = 2 V
V2 = 0.2 × 20 = 4 V
V3 = 0.2 × 30 = 6 V
ResistorResistanceVoltage drop
R110 Ω2 V
R220 Ω4 V
R330 Ω6 V
Total60 Ω12 V
Answer: Total resistance = 60 Ω; current = 0.2 A. The voltage drops add up to the supply voltage.

5. Parallel Resistor Circuits

In a parallel circuit, components are connected across the same two electrical nodes. Each branch has the same voltage.

Characteristics

  • Voltage is the same across all parallel branches.
  • Total current equals the sum of branch currents.
  • Equivalent resistance is less than the smallest positive branch resistance.
  • Each branch provides a separate current path.
1 / RT = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + 1 / R3 + ... + 1 / Rn

For two parallel resistors:

RT = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)

Parallel Circuit Diagram

Solved Example 1: Two Parallel Resistors

Two resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω are connected in parallel. Calculate equivalent resistance.

RT = (R1 × R2) / (R1 + R2)

RT = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3)
RT = 18 / 9 = 2 Ω
Answer: 2 Ω.

Solved Example 2: Three Parallel Resistors

Resistors of 6 Ω, 12 Ω and 4 Ω are connected in parallel.

1 / RT = 1/6 + 1/12 + 1/4

1 / RT = 2/12 + 1/12 + 3/12
1 / RT = 6/12 = 1/2
RT = 2 Ω
Answer: 2 Ω.

6. Mixed Series-Parallel Circuits

Many practical circuits contain both series and parallel connections. They are analyzed by simplifying one section at a time.

Analysis Procedure

  1. Identify groups of series and parallel resistors.
  2. Calculate the equivalent resistance of the simplest group.
  3. Replace that group with its equivalent resistance.
  4. Repeat until one total resistance remains.
  5. Calculate the total current using Ohm's law.
  6. Work backwards to calculate branch currents and voltage drops.

Solved Example

A 4 Ω resistor is connected in series with a parallel combination of 6 Ω and 3 Ω. The complete circuit is supplied by 12 V. Calculate total resistance and current.

Rparallel = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3)
Rparallel = 18 / 9 = 2 Ω

RT = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω

IT = 12 / 6 = 2 A

Voltage across the 4 Ω resistor:

V4 = 2 × 4 = 8 V

Voltage across the parallel network:

Vparallel = 12 - 8 = 4 V

Branch currents:

I6 = 4 / 6 = 0.667 A
I3 = 4 / 3 = 1.333 A

I6 + I3 = 2 A
Answer: Total resistance = 6 Ω; total current = 2 A. The branch currents add up to the source current.

7. Voltage Divider Rule

The voltage divider rule is used to calculate the voltage across individual series resistors.

For two series resistors:

V1 = VS × R1 / (R1 + R2)

V2 = VS × R2 / (R1 + R2)

For a resistor Rk in a series chain:

Vk = VS × Rk / RT

Voltage Divider Diagram

Solved Example

A 12 V supply is connected to two series resistors: R1 = 2 kΩ and R2 = 4 kΩ. Find the voltage across each resistor.

RT = 2 kΩ + 4 kΩ = 6 kΩ

V1 = 12 × (2/6) = 4 V
V2 = 12 × (4/6) = 8 V
Answer: V1 = 4 V and V2 = 8 V.

Practical Application: Sensor Voltage Divider

A resistive sensor can be connected with a fixed resistor to generate a voltage that changes with sensor resistance. This voltage can be measured by a microcontroller ADC after ensuring the voltage remains within the ADC's permitted input range.

A voltage divider's output changes when a load is connected. The simple unloaded divider equation is accurate only when the output load has negligible effect. Otherwise, include the load resistance in the equivalent circuit.

8. Current Divider Rule

The current divider rule is used to calculate current through parallel branches.

For two parallel resistors:

I1 = IT × R2 / (R1 + R2)

I2 = IT × R1 / (R1 + R2)

The current in a branch is inversely proportional to that branch's resistance.

Solved Example

A total current of 6 A enters two parallel resistors of 3 Ω and 6 Ω. Calculate branch currents.

I3 = 6 × 6 / (3 + 6)
I3 = 4 A

I6 = 6 × 3 / (3 + 6)
I6 = 2 A
Answer: The 3 Ω branch carries 4 A and the 6 Ω branch carries 2 A.

9. Kirchhoff's Laws and Ohm's Law

Ohm's law is frequently used together with Kirchhoff's laws to analyze larger circuits.

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

The algebraic sum of currents at a node is zero. Equivalently, total current entering a node equals total current leaving it.

ΣI = 0

ΣI entering = ΣI leaving

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

The algebraic sum of all voltage rises and drops around a closed loop is zero.

ΣV = 0

Solved KCL Example

Two currents of 2 A and 3 A enter a node. One current of 1 A leaves the node. Calculate the remaining outgoing current.

2 + 3 = 1 + I
I = 4 A
Answer: 4 A.

Solved KVL Example

A series loop contains a 12 V source and two voltage drops. One resistor has a drop of 5 V. Calculate the second drop.

12 - 5 - V2 = 0
V2 = 7 V
Answer: 7 V.

A detailed treatment of Kirchhoff's laws and circuit analysis is provided in Lecture 5.

10. Electrical Power Calculations

For a DC circuit, power absorbed by a component is calculated using voltage and current.

P = V × I

P = I²R

P = V² / R

Choosing the Correct Formula

Known valuesUseful formula
Voltage and currentP = VI
Current and resistanceP = I²R
Voltage and resistanceP = V²/R

Solved Example 1

A 24 V DC supply drives a 12 Ω resistor. Calculate current and power.

I = 24 / 12 = 2 A
P = 24 × 2 = 48 W
Answer: Current = 2 A; power = 48 W.

Solved Example 2

A 5 Ω resistor carries 4 A. Calculate voltage and power.

V = 4 × 5 = 20 V
P = 4² × 5 = 80 W
Answer: Voltage = 20 V; power = 80 W.

Solved Example 3

A 100 Ω resistor is connected across 10 V. Calculate power.

P = V² / R
P = 10² / 100 = 1 W
Answer: 1 W.

11. Power Dissipation and Resistor Selection

A resistor dissipates electrical power as heat. Its power rating must be selected according to the expected operating conditions.

Example: Selecting a Resistor

A 1 kΩ resistor is connected across 12 V. Calculate its power and evaluate a 0.25 W resistor.

P = V² / R
P = 12² / 1000
P = 144 / 1000 = 0.144 W

The calculated dissipation is 0.144 W. It is below the nominal 0.25 W rating, but the actual suitability must be checked against the manufacturer's derating curve and operating temperature.

Design Margin

  • Calculate worst-case resistor dissipation.
  • Check maximum working voltage.
  • Check ambient temperature and thermal derating.
  • Consider ventilation and PCB copper area.
  • Use an appropriate safety margin for reliability.
A resistor can fail even when its calculated power is below its nominal rating if voltage, pulse energy, temperature or other datasheet limits are exceeded.

12. Practical Application: LED Current-Limiting Circuit

An LED requires a current-limiting element in a simple voltage-driven circuit. The resistor limits current and protects the LED from excessive current.

Design Problem

Design a series resistor for a 5 V supply, an LED forward voltage of 2 V and a target current of 15 mA.

VR = VS - VF
VR = 5 - 2 = 3 V

R = VR / I
R = 3 / 0.015 = 200 Ω

A standard 220 Ω resistor gives approximately:

I = 3 / 220
I ≈ 13.64 mA

Resistor power:

PR = I²R
PR = (0.01364)² × 220
PR ≈ 0.041 W
Design result: Use 220 Ω as a practical standard value for approximately 13.6 mA under the stated assumptions. A 0.125 W resistor has nominal power headroom, subject to its datasheet limits and operating conditions.

13. Practical Application: Voltage Divider for an ADC

A microcontroller ADC may accept only a limited input voltage. A voltage divider can scale a higher voltage to a safe measurement range, provided the divider and ADC input specifications are properly considered.

Design Problem

A 12 V signal must be reduced to 3 V using a two-resistor divider. Choose R1 and R2 such that:

  • R1 is connected from the input to the output node.
  • R2 is connected from the output node to ground.
  • Output voltage is 3 V when input is 12 V.

Using the divider equation:

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

3 = 12 × R2 / (R1 + R2)
1/4 = R2 / (R1 + R2)
R1 = 3R2

Choose R2 = 10 kΩ and R1 = 30 kΩ.

Vout = 12 × 10 / (30 + 10)
Vout = 3 V
Design result: R1 = 30 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ produce 3 V from 12 V under the unloaded ideal-divider assumption.
Check ADC absolute maximum ratings, input leakage, sampling capacitor requirements, source impedance, transient protection and ground reference. A divider alone does not guarantee safe measurement of an unknown or surge-prone input.

14. Practical Application: Measuring Current Using a Shunt Resistor

A shunt resistor is a low-value precision resistor used to measure current by measuring the small voltage developed across it.

Vshunt = I × Rshunt

I = Vshunt / Rshunt

Solved Example

A 0.1 Ω shunt resistor produces a voltage drop of 50 mV. Calculate current and shunt power.

V = 50 mV = 0.05 V
I = 0.05 / 0.1 = 0.5 A

P = I²R
P = 0.5² × 0.1 = 0.025 W
Answer: Current = 0.5 A; shunt power = 25 mW.

Engineering Considerations

  • Use a precision resistor with a suitable tolerance.
  • Check temperature coefficient.
  • Consider PCB trace resistance.
  • Use Kelvin connections when accuracy requires them.
  • Ensure the sensing amplifier supports the common-mode voltage.

15. Practical Application: Power Supply Load Calculation

A power supply must provide sufficient current and power for all connected loads.

Solved Example

A 12 V DC supply powers three loads drawing 0.5 A, 0.8 A and 0.2 A respectively. Calculate total current and output power.

IT = 0.5 + 0.8 + 0.2
IT = 1.5 A

P = V × I
P = 12 × 1.5 = 18 W
Answer: Total load current = 1.5 A; output power = 18 W.

The selected supply should support the required voltage and continuous current with suitable margin. Also verify startup current, transient load behavior, temperature derating and protection requirements.

Recommended Troubleshooting Sequence

  1. Read the circuit diagram and identify the expected operating values.
  2. Verify the supply voltage before connecting the load.
  3. Inspect component values and polarity.
  4. Measure voltage across individual components.
  5. Measure current using a properly configured meter.
  6. Calculate actual resistance and power where appropriate.
  7. Compare measurements with expected values.
  8. Correct the fault and repeat the measurements.

17. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. State Ohm's law.

For an ohmic conductor under constant physical conditions, voltage is equal to current multiplied by resistance: V = IR.

Q2. What happens to current when resistance doubles at constant voltage?

Current becomes half its original value.

Q3. What happens to current when voltage doubles at constant resistance?

Current doubles.

Q4. What is equivalent resistance in a series circuit?

It is the sum of all individual resistances.

Q5. What is equivalent resistance in a parallel circuit?

The reciprocal of total resistance equals the sum of reciprocals of the individual branch resistances.

Q6. Why is parallel equivalent resistance smaller than the smallest branch resistance?

Adding parallel branches creates additional current paths, increasing total conductance and reducing equivalent resistance.

Q7. What is the voltage divider rule?

In a series resistor network, the voltage across a resistor is proportional to its resistance.

Q8. What is the current divider rule?

In a parallel network, branch current is inversely proportional to branch resistance.

Q9. What is the difference between series and parallel circuits?

Series elements carry the same current. Parallel elements have the same voltage across them.

Q10. What is the power equation for a resistor?

P = VI, P = I²R or P = V²/R.

Q11. Why is a resistor used with an LED?

It limits current to a safe operating value.

Q12. What happens when a resistor opens in a series circuit?

The current path is interrupted, so current stops throughout the series loop.

Q13. What happens if one branch opens in a parallel circuit?

Current stops in that branch, but other branches can continue operating if the source remains functional.

Q14. What is a shunt resistor?

A low-value resistor used to determine current from the voltage drop across it.

Q15. What is the difference between static and dynamic resistance?

Static resistance is V/I at an operating point. Dynamic resistance is the local slope dV/dI around that point.

Q16. Why does a voltage divider output change when a load is connected?

The load forms a parallel combination with the lower divider resistor, changing the effective resistance and output voltage.

Q17. What should be checked when selecting a resistor?

Resistance, tolerance, power rating, maximum working voltage, temperature coefficient and derating requirements.

19. Lecture Summary

  • Ohm's law: V = IR.
  • Series resistors carry the same current.
  • Parallel resistors have the same voltage.
  • Series equivalent resistance is the sum of resistances.
  • Parallel equivalent resistance is calculated using reciprocal addition.
  • Voltage divider rule calculates voltage across series elements.
  • Current divider rule calculates current in parallel branches.
  • Electrical power is P = VI = I²R = V²/R for a resistor.
  • Power rating, working voltage and thermal derating matter during component selection.
  • Shunt resistors enable current measurement.
  • Voltage dividers are useful for signal scaling, subject to loading and input limits.
