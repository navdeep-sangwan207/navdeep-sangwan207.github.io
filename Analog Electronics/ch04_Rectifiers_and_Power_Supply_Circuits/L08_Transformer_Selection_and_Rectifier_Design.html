1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Interpret transformer primary and secondary voltage ratings.
  • Distinguish an ordinary secondary from a centre-tapped secondary.
  • Calculate secondary peak voltage from RMS voltage.
  • Select a transformer based on load requirements and VA rating.
  • Calculate rectifier diode PIV and current requirements.
  • Understand the difference between bridge and centre-tapped rectifiers.
  • Account for diode drops, ripple, regulation and capacitor-filter loading.
  • Develop a practical low-voltage DC power supply design.

2. Role of the Transformer in a DC Power Supply

A transformer is commonly used before a rectifier to provide the required AC voltage and electrical isolation. The rectifier converts the AC voltage into pulsating DC, and the filter reduces the ripple.

Why use a transformer?

  • Voltage conversion: Steps AC voltage up or down according to the design.
  • Isolation: A suitably designed isolation transformer provides galvanic separation between primary and secondary.
  • Current capability: Supplies the required secondary current within its thermal and VA ratings.
  • Impedance transformation: The turns ratio also transforms impedance between windings.
Ideal transformer voltage ratio:
Vp / Vs = Np / Ns

Ideal power relationship:
Vp × Ip ≈ Vs × Is

Real transformers have winding resistance, core losses, leakage reactance and voltage regulation limitations.

3. Transformer Voltage Ratings: RMS and Peak

Transformer secondary voltage ratings are normally specified as RMS values for a sinusoidal AC waveform.

Vpeak = √2 × Vrms
Vrms = Vpeak / √2

Example

A transformer has a 12 V RMS secondary winding.

Vpeak = 1.414 × 12
Vpeak ≈ 16.97 V

The rectifier receives a sinusoidal voltage whose peak is approximately 16.97 V before diode drops and transformer loading effects are considered.

Do not confuse RMS voltage with peak voltage. A 12 V AC secondary does not have a peak voltage of 12 V; its ideal sinusoidal peak is approximately 16.97 V.

4. Ordinary and Centre-Tapped Transformer Secondaries

Ordinary secondary

Has two output terminals. A bridge rectifier normally uses the complete secondary winding.

Example: 12 V AC, two-terminal secondary.

Centre-tapped secondary

Has three terminals. The centre terminal divides the secondary into two equal nominal voltage sections.

Example: 12-0-12 V AC. Each half is nominally 12 V RMS relative to the centre tap.

Transformer markingMeaning
12 VOne 12 V RMS secondary winding with two terminals.
12-0-12 VTwo 12 V RMS sections with a centre tap; end-to-end voltage is nominally 24 V RMS.
6-0-6 VTwo 6 V RMS sections; end-to-end voltage is nominally 12 V RMS.
0-12 VUsually indicates a single 12 V secondary with one terminal marked as reference; check the manufacturer's diagram.
Always verify the manufacturer's winding diagram and terminal markings. Do not assume wire colours or terminal order are universal.

5. Bridge Rectifier Transformer Connection

A bridge rectifier uses the complete two-terminal secondary winding. Two diodes conduct in each half-cycle.

Bridge operation

  • During the positive half-cycle, one diagonal pair of diodes conducts.
  • During the negative half-cycle, the other diagonal pair conducts.
  • Load current flows in the same direction during both half-cycles.
  • Two forward-biased diode drops appear in the conducting path.
Bridge peak output ≈ √2 × Vsec(rms) − 2VF
PIV per diode ≈ Vpeak

For a practical bridge, diode forward voltage depends on current, temperature and the selected diode. Transformer regulation and source impedance also affect the actual peak voltage.

6. Centre-Tapped Full-Wave Rectifier Transformer Connection

A centre-tapped full-wave rectifier uses two diodes. Each diode conducts on alternate half-cycles of the secondary winding.

Important voltage definition

For a 12-0-12 V transformer:

  • Each half-secondary voltage = 12 V RMS.
  • End-to-end secondary voltage = 24 V RMS.
  • Peak voltage of each half = 12 × √2 = 16.97 V.
PIV per diode ≈ 2Vm
where Vm = peak voltage of one half-secondary.
The centre-tapped PIV formula uses the peak voltage of one half-secondary, not the peak voltage of the complete end-to-end secondary.

7. Transformer VA Rating and Current Selection

Transformer capacity is commonly specified in volt-amperes (VA). For a single-phase secondary winding:

VA = Vrms × Irms
Irms = VA / Vrms

For a transformer with multiple secondary windings, the total VA rating and the permitted current of each winding must both be respected.

Example: Secondary current rating

A 12 V, 24 VA transformer has a secondary current rating of:

I = 24 / 12 = 2 A RMS

This is the nominal RMS secondary current rating under the manufacturer's specified operating conditions. It does not mean that a capacitor-input rectifier can automatically deliver 2 A DC continuously.

Why rectifier loading is different

With a capacitor-input filter, the rectifier diodes conduct in short pulses near the AC voltage peaks to recharge the capacitor. The transformer secondary current is therefore not a smooth sinusoidal current equal to the DC load current.

  • Secondary RMS current can be substantially greater than DC load current.
  • Charging-current pulses increase diode and transformer heating.
  • Transformer winding resistance and regulation influence the charging waveform.
  • The exact relationship depends on topology, capacitance, load and source impedance.
Do not select a transformer using only the equation VA = VDC × IDC. Use the transformer's continuous RMS winding-current rating and a design method appropriate to the rectifier topology and filter. Verify the final design against manufacturer data and measured temperature.

8. Rectifier Diode Selection

Diodes must withstand the reverse voltage and forward current conditions of the circuit, including startup and fault conditions.

ParameterMeaningDesign consideration
VRRMRepetitive peak reverse voltageMust exceed the expected repetitive reverse voltage with suitable margin.
IF(AV)Average forward current ratingCheck actual waveform, conduction angle, thermal conditions and datasheet test conditions.
IFSMNon-repetitive peak surge currentImportant for capacitor charging and fault transients; check specified pulse conditions.
VFForward voltage dropAffects output voltage and diode power dissipation.
IRReverse leakage currentRelevant at high temperature and high reverse voltage.
trrReverse recovery timeImportant for switching frequency and recovery losses.
RθThermal resistanceUsed to estimate junction temperature and heatsinking needs.

Practical diode selection checklist

  1. Identify rectifier topology and maximum secondary voltage.
  2. Calculate the diode PIV requirement.
  3. Choose VRRM with appropriate engineering margin.
  4. Determine diode average and RMS current from the actual circuit waveform.
  5. Check IFSM for startup and capacitor-charging pulses.
  6. Calculate conduction loss using actual forward voltage and current.
  7. Check package, thermal resistance, mounting and cooling.
  8. Confirm pinout and polarity from the datasheet.
Approximate diode conduction loss:
P ≈ VF × IF(average)

This is a first-order estimate. Use the actual current waveform and the diode's forward-voltage characteristics for accurate thermal design.

10. Complete Worked Design: 5 V DC Supply

Design objective: Develop a low-voltage, isolated transformer-based supply for a nominal 5 V DC load. This example illustrates the design sequence, not a finished certified power supply.

Given specifications

ParameterDesign value
Target regulated output5 V DC
Load current500 mA
AC frequency50 Hz
RectifierBridge
Transformer secondary9 V RMS
Filter capacitor2200 µF (illustrative starting value)
Regulator5 V regulator, subject to dropout and thermal checks

Step 1: Calculate secondary peak voltage

Vpeak = 9 × 1.414
Vpeak ≈ 12.73 V

Step 2: Estimate capacitor peak voltage

Assume approximately 0.8 V forward drop per conducting silicon diode at the illustrative operating current.

Vpeak,cap ≈ 12.73 − (2 × 0.8)
≈ 11.13 V

The actual drop must be taken from the selected diode's datasheet at the operating current and temperature.

Step 3: Determine ripple frequency

fripple = 2 × fAC
= 2 × 50
= 100 Hz

Step 4: Estimate capacitor ripple

For a full-wave rectifier with a capacitor-input filter and small ripple:

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fripple × C)
= 0.5 / (100 × 0.0022)
≈ 2.27 V peak-to-peak

Step 5: Estimate minimum capacitor voltage

Vmin ≈ Vpeak,cap − Vr(pp)
≈ 11.13 − 2.27
≈ 8.86 V

This is a simplified estimate. Transformer regulation, winding resistance, diode conduction pulses, capacitor ESR and load behaviour can reduce the actual minimum voltage further.

Step 6: Check regulator headroom

A linear 5 V regulator needs its input voltage to remain above its required minimum operating voltage, including dropout/headroom, at the lowest point of the ripple waveform.

The estimated 8.86 V minimum is not sufficient by itself to prove the design. Verify the chosen regulator's input requirements, dropout, maximum input voltage, thermal dissipation and transient behaviour. Measure the actual minimum input voltage at full load.

Step 7: Estimate regulator dissipation

If the regulator input is approximately 10 V under load:

P ≈ (Vin − Vout) × Iload
= (10 − 5) × 0.5
= 2.5 W

This is significant heat. A suitable thermal design or a more efficient conversion method may be required.

Step 8: Select the remaining components

  • Bridge rectifier: select VRRM, average/RMS current, surge rating and thermal capability.
  • Capacitor: select voltage rating, capacitance tolerance, ESR, ripple-current rating and temperature/lifetime rating.
  • Transformer: verify RMS secondary current and VA capacity for capacitor-input operation.
  • Regulator: verify input/output limits, dropout, current limit and heatsinking.
  • Protection: consider fuse, reverse protection and suitable surge protection where required.

13. Transformer and Rectifier Datasheet Checklist

Transformer datasheet

  • Primary rated voltage and frequency.
  • Secondary RMS voltage and current rating.
  • Total VA rating and permissible loading.
  • Voltage regulation and no-load voltage.
  • Insulation class, isolation rating and safety approvals.
  • Temperature rise and ambient operating conditions.
  • Thermal protection and mounting requirements.

Rectifier datasheet

  • VRRM and repetitive reverse voltage.
  • IF(AV), IFSM and RMS current information.
  • Forward voltage at the intended current.
  • Reverse leakage and reverse recovery where relevant.
  • Thermal resistance and permitted junction temperature.
  • Package pinout, polarity and mounting recommendations.

15. Laboratory Exercises

Experiment 1: Measure transformer secondary voltage

Objective: Compare the measured RMS secondary voltage with its rated value.

  1. Use a suitably rated isolated low-voltage transformer.
  2. Identify the secondary terminals from the datasheet.
  3. Measure the unloaded secondary RMS voltage using an appropriate meter.
  4. Connect a rated test load and repeat the measurement.
  5. Record the voltage change under load.

Expected observation: Secondary voltage can decrease under load because of transformer winding impedance and regulation.

Experiment 2: Compare bridge and centre-tapped rectifiers

  1. Build a bridge rectifier with a suitable two-terminal secondary.
  2. Measure the rectified waveform and output voltage.
  3. Build a centre-tapped full-wave circuit using a suitable three-terminal secondary.
  4. Observe the two alternating conduction intervals.
  5. Compare diode drops and transformer winding requirements.

Experiment 3: Verify diode PIV concept

  1. Use a low-voltage isolated source and suitable test circuit.
  2. Identify the reverse-biased diode during each half-cycle.
  3. Use a suitable differential measurement method to observe voltage stress.
  4. Compare the measured result with the ideal topology calculation.
Never connect an ordinary oscilloscope ground clip to an unknown or mains-referenced circuit node. Use an appropriately rated isolated setup and measurement method. De-energize the circuit before changing connections, and discharge filter capacitors through a suitable resistor before handling.

16. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

  1. Why is a transformer used in a rectifier supply?
    To provide the required AC voltage and, where designed for it, galvanic isolation.
  2. What does a 12 V transformer secondary rating mean?
    It normally means 12 V RMS under specified rated conditions.
  3. What is the peak voltage of a 12 V RMS sine wave?
    Approximately 16.97 V.
  4. What does 12-0-12 V mean?
    Two 12 V RMS secondary sections with a centre tap.
  5. What is the end-to-end voltage of a 12-0-12 V transformer?
    Nominally 24 V RMS.
  6. How many diodes conduct in a bridge rectifier at a time?
    Two.
  7. How many diodes conduct in a centre-tapped full-wave rectifier at a time?
    One.
  8. What is the PIV per diode in a bridge rectifier?
    Approximately the peak of the complete secondary voltage.
  9. What is the PIV per diode in a centre-tapped full-wave rectifier?
    Approximately twice the peak voltage of one half-secondary.
  10. What does VA mean?
    Volt-ampere, a measure of apparent power rating.
  11. How is transformer VA calculated?
    For a single-phase winding, VA = RMS voltage × RMS current.
  12. Why can secondary RMS current exceed DC load current?
    Capacitor-input rectifiers draw short charging pulses near voltage peaks.
  13. What is IF(AV)?
    The diode's specified average forward-current rating under stated conditions.
  14. What is IFSM?
    The specified non-repetitive peak forward surge current under defined pulse conditions.
  15. What is VRRM?
    Repetitive peak reverse voltage rating.
  16. Why is a diode heatsink sometimes required?
    To keep junction temperature within the permitted limit when power dissipation is significant.
  17. Why does a transformer secondary voltage fall under load?
    Because of winding resistance, leakage reactance and transformer regulation.
  18. What is transformer regulation?
    A measure of the change in secondary voltage between specified no-load and loaded conditions.
  19. Why can a large filter capacitor stress a diode?
    It can produce high charging-current pulses and startup surge current.
  20. What must be checked before selecting a rectifier diode?
    Reverse voltage, average and surge current, thermal performance, forward drop, package and datasheet conditions.

17. Numerical Practice

Question 1

A transformer secondary is rated at 15 V RMS. Calculate its ideal peak voltage.

Show solution
Vpeak = 15 × 1.414 ≈ 21.21 V

Question 2

A 12-0-12 V transformer is used with a centre-tapped rectifier. Calculate the ideal PIV per diode.

Show solution
Vm = 12 × 1.414 = 16.97 V
PIV = 2 × 16.97 ≈ 33.94 V

Question 3

A 24 VA transformer has a 12 V RMS secondary. Calculate its nominal secondary RMS current rating.

Show solution
I = 24 / 12 = 2 A RMS

Question 4

A 9 V RMS secondary feeds a bridge rectifier. Assume 0.7 V forward drop per conducting diode. Estimate the capacitor peak voltage before ripple.

Show solution
Vpeak = 9 × 1.414 = 12.73 V
Vpeak,cap ≈ 12.73 − 1.4
≈ 11.33 V

Question 5

A 50 Hz supply uses a full-wave bridge rectifier. Find the ripple frequency.

Show solution
fripple = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz

18. Key Technical Takeaways

  • Transformer secondary ratings are normally expressed in RMS volts.
  • For a sinusoidal voltage, peak voltage is approximately 1.414 times RMS voltage.
  • A bridge rectifier uses the complete two-terminal secondary and has two diode drops in its conducting path.
  • A centre-tapped rectifier uses one half-secondary at a time and one diode drop in its conducting path.
  • Centre-tapped PIV is approximately twice the peak voltage of one half-secondary.
  • Transformer VA selection must account for rectifier current waveform and thermal limits.
  • Diode selection requires reverse-voltage, average-current, surge-current and thermal checks.
  • Capacitor-input filters can produce high charging-current pulses.
  • Verify the complete design through datasheets, calculations and safe measurements.
