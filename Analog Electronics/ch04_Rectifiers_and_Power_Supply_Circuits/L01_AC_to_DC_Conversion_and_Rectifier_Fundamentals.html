Chapter 4: Rectifiers and Power Supply Circuits

Understanding AC-to-DC conversion and the fundamental operation of rectifier circuits.

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

2. Understanding AC and DC

2.1 Alternating Current (AC)

Alternating current is an electric current that changes its magnitude and periodically reverses its direction with time.

A sinusoidal AC voltage can be represented mathematically as:

v(t) = Vm sin(ωt)

Where:

  • Vm = Peak voltage.
  • ω = Angular frequency in rad/s.
  • t = Time in seconds.

The angular frequency is related to frequency by:

ω = 2πf

2.2 Direct Current (DC)

Direct current flows in one direction. Its magnitude may be constant or vary with time, but its polarity does not periodically reverse.

Batteries, DC adapters and regulated power supplies provide DC voltage.

2.3 Difference Between AC and DC

Parameter AC DC
Direction Periodically reverses Remains in one direction

3. What Is Rectification?

Rectification is the process of converting alternating current (AC) into unidirectional current (pulsating DC) using semiconductor devices, mainly diodes.

Definition: A rectifier is an electronic circuit that converts an alternating input voltage into a unidirectional output voltage.

Rectifiers are important because many electronic circuits require DC power, while the electrical supply available from the mains is AC.

3.1 Basic Rectifier Circuit

A simple rectifier consists of an AC source, a diode and a load resistor.

The diode allows current during one polarity of the AC input and blocks current during the opposite polarity. The output therefore becomes unidirectional.

This is a conceptual illustration. The detailed half-wave rectifier circuit, diode operation and accurate waveforms are covered in Lesson 2.

4. Working Principle of a Rectifier

A semiconductor diode conducts when it is forward biased and blocks current when it is reverse biased, provided it operates within its specified ratings.

4.1 Positive Half-Cycle

During the positive half-cycle, the diode is forward biased in a suitable rectifier arrangement. Current flows through the load.

4.2 Negative Half-Cycle

During the negative half-cycle, the diode becomes reverse biased in a half-wave rectifier. It blocks current, so the load current is approximately zero.

4.3 Output

The resulting output contains voltage pulses of one polarity. This is called pulsating DC.

Important distinction: Rectification produces unidirectional pulsating DC. A filter is generally required to reduce the ripple, and a regulator may be required to maintain a stable DC output voltage.

5. Types of Rectifiers

Rectifiers are broadly classified according to how much of the AC input waveform is used.

5.1 Half-Wave Rectifier

A half-wave rectifier uses one diode to convert only one half-cycle of the AC input into output pulses.

  • Simple circuit configuration.
  • Uses one diode.
  • Higher ripple compared with full-wave rectification.
  • Lower rectification efficiency.

5.2 Full-Wave Centre-Tapped Rectifier

A full-wave centre-tapped rectifier uses two diodes and a centre-tapped transformer. Both half-cycles contribute to the output.

  • Uses two diodes.
  • Requires a centre-tapped transformer.
  • Produces output during both half-cycles.

5.3 Bridge Rectifier

A bridge rectifier uses four diodes arranged in a bridge configuration. It converts both half-cycles into unidirectional output without requiring a centre-tapped transformer.

  • Uses four diodes.
  • Does not require a centre-tapped transformer.
  • Two diodes conduct during each half-cycle.
  • Widely used in DC power supply circuits.

6. Rectifier in a DC Power Supply

A practical DC power supply generally contains several stages. The rectifier is one of the main stages.

Typical DC power supply sequence:

AC Input → Transformer → Rectifier → Filter → Voltage Regulator → DC Output

Stage Function
Transformer Changes AC voltage level and provides isolation where required.
Rectifier Converts AC into pulsating DC.
Filter Reduces AC ripple in the rectified output.
Voltage regulator Maintains a more stable DC output voltage.
Load Uses the supplied electrical power.

For example, an embedded system may require a regulated 5 V or 3.3 V DC supply. A rectifier and filter can form part of the power conversion stage when the source is AC.

7. Applications of Rectifiers

  • AC-to-DC adapters and chargers.
  • Battery charging systems.
  • Embedded system power supplies.
  • Industrial DC power supplies.
  • DC motor drive circuits.

  • Electronic instrumentation equipment.

8. Lesson Summary

  • AC periodically reverses its direction.
  • DC flows in one direction.
  • Rectification converts AC into unidirectional pulsating DC.
  • Diodes are the basic semiconductor devices used in rectifiers.
  • Half-wave rectifiers use one half-cycle.
  • Full-wave rectifiers use both half-cycles.
  • Filters reduce ripple in rectifier output.
  • Regulators help maintain a stable DC output voltage.

9. Interview Questions

Q1. What is a rectifier?

A rectifier is an electronic circuit that converts AC voltage into unidirectional pulsating DC voltage.

Q2. Which semiconductor device is commonly used in rectifiers?

A PN junction diode is commonly used because it conducts in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias.

Q3. What is pulsating DC?

Pulsating DC is a unidirectional voltage or current whose magnitude varies with time.

Q4. What is the main difference between half-wave and full-wave rectifiers?

A half-wave rectifier uses one half-cycle of the AC input, whereas a full-wave rectifier uses both half-cycles.

Q5. Why is a filter used after a rectifier?

A filter reduces the AC ripple component in the rectified output to provide smoother DC.

Q6. Why is a bridge rectifier widely used?

It uses both half-cycles of the input and does not require a centre-tapped transformer.

Q7. What is the ripple frequency of a full-wave rectifier?

It is twice the input AC frequency, or 2f.
