2. What is Alternating Current (AC)?

Alternating current is an electrical current that periodically changes its magnitude and direction. Its voltage polarity also reverses periodically.

A sinusoidal AC voltage can be represented as:

v(t) = Vm sin(ωt)

ω = 2πf
T = 1/f

Where:

  • Vm: Peak voltage.
  • ω: Angular frequency in rad/s.
  • f: Frequency in hertz (Hz).
  • T: Time period in seconds.

At 50 Hz, one complete cycle takes 20 ms. The waveform completes 50 cycles every second.

3. What is Direct Current (DC)?

Direct current flows in one direction. In an ideal DC supply, voltage remains constant with time.

3.1 Examples of DC Sources

  • Battery.
  • DC power supply.
  • Solar panel output (with changing operating conditions).
  • Rectifier followed by suitable filtering and regulation.

Most electronic systems need a DC supply for their internal semiconductor devices and integrated circuits.

4. Why is AC Converted into DC?

Electrical power distribution commonly uses AC. However, many electronic devices require DC voltage to operate. A rectifier converts AC into a unidirectional output.

4.1 Stages of a Basic Linear Power Supply

  1. Transformer: Changes AC voltage and provides isolation when an appropriately designed isolation transformer is used.
  2. Rectifier: Converts AC into pulsating unidirectional voltage.
  3. Filter: Reduces the AC ripple component.
  4. Voltage regulator: Maintains a more stable DC output under specified input and load conditions.
A rectifier alone does not produce perfectly smooth DC. Its output is generally pulsating DC. Filtering and regulation are separate functions.

5. What is Rectification?

Rectification is the process of converting an alternating voltage or current into a unidirectional voltage or current using semiconductor devices such as diodes.

5.1 Basic Rectifier Circuit

A diode conducts when it is forward biased and blocks current when reverse biased, subject to its real electrical characteristics and ratings.

5.2 Types of Rectifiers

7. Half-Wave Rectification

A half-wave rectifier uses one diode to allow one half-cycle of an AC waveform to pass through the load. The opposite half-cycle is blocked in the ideal model.

7.1 Positive Half-Wave Rectification

7.2 Working Principle

  • Positive input half-cycle: diode is forward biased and conducts.
  • Negative input half-cycle: diode is reverse biased and blocks.
  • The output contains separated positive pulses.

For an ideal half-wave rectifier with a sinusoidal input and resistive load:

VDC = Vm / π
Vrms = Vm / 2
Ripple frequency = f

8. Full-Wave Rectification

A full-wave rectifier converts both positive and negative input half-cycles into output pulses with the same polarity.

8.1 Full-Wave Output

8.2 Working Principle

  • During the positive half-cycle, one conducting path carries load current.
  • During the negative half-cycle, a different conducting path carries load current.
  • Load current flows in the same direction during both half-cycles.
VDC = 2Vm / π
Vrms = Vm / √2
Ripple frequency = 2f

The centre-tapped and bridge configurations both provide full-wave rectification. Their circuit structures and component voltage stresses differ.

10. Ripple in Rectifier Output

The output of an unfiltered rectifier is not constant DC. It contains a varying component called ripple.

Ripple is the periodic variation in the rectifier's DC output voltage. A filter reduces ripple by smoothing the output waveform.

RectifierRipple Frequency
Half-wavef
Full-wave2f

For a 50 Hz input:

Half-wave ripple frequency = 50 Hz
Full-wave ripple frequency = 100 Hz

This higher ripple frequency is one reason full-wave rectification is commonly preferred in DC power supplies.

13. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Experiment: Observation of AC and Rectified Waveforms

Objective: To observe AC, half-wave and full-wave rectified waveforms.

Equipment:

  • Low-voltage isolated AC source.
  • Suitable rectifier diodes.
  • Resistive load.
  • Breadboard or suitable circuit board.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope with an appropriate safe measurement arrangement.

Procedure:

  1. Observe the AC input waveform.
  2. Construct a low-voltage half-wave rectifier.
  3. Observe the output waveform.
  4. Construct a suitable full-wave rectifier.
  5. Compare the output waveforms.
  6. Measure ripple frequency.
  7. Record average and RMS values using suitable instruments or calculations.

Expected Observations

MeasurementExpected Result
AC inputPositive and negative sinusoidal cycles
Half-wave outputOne positive pulse per input cycle
Full-wave outputTwo positive pulses per input cycle
50 Hz half-wave ripple50 Hz
50 Hz full-wave ripple100 Hz
Use only a suitable low-voltage isolated AC source for breadboard experiments. Never connect a breadboard rectifier directly to household mains. Use appropriate isolation, fusing, enclosures and safe measurement practices for professional power-electronics testing.

14. Common Practical Mistakes

  • Assuming rectifier output is smooth DC without a filter.
  • Confusing average output voltage with RMS voltage.
  • Ignoring diode forward-voltage drops.
  • Using an incorrectly rated diode.
  • Reversing the diode polarity.
  • Confusing rectifier ripple frequency with AC input frequency.
  • Assuming a bridge rectifier has only one diode drop.
  • Ignoring transformer current and voltage ratings.

15. Placement Interview Questions

Q1. What is rectification?

Rectification is the conversion of AC into unidirectional output using semiconductor devices such as diodes.

Q2. Why do electronic circuits require DC?

Most electronic components and integrated circuits require specified DC supply rails for their operation.

Q3. What is a rectifier?

A circuit that converts alternating input voltage into a unidirectional output voltage.

Q4. What is pulsating DC?

A unidirectional voltage that varies with time rather than remaining constant.

Q5. What is the difference between half-wave and full-wave rectification?

Half-wave uses one input half-cycle, while full-wave uses both half-cycles.

Q6. What is ripple?

The periodic AC variation present in the output of a rectifier or DC power supply.

Q7. Why is a filter used after a rectifier?

To reduce output ripple and obtain a smoother DC voltage.

Q8. What is the ripple frequency of a full-wave rectifier?

It is twice the input AC frequency.

Q9. What is the role of a transformer?

It can change AC voltage and provide galvanic isolation when designed as an isolation transformer.

Q10. Does a rectifier regulate voltage?

No. Rectification converts AC to pulsating DC. Voltage regulation is a separate function.

Q11. Which rectifier uses a centre-tapped transformer?

The centre-tapped full-wave rectifier.

Q12. How many diodes are used in a bridge rectifier?

Four diodes.

Q13. Why is full-wave rectification generally preferred?

It uses both input half-cycles, has higher ideal average output for the same peak voltage and produces a higher ripple frequency.

Q14. What is the time period of a 50 Hz supply?

20 milliseconds.

Q15. What are the four main stages of a linear DC power supply?

Transformer, rectifier, filter and voltage regulator.

17. Lecture Summary

  • AC periodically changes magnitude and direction.
  • DC flows in one direction.
  • Rectification converts AC into unidirectional voltage.
  • A half-wave rectifier uses one half-cycle.
  • A full-wave rectifier uses both half-cycles.
  • Rectifier output contains ripple.
  • Filters reduce ripple.
  • Voltage regulators provide a more stable output.
