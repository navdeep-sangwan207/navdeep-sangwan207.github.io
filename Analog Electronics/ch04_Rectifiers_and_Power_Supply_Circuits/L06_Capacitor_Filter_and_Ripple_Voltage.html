2. Why Is a Capacitor Filter Required?

A rectifier converts AC into pulsating DC. Although the output current flows in one direction, its magnitude is not constant. Many electronic circuits require a relatively stable DC voltage.

A capacitor connected in parallel with the load stores electrical energy when the rectifier voltage rises. It releases this energy when the rectifier voltage falls. This reduces the variation in output voltage.

Important: A capacitor filter reduces ripple voltage. It does not produce perfectly constant DC. The remaining AC variation is called ripple.

3. Capacitor Filter Circuit

The capacitor is connected across the load resistor, not in series with it. For a positive DC output, the positive terminal of the electrolytic capacitor connects to the positive output rail.

This is a functional block-style circuit illustration. In a detailed bridge schematic, four individual diodes form the bridge. The capacitor's positive terminal must connect to the positive DC rail and its negative terminal to the return rail.

Basic operation

  1. When rectifier voltage rises above capacitor voltage, the diodes conduct and the capacitor charges.
  2. Near the waveform peak, the capacitor charges to approximately the rectifier peak voltage.
  3. When rectifier voltage falls below capacitor voltage, the rectifier diodes stop conducting.
  4. The capacitor discharges through the load resistor.
  5. At the next rectified peak, the capacitor recharges.

4. Capacitor Charging and Discharging

Charging

The capacitor stores charge as its voltage increases.

Q = C × V

Where:

  • Q = charge in coulombs
  • C = capacitance in farads
  • V = capacitor voltage in volts

Discharging

When the rectifier voltage decreases, the capacitor supplies current to the load.

V(t) = V₀e−t/RC

The voltage decreases exponentially for a resistor-capacitor discharge circuit.

RC time constant

τ = R × C

The time constant determines how quickly a capacitor discharges through a resistance.

  • Large capacitance → slower discharge.
  • Large load resistance → lower load current and slower discharge.
  • Small load resistance → higher load current and faster discharge.
In a practical rectifier filter, the capacitor does not simply discharge for the entire cycle. It is periodically recharged near the rectified voltage peaks.

5. Ripple Voltage

Ripple voltage is the residual periodic variation present in the DC output after filtering. For a capacitor-input filter, the output voltage rises during recharge and falls between recharge peaks.

The peak-to-peak ripple voltage is the difference between the maximum and minimum output voltage during a ripple period.

Vr(pp) = Vmax − Vmin

Ripple frequency

RectifierRipple frequencyRecharge interval
Half-wavef1/f
Full-wave centre-tapped2f1/(2f)
Bridge2f1/(2f)

For a 50 Hz AC supply, the approximate ripple frequency is 50 Hz for a half-wave rectifier and 100 Hz for a full-wave rectifier.

6. Ripple Voltage Derivation

Assume the capacitor discharges approximately linearly between recharge peaks. This approximation is suitable when ripple is small compared with the DC output voltage.

Q = I × t
Q = C × ΔV
C × Vr(pp) ≈ IL × Δt
Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fripple × C)

Half-wave rectifier

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (f × C)

Full-wave rectifier

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (2f × C)

Where:

  • IL = approximate DC load current in amperes.
  • f = AC input frequency in hertz.
  • C = filter capacitance in farads.
  • fripple = rectified ripple frequency.

RMS ripple voltage

For small, approximately triangular ripple:

Vr(rms) ≈ Vr(pp) / (2√3)

Approximate DC output voltage

VDC ≈ Vpeak,cap − Vr(pp)/2

For a bridge rectifier:

Vpeak,cap ≈ √2 × Vsec,rms − 2VF

For a centre-tapped full-wave rectifier, use the RMS voltage of one half of the secondary winding and subtract one conducting diode's forward drop.

Model limitations: These are engineering approximations. Actual ripple depends on transformer winding resistance, diode conduction angle, source impedance, capacitor ESR, load characteristics, mains frequency and capacitor tolerance. The equations assume a sufficiently large capacitor and nearly constant load current.

7. Worked Numerical Example

Example 1: Full-wave capacitor filter

A full-wave bridge rectifier operates at 50 Hz. The load current is 100 mA and the filter capacitor is 1000 µF. Calculate the approximate peak-to-peak ripple voltage.

Given:

  • IL = 100 mA = 0.1 A
  • f = 50 Hz
  • C = 1000 µF = 0.001 F
  • fripple = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz
Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fripple × C)
= 0.1 / (100 × 0.001)
= 1 V
Answer: Approximate peak-to-peak ripple voltage = 1 V.

Example 2: Increase capacitance to 2200 µF

C = 2200 µF = 0.0022 F
Vr(pp) ≈ 0.1 / (100 × 0.0022)
≈ 0.455 V

Increasing capacitance from 1000 µF to 2200 µF reduces the approximate ripple voltage from 1 V to 0.455 V, assuming the same load and frequency.

Example 3: Calculate required capacitance

Find the approximate capacitor required for a full-wave 50 Hz supply delivering 200 mA with a maximum ripple of 0.5 V peak-to-peak.

C ≈ IL / (fripple × Vr(pp))
= 0.2 / (100 × 0.5)
= 0.004 F
= 4000 µF

A practical design may select a standard value above this estimate, such as 4700 µF, after checking voltage rating, ripple-current rating, tolerance, inrush current and thermal conditions.

10. Capacitor Selection for Practical Power Supplies

1. Capacitance value

Choose capacitance according to load current, ripple frequency and permitted ripple voltage.

C ≈ IL / (fripple × Vr(pp))

2. Voltage rating

The capacitor voltage rating must be higher than the maximum expected DC voltage, including high-line and no-load conditions. Allow suitable engineering margin and follow the manufacturer's derating recommendations.

3. Ripple-current rating

The capacitor must tolerate the AC ripple current flowing through it. Excessive ripple current causes internal heating and can shorten capacitor life.

4. ESR — Equivalent Series Resistance

ESR is the effective internal series resistance of a capacitor. It contributes to heating and can produce additional ripple voltage.

PESR ≈ Iripple,rms2 × ESR

5. Temperature and lifetime

Electrolytic capacitor life is strongly influenced by operating temperature and ripple current. Select a suitable temperature and lifetime rating for the application.

6. Polarity

Aluminium electrolytic capacitors are normally polarised. Reversing the polarity can cause excessive leakage, heating, venting or failure.

11. Capacitor Energy and Bleeder Resistor

A filter capacitor can retain a dangerous charge even after the power supply is switched off. A bleeder resistor provides a discharge path.

Stored energy

E = ½CV²

Discharge through a bleeder resistor

V(t) = V₀e−t/(RbC)
τ = RbC

For an ideal RC discharge, after one time constant the capacitor voltage falls to approximately 36.8% of its initial value. After five time constants, it falls to below 1%.

A bleeder resistor must be selected for suitable resistance, continuous power dissipation, voltage rating and safety. Do not assume that a bleeder resistor has discharged a capacitor; verify voltage with an appropriate meter before handling the circuit.

13. Practical Laboratory Experiments

Experiment 1: Observe capacitor charging and discharging

Objective: Observe how a capacitor changes the rectifier output waveform.

Equipment: Isolated low-voltage AC source, bridge rectifier, electrolytic capacitor, load resistor, oscilloscope and multimeter.

  1. Build a low-voltage bridge rectifier without a filter capacitor.
  2. Observe the pulsating DC waveform across the load.
  3. Switch off the source and safely discharge the circuit.
  4. Connect a correctly rated capacitor in parallel with the load, observing polarity.
  5. Power the circuit and observe the filtered waveform.
  6. Record the maximum and minimum output voltage.

Expected observation: The capacitor reduces the voltage variation between successive rectified peaks.

Experiment 2: Effect of capacitance

  1. Use a fixed rectifier, input voltage and load.
  2. Measure ripple with a suitable capacitor.
  3. Repeat with a larger capacitor value, ensuring all voltage and current ratings are suitable.
  4. Compare the measured ripple voltages.

Expected observation: Increasing capacitance reduces ripple, approximately in inverse proportion to capacitance under the assumptions used in the ripple equation.

Experiment 3: Effect of load current

  1. Keep the rectifier and capacitor unchanged.
  2. Increase load current using suitable load resistors.
  3. Measure ripple at each load condition.
  4. Compare the results with the theoretical relationship.

Expected observation: Higher load current increases ripple voltage.

Experiment 4: Half-wave versus full-wave filtering

  1. Build or simulate half-wave and full-wave rectifier circuits.
  2. Use the same input frequency, load current and capacitance.
  3. Measure ripple frequency and peak-to-peak ripple.
  4. Compare the two outputs.

Expected observation: A full-wave rectifier recharges the capacitor twice per input cycle, producing a higher ripple frequency and lower ripple for the same load current and capacitance under comparable conditions.

Laboratory safety: Use an isolated, low-voltage source for these experiments. Never connect an oscilloscope ground clip directly to a live mains circuit. Discharge capacitors safely and verify their voltage before touching the circuit.

15. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

  1. What is a capacitor filter?
    A capacitor connected across the rectifier load to reduce output ripple.
  2. Why is the capacitor connected in parallel with the load?
    It must maintain the load voltage while supplying current between rectifier peaks.
  3. How does a capacitor filter work?
    It charges near rectified voltage peaks and discharges through the load between peaks.
  4. What is ripple voltage?
    The residual periodic AC variation present in the DC output.
  5. What is peak-to-peak ripple voltage?
    The difference between maximum and minimum output voltage during a ripple period.
  6. What is the ripple frequency of a half-wave rectifier?
    It is equal to the input AC frequency.
  7. What is the ripple frequency of a full-wave rectifier?
    It is twice the input AC frequency.
  8. What happens when capacitance increases?
    Ripple voltage decreases for the same load current and frequency.
  9. What happens when load current increases?
    The capacitor discharges more quickly and ripple increases.
  10. Why does a full-wave rectifier need less capacitance for the same ripple?
    Its ripple frequency is twice the input frequency, reducing the time between recharge peaks.
  11. What is the approximate full-wave ripple equation?
    Vr(pp) ≈ IL/(2fC).
  12. What is the approximate half-wave ripple equation?
    Vr(pp) ≈ IL/(fC).
  13. What is capacitor ESR?
    Equivalent Series Resistance, which contributes to losses and internal heating.
  14. Why is ripple-current rating important?
    Excessive ripple current heats the capacitor and reduces its life.
  15. What is a bleeder resistor?
    A resistor connected to discharge a capacitor after power is removed.
  16. What happens if an electrolytic capacitor is connected in reverse?
    It may overheat, leak, vent or fail.
  17. Why can a large filter capacitor increase startup current?
    It initially draws charging current to reach its operating voltage.
  18. Does a capacitor filter eliminate ripple completely?
    No. It reduces ripple but normally does not eliminate it.
  19. Why can a capacitor become hot?
    Possible causes include excessive ripple current, high ESR, overvoltage or reverse polarity.
  20. How can ripple be measured?
    Using an oscilloscope with appropriate coupling, probe configuration and circuit grounding.

16. Numerical Practice

Question 1

A full-wave rectifier operates at 50 Hz and supplies 150 mA to a load. Calculate the approximate ripple voltage with a 1500 µF capacitor.

Show solution
fripple = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz
C = 1500 × 10−6 F
Vr(pp) = 0.15/(100 × 0.0015)
= 1 V

Question 2

A half-wave rectifier supplies 50 mA at 50 Hz. Find the capacitance required for 1 V peak-to-peak ripple.

Show solution
C = I/(f × Vr(pp))
= 0.05/(50 × 1)
= 0.001 F = 1000 µF

Question 3

A full-wave supply has 0.8 V peak-to-peak ripple. Estimate its RMS ripple voltage using the triangular approximation.

Show solution
Vr(rms) ≈ 0.8/(2√3)
≈ 0.231 V

Question 4

A 2200 µF capacitor is charged to 20 V. Calculate its stored energy.

Show solution
E = ½CV²
= 0.5 × 0.0022 × 20²
= 0.44 J

17. Key Technical Takeaways

  • A capacitor filter is connected across the rectifier load.
  • The capacitor charges near voltage peaks and discharges through the load between peaks.
  • Full-wave rectification produces ripple at twice the AC input frequency.
  • Ripple decreases as capacitance or ripple frequency increases.
  • Ripple increases when load current increases.
  • Capacitor selection must consider voltage, ESR, ripple current, temperature, polarity and lifetime.
  • Large capacitors can produce significant startup inrush current.
  • Always verify capacitor discharge before handling a circuit.
