1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Design the main stages of a regulated DC power supply.
  • Calculate transformer secondary voltage and VA rating.
  • Select rectifier diodes using voltage, current and surge ratings.
  • Calculate filter capacitance from load current and ripple limits.
  • Check regulator input headroom and power dissipation.
  • Estimate junction temperature and determine the need for a heatsink.
  • Choose suitable protection components and verify a completed design.

2. DC Power Supply: Complete System

A DC power supply converts an AC source into a stable DC voltage suitable for an electronic load. A conventional linear regulated supply contains several functional stages.

Function of each stage

StageFunctionImportant design parameter
TransformerProvides isolation and changes AC voltage.Secondary RMS voltage, VA rating, regulation.
RectifierConverts AC into unidirectional pulsating voltage.PIV/VRRM, forward current, surge current.
FilterReduces the AC ripple component.Capacitance, ESR, ripple-current rating.
RegulatorMaintains the required DC output.Dropout, input limit, output current, thermal dissipation.
ProtectionProtects against abnormal electrical conditions.Fuse, current limit, thermal and voltage ratings.

3. Capture the Power Supply Requirements

Do not select components before defining the electrical requirements. A design specification should include:

  • Required regulated output voltage \(V_{out}\).
  • Maximum continuous load current \(I_{out}\).
  • Permitted output ripple and noise.
  • Output voltage tolerance and load regulation.
  • Input AC voltage and frequency range.
  • Isolation and safety requirements.
  • Operating temperature and expected duty cycle.
  • Overcurrent, short-circuit and overvoltage requirements.
Design example used in this lecture: A 12 V regulated DC supply delivering 500 mA, supplied by an isolated 15 V RMS secondary transformer at 50 Hz, using a bridge rectifier and capacitor-input filter. The example is illustrative; component ratings must be verified against actual datasheets and worst-case conditions.

4. Transformer Selection

The transformer secondary voltage determines the approximate peak voltage available to the rectifier and filter.

Vpeak = √2 × Vsecondary RMS

For a 15 V RMS secondary:

Vpeak = 1.414 × 15 = 21.21 V

For a bridge rectifier, two diodes conduct during each half-cycle. If each conducting diode has a forward drop of approximately 0.8 V:

Vpeak,filter ≈ 21.21 − (2 × 0.8) = 19.61 V

This is an approximate capacitor charging peak, not a guaranteed minimum DC voltage. The actual value depends on transformer regulation, line voltage, diode current, winding resistance and load.

Transformer VA rating

VA = Vsecondary RMS × Isecondary RMS

For a capacitor-input rectifier, secondary current is pulsed. Its RMS value can be significantly greater than the DC load current. Therefore, do not automatically use \(I_{secondary}=I_{DC}\).

A preliminary transformer estimate can use a topology- and design-dependent current factor, but the final selection must be checked against transformer manufacturer guidance, winding RMS current, temperature rise and the actual rectifier/filter load.

Transformer selection checklist

  • Choose an isolated transformer with the required secondary voltage.
  • Verify secondary RMS current under the intended rectifier and filter load.
  • Check regulation and no-load secondary voltage.
  • Check VA rating and temperature rise.
  • Check insulation, isolation and applicable safety requirements.

5. Bridge Rectifier Selection

A bridge rectifier uses four diodes. Two diodes conduct in each half-cycle, keeping load current in the same direction.

Important diode parameters

ParameterMeaningSelection consideration
VRRMMaximum repetitive reverse voltageMust exceed worst-case reverse stress with margin.
IF(AV)Average forward current ratingCheck against the actual conduction waveform and thermal conditions.
IFSMNon-repetitive surge currentImportant during capacitor charging and startup.
VFForward voltage dropAffects output voltage and diode heating.
RθThermal resistanceUsed to estimate junction temperature.

For an ideal bridge rectifier without a capacitor filter, the PIV of each diode is approximately the peak secondary voltage. With a capacitor-input filter, verify reverse stress under actual circuit conditions and transients.

6. Capacitor Filter Design

The filter capacitor charges near the peaks of the rectified waveform and discharges through the load between charging peaks. This reduces output ripple.

Vr(pp) ≈ Iload / (fripple × C)
C ≈ Iload / (fripple × Vr(pp))

For a full-wave rectifier:

fripple = 2 × fline

For 50 Hz mains, the ripple frequency is 100 Hz.

Worked capacitor calculation

Given:

  • Load current = 0.5 A
  • Ripple frequency = 100 Hz
  • Maximum desired ripple = 1.1 V peak-to-peak
C = 0.5 / (100 × 1.1)
C ≈ 0.004545 F = 4545 µF

A nominal 4700 µF capacitor is a reasonable starting candidate, subject to tolerance, ripple-current rating, ESR, voltage rating and the complete design's minimum input voltage.

The ripple formula is an approximation for a capacitor-input filter with relatively small ripple and approximately constant load current. Real ripple depends on source impedance, transformer regulation, diode conduction angle, capacitor ESR and load.

Capacitor voltage rating

The capacitor must tolerate the highest expected charging voltage, including high-line and no-load conditions. Do not select its voltage rating using only the nominal loaded output voltage. Check capacitor ripple-current rating and temperature-life specifications as well.

7. Regulator Selection and Headroom

A regulator maintains the output voltage only while its input voltage, current and thermal conditions remain within its operating limits.

Vin,min ≥ Vout + Vdropout

For a conventional linear regulator, the filtered input must remain above the required output plus the regulator's minimum headroom, including worst-case ripple troughs and input variation.

Minimum input voltage estimate

Vin,min ≈ Vpeak,filter − Vr(pp)

Using the example:

  • Capacitor charging peak ≈ 19.61 V
  • Ripple ≈ 1.06 V peak-to-peak
Vin,min ≈ 19.61 − 1.06 = 18.55 V

This is above 12 V, but the regulator's maximum input rating, startup conditions, high-line input and thermal limits must also be checked.

Linear regulator power dissipation

Preg ≈ (Vin,avg − Vout) × Iout

If the regulator input averages approximately 19.1 V and the output is 12 V at 0.5 A:

Preg ≈ (19.1 − 12) × 0.5 = 3.55 W

This is substantial heat for a small package. A suitable heatsink and thermal verification are likely necessary.

Junction temperature estimation

Tj ≈ Ta + P × RθJA

For a heatsink, use the full thermal path:

Tj = Ta + P(RθJC + RθCS + RθSA)

Where:

  • \(R_{\theta JC}\): junction-to-case thermal resistance.
  • \(R_{\theta CS}\): case-to-heatsink thermal resistance.
  • \(R_{\theta SA}\): heatsink-to-ambient thermal resistance.
Never assume that a regulator can safely dissipate several watts without checking its datasheet, package, safe operating area, thermal shutdown behavior and heatsink requirements.

8. Protection Circuit Design

ProtectionPurposeTypical implementation
Input fuseProtects against excessive input current and faults.Correctly rated fuse on the appropriate input conductor.
Secondary fuseProtects transformer secondary wiring.Fuse selected for expected RMS and startup current.
Reverse polarityPrevents damage from reversed DC connections.Series diode, MOSFET protection or suitable bridge arrangement.
OvervoltageLimits abnormal output voltage.TVS, crowbar or dedicated protection circuit.
OvercurrentLimits excessive load current.Current limiting or electronic protection.
ThermalProtects against excessive temperature.Thermal shutdown or external thermal protection.
Bleeder resistorDischarges stored capacitor energy after shutdown.Resistor across the DC capacitor.

Bleeder resistor calculation

Capacitor discharge is approximately exponential through a resistor:

V(t) = V0 × e−t/(RC)
τ = R × C

The bleeder resistor must be rated for its continuous power loss while the supply is operating:

PR = V² / R

Also check resistor voltage rating, power derating and the required discharge time.

11. Linear vs Switching Power Supply

ParameterLinear regulator supplySwitch-mode supply (SMPS)
PrinciplePass element operates in its linear region.Power device switches at high frequency.
EfficiencyOften lower when input-output voltage difference is large.Often higher, depending on topology and operating point.
NoiseGenerally low switching noise.Switching ripple and EMI require design attention.
HeatCan dissipate significant heat.Usually lower conversion loss for suitable designs.
ComplexityRelatively simple.Control, magnetics, layout and EMI design are more involved.
ApplicationsLow-noise analog circuits, simple regulated rails.Computers, chargers, embedded systems and efficient converters.

12. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

To observe the output of a bridge rectifier and capacitor filter and evaluate the effect of load current and capacitance on ripple voltage.

Equipment

  • Isolated low-voltage AC transformer.
  • Bridge rectifier or four suitably rated diodes.
  • Electrolytic filter capacitor with appropriate voltage and ripple-current ratings.
  • Resistive load, DMM and oscilloscope.
  • Fuse and correctly rated connecting wires.

Procedure

  1. Verify the transformer secondary voltage before connecting the rectifier.
  2. Connect the bridge rectifier and confirm its positive and negative DC terminals.
  3. Connect the filter capacitor with correct polarity across the DC output.
  4. Connect a suitable resistive load.
  5. Measure the DC output using a DMM.
  6. Observe the rectified and filtered waveforms using an oscilloscope.
  7. Measure ripple peak-to-peak voltage.
  8. Increase the load current within the component ratings and observe ripple changes.
  9. Repeat with a different capacitor value and compare the results.
  10. Record theoretical and measured values and explain the differences.

Observation table

Load currentCapacitanceCalculated rippleMeasured rippleDifference
100 mA1000 µF
250 mA2200 µF
500 mA4700 µF
Electrical safety: Use an isolated, suitably rated low-voltage AC source for this laboratory exercise. Never connect an oscilloscope ground clip to an unknown mains-referenced circuit. Do not probe exposed mains wiring. Capacitors can retain hazardous energy after power is removed; discharge them through an appropriately rated resistor and verify the voltage before handling.

13. Troubleshooting and Design Verification

Observed problemPossible causeVerification
Output voltage too lowInsufficient transformer voltage, excessive diode drop, overload, excessive ripple or regulator dropout.Measure transformer secondary, filter minimum and regulator input.
Excessive rippleLow capacitance, high ESR, excessive load or faulty diode.Measure ripple frequency and peak-to-peak voltage.
Regulator overheatingHigh input-output difference, high load current or inadequate heatsinking.Calculate dissipation and estimate junction temperature.
Output unstableInsufficient input headroom, unsuitable regulator capacitors or oscillation.Check datasheet layout and capacitor requirements.
Fuse repeatedly blowsShort circuit, incorrect fuse selection, excessive inrush or component failure.Check wiring, bridge, capacitor and transformer load.
Capacitor becomes hotExcessive ripple current, high ESR, wrong polarity or overvoltage.Check capacitor rating, polarity and ripple-current specification.

Final design verification checklist

  • Transformer secondary voltage and RMS current verified.
  • Transformer VA and temperature rise checked.
  • Rectifier VRRM, forward current and surge ratings checked.
  • Capacitor voltage, capacitance, ESR and ripple-current ratings checked.
  • Regulator minimum and maximum input voltage verified.
  • Regulator output current and thermal dissipation verified.
  • Heatsink and junction-temperature limits checked.
  • Fuse, current limiting and output protection checked.
  • Worst-case input voltage, load and ambient temperature considered.
  • Measured output voltage, ripple and temperature recorded.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

  1. What is a regulated DC power supply?
    A circuit that converts an input source into a DC output maintained within specified limits despite changes in input or load.
  2. Why is a transformer used?
    To provide electrical isolation and adjust the AC voltage to a suitable level.
  3. Why is a bridge rectifier popular?
    It provides full-wave rectification without requiring a centre-tapped secondary.
  4. How many diodes conduct in a bridge during each half-cycle?
    Two.
  5. What is ripple voltage?
    The residual periodic AC variation present in a DC output.
  6. Why does a full-wave rectifier produce ripple at twice the line frequency?
    Both AC half-cycles produce output pulses.
  7. What is the purpose of the filter capacitor?
    It stores charge near waveform peaks and supplies the load between peaks.
  8. How does increasing capacitance affect ripple?
    It generally reduces ripple for the same load and ripple frequency.
  9. What happens when load current increases?
    The capacitor discharges faster, so ripple generally increases.
  10. What is regulator dropout voltage?
    The minimum input-to-output voltage difference required for regulation.
  11. Why can a regulator overheat?
    It dissipates power due to the voltage difference between its input and output while supplying current.
  12. How is regulator dissipation estimated?
    Approximately \((V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}\) for a linear regulator.
  13. Why is a heatsink used?
    To reduce thermal resistance from the regulator case to ambient air.
  14. What is transformer VA rating?
    The product of its RMS voltage and RMS current rating, expressed in volt-amperes.
  15. Why is secondary RMS current greater than DC load current in some rectifier supplies?
    Capacitor-input rectifiers draw short, high-amplitude charging pulses.
  16. What is the function of a bleeder resistor?
    It provides a discharge path for stored capacitor energy.
  17. What is the difference between a linear regulator and an SMPS?
    A linear regulator controls a pass element continuously; an SMPS controls energy transfer using high-frequency switching.
  18. What is PSRR?
    Power Supply Rejection Ratio, a measure of how effectively a circuit rejects supply-voltage variations.
  19. Why must capacitor polarity be checked?
    Reverse voltage can damage a polarized electrolytic capacitor and may cause overheating or failure.
  20. Why must the maximum regulator input voltage be checked?
    The input can rise at high line or light load, potentially exceeding the regulator's absolute maximum rating.

15. Numerical Practice

Problem 1: Transformer peak voltage

A transformer has a 12 V RMS secondary. Calculate its ideal peak voltage.

Vpeak = 1.414 × 12 = 16.97 V

Problem 2: Full-wave ripple frequency

A full-wave rectifier is supplied by 50 Hz AC.

fripple = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz

Problem 3: Filter capacitor

A full-wave supply delivers 200 mA at 100 Hz. Desired ripple is 0.5 V peak-to-peak.

C = 0.2 / (100 × 0.5) = 0.004 F = 4000 µF

A practical nominal value must be selected after checking capacitor tolerance, ripple current and voltage rating.

Problem 4: Regulator power dissipation

A linear regulator receives 15 V and delivers 5 V at 300 mA.

P = (15 − 5) × 0.3 = 3 W

Problem 5: Bleeder resistor power

A 1 kΩ resistor is connected across a 20 V DC capacitor bank.

P = V²/R = 20²/1000 = 0.4 W

Choose a resistor with appropriate power and voltage derating; also verify its discharge-time performance.

16. Key Design Takeaways

  • Start with a clear output-voltage, current and ripple specification.
  • Transformer selection must consider RMS winding current, not only DC load current.
  • Rectifier selection requires voltage, current, surge and thermal checks.
  • Capacitor sizing depends on load current, ripple frequency and allowed ripple.
  • Regulator input must remain above the required headroom at the ripple trough.
  • Linear regulator thermal dissipation can be a major design constraint.
  • Protection and worst-case verification are part of the design, not optional extras.
