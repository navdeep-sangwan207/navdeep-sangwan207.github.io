1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Diagnose common faults in transformer-based DC power supplies.
  • Identify open-circuit, short-circuit and leaky rectifier diodes.
  • Recognize capacitor failure through ripple and voltage measurements.
  • Test transformer secondary voltage and winding continuity.
  • Diagnose regulator dropout, overheating and unstable output.
  • Use a digital multimeter and oscilloscope to isolate faults.
  • Follow a systematic troubleshooting procedure instead of replacing components randomly.

2. Troubleshooting a DC Power Supply

A practical DC power supply contains several stages. A fault in any stage can affect the final output.

Fundamental troubleshooting rule

Start at the input and verify each stage in sequence. Compare measured values with expected values, isolate the stage where the signal becomes abnormal, and then test individual components.

4. Fault Analysis: Transformer

The transformer provides isolation and the required AC voltage to the rectifier.

Common transformer faults

  • Primary winding open circuit.
  • Secondary winding open circuit.
  • Shorted turns in a winding.
  • Insulation breakdown between windings.
  • Excessive loading and overheating.
  • Loose terminals or damaged connecting wires.

Testing procedure

  1. Disconnect the transformer from the power supply circuit.
  2. Inspect the body, terminals, insulation and wiring.
  3. Use a multimeter to check winding continuity with power removed.
  4. Check the secondary AC voltage using an appropriately rated meter and safe isolated test setup.
  5. Compare the measured voltage with the transformer specification.
  6. Check whether the voltage falls excessively under the intended load.
A normal resistance reading does not prove that a transformer is healthy. Shorted turns can be difficult to identify with a basic multimeter. A proper loaded-voltage test, current measurement or specialized transformer test may be required.

Transformer fault indications

MeasurementPossible interpretation
Open winding continuity testBroken wire, open winding or internal thermal fuse.
Secondary voltage absentInput fault, open winding, internal protection or wiring issue.
Secondary voltage collapses under loadOverload, shorted turns, poor connection or inadequate transformer rating.
Excessive temperature or hummingOverload, mechanical vibration, abnormal magnetic operation or winding fault.

5. Fault Analysis: Rectifier Diodes

A faulty diode can interrupt rectification, cause half-wave operation, increase ripple or create a short circuit across the transformer secondary.

Diode testing using a digital multimeter

  1. Disconnect power from the circuit.
  2. Discharge the filter capacitor safely and verify its voltage.
  3. Isolate at least one diode terminal if parallel circuit paths affect the reading.
  4. Select diode-test mode on the multimeter.
  5. Connect the red probe to the anode and black probe to the cathode.
  6. Record the forward voltage reading.
  7. Reverse the probes and record the reverse reading.
Observed resultLikely interpretation
Forward drop and reverse OLConsistent with a healthy diode, subject to diode type.
Near zero in both directionsPossible shorted diode.
OL in both directionsPossible open diode or poor probe contact.
Unexpected conduction in reversePossible leakage, wrong diode type or parallel circuit path.
A silicon rectifier diode often shows a forward drop in the approximate range of 0.5–0.8 V at the multimeter's test current. Schottky and other diode types can differ. In-circuit measurements can be misleading.

Effect of diode faults

  • Open diode in a bridge: one half-cycle may be lost, producing abnormal output and increased ripple.
  • Shorted diode: can cause excessive current, fuse operation and transformer heating.
  • Leaky diode: may cause abnormal reverse current, heating and poor rectification.
  • Wrong diode orientation: may prevent correct output or create a damaging short-circuit path.

6. Fault Analysis: Filter Capacitor

The filter capacitor stores energy and reduces the ripple voltage after rectification.

Common capacitor faults

FaultEffect on power supply
Capacitance reducedHigher ripple and lower minimum DC voltage.
High ESRGreater ripple under load and additional heating.
Open capacitorOutput becomes strongly pulsating DC.
Shorted capacitorMay short the rectifier output and blow the fuse.
Reverse polarityCan cause heating, leakage, venting and failure.
Leakage current increasedCan cause voltage loss, heating and poor regulation.

Testing methods

  • Visual inspection for bulging, leakage, corrosion or vent damage.
  • Capacitance measurement after safe discharge and isolation.
  • ESR measurement using an appropriate ESR meter.
  • Oscilloscope measurement of ripple under a known load.
  • Temperature observation during a controlled test.
Vr(pp) ≈ Iload / (fripple × C)

If capacitance decreases while load current and ripple frequency remain constant, ripple generally increases.

7. Fault Analysis: Voltage Regulator

The regulator maintains the required output voltage while operating within its input-voltage, current and thermal limits.

Common regulator faults

SymptomPossible causeTest
No outputIncorrect pinout, damaged regulator or missing inputCheck pin configuration and input voltage.
Low outputInsufficient headroom, overload or current limitingMeasure input trough and output current.
High outputFailed regulator or feedback network faultCheck regulator and feedback connections.
Output oscillationUnsuitable capacitors, layout or stability issueCheck datasheet capacitor and layout requirements.
OverheatingExcessive power dissipation or poor heatsinkingCalculate thermal dissipation.

Regulator power calculation

PD ≈ (Vin − Vout) × Iout

Example: A linear regulator receives 18 V, supplies 12 V and delivers 0.5 A.

PD = (18 − 12) × 0.5 = 3 W

The regulator must safely dissipate approximately 3 W under the stated operating condition. Check the actual datasheet thermal limits and heatsink requirements.

10. Step-by-Step Troubleshooting Procedure

  1. Confirm the complaint: Record the reported output voltage, load condition, temperature and operating behavior.
  2. Perform a visual inspection: Look for burnt components, damaged insulation, bulging capacitors and loose connectors.
  3. Check the input: Verify the input source and fuse rating.
  4. Check the transformer: Measure the secondary AC voltage under a suitable test condition.
  5. Check the rectifier: Measure the rectified DC and inspect the diode waveform.
  6. Check the filter: Measure capacitor voltage and ripple under load.
  7. Check the regulator: Verify input voltage, output voltage, load current and temperature.
  8. Check the load: Disconnect the load if appropriate and investigate a possible short or overload.
  9. Identify the root cause: Confirm the defective component or design limitation before replacement.
  10. Verify the repair: Repeat voltage, ripple, current and thermal tests.

11. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

To identify faults in a low-voltage rectifier and regulated DC power supply by measuring voltage, ripple and component behavior.

Equipment

  • Isolated low-voltage transformer.
  • Bridge rectifier or individual rectifier diodes.
  • Filter capacitor and resistive load.
  • Suitable regulator circuit.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope and correctly rated probes.
  • Current-limited source or suitable fuse protection.

Experiment A: Healthy supply measurements

  1. Inspect the circuit with power disconnected.
  2. Verify diode orientation and capacitor polarity.
  3. Apply power from an isolated low-voltage AC source.
  4. Measure transformer secondary RMS voltage.
  5. Measure the rectifier DC output.
  6. Measure filter capacitor DC voltage and ripple.
  7. Measure regulator output voltage under the specified load.
  8. Record regulator temperature after a controlled operating period.

Experiment B: Simulated fault analysis

Use instructor-prepared, current-limited fault conditions or simulation. Do not deliberately short a transformer secondary or capacitor.

  • Compare a healthy full-wave waveform with a half-wave-like waveform.
  • Compare ripple with different filter capacitor values.
  • Observe the effect of increasing load current within component ratings.
  • Study regulator dropout by safely reducing the input voltage in a controlled setup.
  • Record the measurement that best identifies each fault.

12. Worked Troubleshooting Examples

Example 1: Excessive ripple voltage

A full-wave rectifier operates at 50 Hz. The load current is 0.2 A and the filter capacitor is 1000 µF. Estimate ripple.

fripple = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz
Vr(pp) ≈ 0.2 / (100 × 0.001)
Vr(pp) ≈ 2 V

If the measured ripple is much higher, investigate capacitor degradation, ESR, excessive load and rectifier faults.

Example 2: Missing full-wave operation

A 50 Hz bridge rectifier should produce a dominant ripple repetition frequency of approximately 100 Hz after rectification. If the output instead resembles half-wave rectification with a 50 Hz repetition frequency, an open diode, bad connection or incorrect bridge wiring may be responsible.

Example 3: Regulator overheating

A linear regulator has 20 V input, 12 V output and supplies 0.4 A.

P = (20 − 12) × 0.4 = 3.2 W

Check the package thermal resistance, heatsink, ambient temperature and junction-temperature limit. If the thermal design is inadequate, consider a suitable switching pre-regulator or a different supply architecture.

13. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

  1. What is the first step in troubleshooting a DC power supply?
    Confirm the symptom and inspect the circuit before taking measurements.
  2. How do you check a transformer using a multimeter?
    Check winding continuity with power removed, then measure the isolated secondary AC voltage using a safe setup.
  3. How do you identify a shorted diode?
    A diode-test measurement may show very low voltage in both directions, after isolating the diode from parallel paths.
  4. What happens if one diode in a bridge rectifier becomes open?
    One rectification path may be lost, producing abnormal output and increased ripple.
  5. What happens if a bridge diode becomes shorted?
    It can create excessive current, cause fuse operation and potentially damage other components.
  6. How can you identify a weak filter capacitor?
    Check capacitance, ESR, visual condition and output ripple under load.
  7. Why is output ripple measured using an oscilloscope?
    It allows the periodic AC variation superimposed on the DC output to be observed and measured.
  8. What is the expected ripple frequency of a full-wave rectifier supplied by 50 Hz AC?
    100 Hz.
  9. What is the expected ripple frequency of a half-wave rectifier supplied by 50 Hz AC?
    50 Hz.
  10. Why can a regulator output become low?
    Insufficient input headroom, overload, current limiting or regulator failure.
  11. Why does a linear regulator become hot?
    It dissipates power approximately equal to its input-output voltage difference multiplied by load current.
  12. How can you check whether a regulator is in dropout?
    Measure the regulator input voltage at its minimum point and compare it with the required output plus dropout voltage.
  13. What causes a fuse to blow repeatedly?
    Short circuits, faulty rectifiers, damaged capacitors, excessive inrush or an incorrectly selected fuse.
  14. What is ESR in a capacitor?
    Equivalent Series Resistance, which contributes to voltage drop, ripple and heating.
  15. Can a capacitor have normal capacitance but still be faulty?
    Yes. High ESR, leakage or poor performance under ripple current can cause problems.
  16. Why should a capacitor be discharged before testing?
    It may retain stored electrical energy even after the power source is disconnected.
  17. Why is a transformer tested under load?
    To identify excessive voltage drop and performance problems that may not appear at no load.
  18. What is root-cause analysis?
    Finding the underlying reason for a failure rather than only replacing the visibly damaged component.
  19. What is the difference between preventive and corrective maintenance?
    Preventive maintenance aims to reduce the chance of failure; corrective maintenance restores equipment after a fault.
  20. What measurements should be recorded after a repair?
    Input voltage, secondary voltage, output DC voltage, ripple, load current and relevant component temperatures.

14. Numerical and Practical Assessment

  1. A full-wave rectifier is supplied by 60 Hz AC. Calculate the ripple frequency.
  2. A 2200 µF capacitor supplies a 100 mA load at 100 Hz. Estimate peak-to-peak ripple.
  3. A 15 V RMS transformer secondary feeds a bridge rectifier. Estimate the ideal secondary peak voltage.
  4. A regulator receives 18 V and provides 9 V at 250 mA. Calculate its approximate power dissipation.
  5. A 4700 µF capacitor is replaced by a 1000 µF capacitor. Explain the expected change in ripple.
  6. A supply shows 50 Hz ripple instead of the expected 100 Hz after a full-wave rectifier. Give two possible causes.
  7. A regulator output is low only at heavy load. List three possible causes.
  8. A bridge diode shows nearly zero voltage in both directions during an isolated diode test. What does this suggest?

Answer guide

  1. 120 Hz.
  2. \(V_{r(pp)} \approx 0.1/(100×0.0022) \approx 0.455\text{ V}\).
  3. \(V_{peak} \approx 15×1.414 \approx 21.21\text{ V}\).
  4. \(P \approx (18-9)×0.25 = 2.25\text{ W}\).
  5. Ripple generally increases because the capacitor stores less charge.
  6. Possible open diode, incorrect bridge wiring or loss of one rectification path.
  7. Insufficient transformer capacity, excessive ripple/input drop, regulator current limiting or thermal limiting.
  8. A shorted diode is likely; verify the result and inspect surrounding components.
