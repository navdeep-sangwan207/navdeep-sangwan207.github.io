Lesson 10: Practical DC Power Supply Design

Learn how to design a basic regulated DC power supply using a transformer, bridge rectifier, filter capacitor and voltage regulator.

1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Identify the stages of a practical DC power supply.
  • Understand transformer selection.
  • Calculate rectifier output voltage.
  • Select a filter capacitor using ripple specifications.
  • Understand voltage regulator requirements.
  • Estimate power dissipation in a linear regulator.
  • Understand practical testing and troubleshooting.

2. What Is a DC Power Supply?

A DC power supply converts electrical energy from an AC source into a DC voltage suitable for electronic circuits. It may also regulate the output voltage and provide protection against abnormal operating conditions.

Applications

  • Microcontroller development boards.
  • Embedded systems and electronic circuits.
  • Sensor and instrumentation circuits.
  • Audio amplifier circuits.
  • Industrial control systems.
  • Laboratory electronic equipment.
A practical power supply commonly contains a transformer, rectifier, filter and regulator. Some modern power supplies use switching converters instead of the traditional transformer-based linear arrangement.

3. Block Diagram of a DC Power Supply

Typical stages of a transformer-based regulated DC power supply.

Functions of each stage

Stage Function
Transformer Changes the AC voltage and provides isolation in a suitable transformer-based design.
Rectifier Converts AC into pulsating DC.
Filter Reduces ripple in the rectified output.
Voltage regulator Maintains a stable DC output voltage.
Load Uses the regulated DC power.

4. Step 1 – Transformer Selection

The transformer reduces the mains AC voltage to a lower AC voltage suitable for the rectifier and regulator.

Example

Suppose a power supply requires approximately 12 V DC at its output. A suitable transformer secondary might be rated at 12 V AC, subject to the required regulator headroom, ripple and load conditions.

Transformer RMS Voltage Ratio

VS / VP = NS / NP

VS = Secondary RMS voltage
VP = Primary RMS voltage
NS = Secondary turns
NP = Primary turns

Transformer selection factors

  • Required secondary RMS voltage.
  • Required output current.
  • Transformer VA rating.
  • Regulator input-voltage requirement.
  • Electrical isolation and safety requirements.
Transformer secondary voltage can rise at light load. Account for mains variation, transformer regulation, rectifier losses and capacitor ripple when selecting the regulator input voltage.

5. Step 2 – Bridge Rectifier Selection

A bridge rectifier uses four diodes to convert both half-cycles of AC into pulsating DC.

Conceptual bridge rectifier arrangement. For an actual circuit, follow the diode polarities and bridge package terminal markings carefully.

Peak output voltage

For a bridge rectifier, two diodes conduct during each half-cycle. Therefore, the approximate capacitor charging peak is:

VPEAK ≈ √2 × VSEC(RMS) − 2VF

Where VF is the forward voltage drop of one conducting diode.

Example

For a 12 V RMS transformer secondary and an assumed 0.7 V forward drop per diode:

VPEAK ≈ 1.414 × 12 − 2 × 0.7

VPEAK ≈ 16.97 − 1.4

VPEAK ≈ 15.57 V
This is an approximate peak under the assumed diode-drop model. Actual voltage depends on diode current, transformer regulation, mains voltage and capacitor charging pulses.

6. Step 3 – Filter Capacitor Selection

The capacitor is connected across the rectifier output. It charges near the waveform peaks and discharges into the load between peaks, reducing ripple.

Ripple voltage formula

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fr × C)

C ≈ IL / (fr × Vr(pp))

Where:

  • Vr(pp) = Peak-to-peak ripple voltage in volts.
  • IL = Load current in amperes.
  • fr = Ripple frequency in hertz.
  • C = Capacitance in farads.

For a full-wave bridge rectifier:

fr = 2f

For a 50 Hz supply, the ripple frequency is approximately 100 Hz.

Example – Calculate the filter capacitor

Design the approximate filter capacitor for a full-wave rectifier supplying 100 mA with a maximum ripple of 1 V peak-to-peak. Assume a 50 Hz AC supply.

Given:
IL = 100 mA = 0.1 A
fr = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz
Vr(pp) = 1 V

Solution:
C = IL / (fr × Vr(pp))

C = 0.1 / (100 × 1)

C = 0.001 F

Required capacitance ≈ 1000 µF

Practical capacitor selection

  • Select a capacitance equal to or higher than the calculated value, considering actual ripple requirements.
  • Choose a voltage rating above the maximum expected DC voltage.
  • Check ripple-current rating and temperature rating.
  • Observe electrolytic capacitor polarity.
The ripple formula is an approximation for relatively small ripple. Actual ripple depends on transformer impedance, diode conduction angle, capacitor ESR and load current.

7. Step 4 – Voltage Regulator Selection

A voltage regulator maintains the required DC output voltage after the rectifier and filter stages.

Example: 7805 regulator

The 7805 is a fixed positive linear regulator that provides a nominal 5 V output within its specified operating conditions.

Conceptual 7805 regulator arrangement. Use the manufacturer's recommended input and output capacitors and verify the pinout for the exact package.

Important regulator parameters

  • Input voltage range.
  • Output voltage and output current.
  • Dropout or minimum input-output voltage difference.
  • Maximum power dissipation.
  • Thermal shutdown and current limiting, if provided.
A 7805 needs sufficient input voltage above its 5 V output. The required headroom depends on the device and load current. Check its datasheet rather than assuming any input voltage will work.

8. Step 5 – Regulator Power Dissipation

A linear regulator dissipates the voltage difference between its input and output as heat.

PD ≈ (VIN − VOUT) × IOUT

Example

Suppose a regulator receives 12 V DC and provides 5 V DC at a load current of 200 mA.

Given:
VIN = 12 V
VOUT = 5 V
IOUT = 0.2 A

Solution:
PD = (12 − 5) × 0.2

PD = 7 × 0.2

PD = 1.4 W

The regulator must safely dissipate approximately 1.4 W under these assumed conditions.

Engineering consideration: Check junction temperature, package thermal resistance, ambient temperature and heat-sink requirements. A regulator that is electrically rated for the current may still overheat.

9. Complete Design Example – 5 V DC Supply

Consider a basic transformer-based supply intended to provide approximately 5 V DC at 100 mA.

Design Parameter Example Selection
AC supply 230 V AC, 50 Hz mains (qualified and isolated design)
Transformer secondary 12 V AC RMS
Rectifier Four-diode bridge rectifier
Filter capacitor 1000 µF, with suitable voltage and ripple-current ratings
Voltage regulator 7805, subject to input and thermal checks
Output voltage Nominal 5 V DC
Target load current 100 mA

Design calculation summary

  1. Transformer secondary = 12 V RMS.
  2. Peak secondary voltage = 12 × 1.414 ≈ 16.97 V.
  3. Bridge peak after two assumed 0.7 V diode drops ≈ 15.57 V.
  4. Full-wave ripple frequency at 50 Hz = 100 Hz.
  5. For 100 mA and 1 V peak-to-peak ripple, C ≈ 1000 µF.
  6. Use a suitable regulator to obtain nominal 5 V DC.
  7. Verify minimum regulator input voltage under full load and low mains conditions.
This is an educational first-pass design, not a validated construction specification. The 12 V secondary can produce a DC level that causes substantial dissipation in a 7805. Check worst-case capacitor voltage, ripple valley, regulator thermal performance, transformer current rating and component ratings before building.

10. Practical Testing Procedure

Equipment required

  • Digital multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope, if available.
  • Suitable isolated low-voltage AC transformer supply.
  • Bridge rectifier or four suitably rated diodes.
  • Filter capacitor.
  • Voltage regulator.
  • Suitable load resistor.

Testing steps

  1. Inspect component values, polarity and connections before applying power.
  2. Measure the transformer secondary AC voltage.
  3. Measure the rectifier output using the DC voltage range.
  4. Measure ripple across the filter capacitor using an oscilloscope, if available.
  5. Measure the regulator input voltage.
  6. Measure the regulated output voltage.
  7. Connect a suitable load and observe output voltage and regulator temperature.
  8. Check that all readings remain within component ratings.
An oscilloscope is useful for distinguishing DC level from ripple. A multimeter alone may not accurately characterize the ripple waveform.

11. Common Faults and Troubleshooting

Observed Problem Possible Cause Check
No DC output Open transformer winding, blown fuse or incorrect wiring. Check input and secondary voltage with appropriate safety precautions.
Low DC voltage Overload, diode fault or insufficient transformer voltage. Measure voltage at each stage.
High ripple Insufficient capacitance or faulty capacitor. Inspect capacitor and measure ripple.
Regulator overheating Excessive voltage drop or load current. Calculate power dissipation and check heat sinking.
Output voltage unstable Input instability, poor connections or unsuitable capacitors. Check regulator input and output conditions.
Fuse repeatedly blows Short circuit, excessive inrush current or component failure. Disconnect power and investigate the fault before replacing the fuse.

12. Electrical Safety

Important safety warning: Mains electricity can cause fatal electric shock and fire. Do not connect a breadboard or exposed circuit directly to mains voltage.
  • Use a properly rated isolated transformer for low-voltage experiments.
  • Keep mains wiring enclosed and inaccessible.
  • Use correctly rated fuses and protective components.
  • Discharge filter capacitors safely before handling a circuit.
  • Observe capacitor polarity and voltage ratings.
  • Never work on an energized mains circuit.
  • Have mains-side design and testing performed by a qualified person.

13. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What are the main stages of a DC power supply?

Transformer, rectifier, filter and voltage regulator.

Q2. Why is a transformer used?

It changes AC voltage and provides isolation in a suitable transformer-based power supply.

Q3. Why is a bridge rectifier commonly used?

It uses both half-cycles of AC and does not require a centre-tapped transformer secondary.

Q4. Why is a capacitor connected after the rectifier?

It stores charge and supplies the load between rectified voltage peaks, reducing ripple.

Q5. What is the ripple frequency of a full-wave rectifier?

It is twice the AC supply frequency.

Q6. What is the function of a 7805 IC?

It provides a nominal regulated positive 5 V output.

Q7. Why does a linear regulator require a heat sink?

It may dissipate significant power as heat due to the voltage difference between input and output.

Q8. How can ripple voltage be reduced?

By selecting appropriate filter capacitance and filter components, or using a suitable regulator and filter design.

Q9. What happens if the filter capacitor is connected with reverse polarity?

It may overheat, leak, fail or rupture. Correct polarity is essential for polarized capacitors.

Q10. How do you test a DC power supply?

Measure AC input and secondary voltage, rectifier output, ripple, regulator input and regulated output, then verify operation under a suitable load.
