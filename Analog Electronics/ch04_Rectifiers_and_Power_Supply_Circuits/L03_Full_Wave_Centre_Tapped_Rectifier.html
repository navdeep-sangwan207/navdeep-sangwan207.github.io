Lesson 3: Full-Wave Centre-Tapped Rectifier

Chapter 4: Rectifiers and Power Supply Circuits

Two-diode operation, transformer configuration, output waveforms and rectifier calculations.

  • Identify the components of a centre-tapped rectifier.
  • Explain alternate diode conduction during both half-cycles.

2. Introduction to Full-Wave Rectifier

A full-wave rectifier is an electronic circuit that converts both positive and negative half-cycles of an AC input into a unidirectional pulsating DC output.

Unlike a half-wave rectifier, which uses only one half-cycle, a full-wave rectifier uses the complete AC waveform. This improves rectification efficiency and reduces ripple for an unfiltered output.

Definition: A full-wave centre-tapped rectifier is a rectifier circuit that uses two diodes and a centre-tapped transformer to convert both half-cycles of AC into pulsating DC.

3. Centre-Tapped Transformer

A centre-tapped transformer has a secondary winding with three terminals: two end terminals and one centre terminal.

The centre terminal divides the secondary winding into two equal halves. The voltages at the two ends are equal in magnitude but opposite in phase with respect to the centre tap.

3.1 Important Characteristics

  • Two equal secondary winding sections.
  • Centre terminal acts as the common reference.
  • Voltages across the two halves are equal in magnitude.
  • The two half-winding voltages are 180° out of phase.
If the voltage from one end to the centre tap is 12 V RMS, the voltage across the complete secondary winding is 24 V RMS, assuming an ideal transformer.

4. Circuit Diagram

The circuit uses two diodes connected to the two ends of the centre-tapped secondary winding. Their cathodes are connected together to provide the positive output. The load is connected between this output and the centre tap.

Important circuit connection: Both diode cathodes connect to the positive output. The centre tap is the return connection for the load.

Components Used

Component Function
Centre-tapped transformer Provides two equal, opposite-phase AC voltages.
Diode D1 Conducts during the positive half-cycle of the upper secondary voltage.
Diode D2 Conducts during the other half-cycle.
Load resistor RL Receives the rectified output.

5. Operation During First Half-Cycle

Consider the half-cycle during which terminal A is positive with respect to the centre tap.

  • D1 becomes forward biased.
  • D2 becomes reverse biased.
  • D1 conducts and D2 blocks.
  • Current flows through the load resistor.
  • The load current flows in the chosen positive direction.
D1 = ON    |    D2 = OFF

Current path:

Upper secondary terminal A → D1 → Load resistor RL → Centre tap.

6. Operation During Second Half-Cycle

During the next half-cycle, terminal B becomes positive with respect to the centre tap.

  • D2 becomes forward biased.
  • D1 becomes reverse biased.
  • D2 conducts and D1 blocks.
  • Current again flows through the load resistor in the same direction.
  • The output polarity across the load remains unchanged.
D1 = OFF    |    D2 = ON
Current path: Lower secondary terminal B → D2 → Load resistor RL → Centre tap.

Thus, both half-cycles produce load current in the same direction. This is full-wave rectification.

7. Input and Output Waveforms

7.1 Secondary Input Voltage

The two secondary half-winding voltages have equal magnitude and are 180° out of phase with each other.

7.2 Rectified Output Voltage

Both half-cycles appear as positive pulses across the load. Therefore, the output frequency is twice the input frequency.

The diagrams show the ideal voltage relationship. In a practical centre-tapped rectifier, one diode forward drop is present in the conducting path.

8. Mathematical Analysis

Let Vm represent the peak voltage of either half of the transformer secondary winding.

8.1 Average or DC Output Voltage

The output is a full-wave rectified sine wave. Average output voltage is calculated over one complete input cycle.

VDC = (1 / 2π) ∫02π |Vm sin θ| dθ

After integration:

VDC = 2Vm / π
VDC = 0.637 Vm

8.2 Average DC Output Current

For an ideal diode and resistive load:

IDC = VDC / RL
IDC = 2Im / π

8.3 RMS Output Voltage

The RMS value is obtained by squaring the output waveform, averaging it over a complete cycle and taking the square root.

VRMS = √[(1 / 2π) ∫02π Vm2 sin²θ dθ]
VRMS = Vm / √2
VRMS = 0.707 Vm

8.4 RMS Output Current

IRMS = Im / √2

8.5 Peak Inverse Voltage (PIV)

When one diode conducts, the other diode is reverse biased. The non-conducting diode experiences a reverse voltage equal to approximately twice the peak voltage of one half-secondary winding.

PIV = 2Vm
For a centre-tapped full-wave rectifier, PIV is 2Vm per diode, where Vm is the peak voltage across one half of the secondary.

8.6 Ripple Factor

The ripple factor for an ideal, unfiltered full-wave rectifier is:

r = √[(VRMS / VDC)² − 1]
r ≈ 0.482

8.7 Rectification Efficiency

For an ideal full-wave rectifier with a resistive load:

η = PDC / PAC × 100%
ηmax ≈ 81.2%

9. Important Performance Parameters

Parameter Ideal Value
Number of diodes 2

RMS output voltage Vm / √2

Maximum rectification efficiency Approximately 81.2%
PIV per diode 2Vm
Ripple frequency 2f

10. Solved Numerical Example

Problem

A full-wave centre-tapped rectifier has a peak voltage of 20 V across each half of the transformer secondary. The load resistance is 1 kΩ. Assume ideal diodes. Calculate:

  1. Average DC output voltage.
  2. RMS output voltage.
  3. Average DC load current.
  4. RMS load current.
  5. Peak inverse voltage per diode.

Given

Vm = 20 V
RL = 1000 Ω

Step 1: Average DC Output Voltage

VDC = 2Vm / π

= (2 × 20) / 3.1416

= 12.73 V

Step 2: RMS Output Voltage

VRMS = Vm / √2

= 20 / 1.414

= 14.14 V

Step 3: Average DC Load Current

IDC = VDC / RL

= 12.73 / 1000

= 12.73 mA

Step 4: RMS Load Current

IRMS = VRMS / RL

= 14.14 / 1000

= 14.14 mA

Step 5: Peak Inverse Voltage

PIV = 2Vm

= 2 × 20

= 40 V
Final Answers:
  • Average DC voltage = 12.73 V
  • RMS voltage = 14.14 V
  • Average DC current = 12.73 mA
  • RMS current = 14.14 mA
  • PIV per diode = 40 V

11. Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Uses both half-cycles of the AC input.
  • Higher rectification efficiency than half-wave rectification.
  • Lower ripple factor than a half-wave rectifier.
  • Ripple frequency is twice the input frequency.
  • Only one diode is in the conducting path at a time.

Disadvantages

  • Requires a centre-tapped transformer.
  • Requires two diodes.
  • Each diode must withstand a PIV of 2Vm.
  • Only half of the secondary winding conducts during each half-cycle.
  • Transformer utilization may be lower than in a bridge rectifier.

12. Applications

  • DC power supply circuits.
  • Battery charging systems.
  • Industrial electronic equipment.
  • Low-frequency power conversion.
  • Rectifier-based laboratory experiments.

13. Interview Questions

Q1. How many diodes are used in a centre-tapped full-wave rectifier?

Two diodes.

Q2. Why is a centre-tapped transformer required?

It provides two equal secondary voltages that are 180° out of phase with respect to the centre tap.

Q3. What is the average DC output voltage?

VDC = 2Vm / π.

Q4. What is the RMS output voltage?

VRMS = Vm / √2.

Q5. What is the PIV of each diode?

PIV = 2Vm.

Q6. What is the ripple frequency?

Twice the input frequency, or 2f.

Q7. How many diodes conduct at a time?

Only one diode conducts at a time in the ideal circuit.

Q8. What is the maximum rectification efficiency?

Approximately 81.2% for an ideal circuit.

Q9. Why is its ripple factor lower than that of a half-wave rectifier?

Because both input half-cycles contribute to the output, making the output pulses more frequent.
