2. Introduction to Filters

A rectifier converts AC voltage into pulsating DC voltage. However, the output contains an unwanted AC component called ripple.

A filter is an electronic circuit connected after the rectifier to reduce this ripple and produce a smoother DC output.

Basic DC power supply sequence:

AC Supply → Transformer → Rectifier → Filter → Voltage Regulator → DC Load

Why Is a Filter Required?

  • To reduce unwanted AC ripple.
  • To provide a smoother DC voltage.
  • To improve the performance of electronic circuits.
  • To provide a more stable input for voltage regulators.
  • To reduce supply-related noise in sensitive circuits.

3. What Is a Capacitor Filter?

A capacitor filter is a simple filtering circuit in which a capacitor is connected in parallel with the load resistance at the output of a rectifier.

The capacitor stores electrical energy when the rectifier output voltage rises and releases this energy when the rectifier output voltage falls.

The capacitor charges near the peaks of the rectified voltage and discharges through the load between successive peaks. This action reduces output voltage variations.

4. Capacitor Filter Circuit Diagram

The capacitor is connected across the load resistance. This is commonly called a shunt capacitor filter.

5. Working Principle of Capacitor Filter

5.1 Capacitor Charging

When the rectified voltage rises towards its peak value, the capacitor charges through the conducting rectifier path.

  • The capacitor voltage increases.
  • It charges approximately to the peak rectifier output voltage.
  • Energy is stored in the electric field of the capacitor.

5.2 Capacitor Discharging

After the rectified voltage starts decreasing below the capacitor voltage, the rectifier diodes become reverse-biased. The capacitor then supplies current to the load.

  • The capacitor releases stored energy.
  • Its voltage gradually decreases.
  • The load continues receiving current.
  • The next rectified peak recharges the capacitor.
The repeated charging and discharging action keeps the output voltage closer to its peak value instead of allowing it to fall to zero between rectifier pulses.

6. Capacitor Charging and Discharging Waveform

Without a filter, the output consists of full-wave pulsating DC. With a capacitor filter, the output voltage remains relatively high between successive peaks.

This is a conceptual waveform. The filtered output has a small ripple rather than a perfectly constant voltage.

7. Ripple Voltage

Ripple voltage is the small periodic variation remaining in the output voltage after filtering.

For a capacitor-input filter, the approximate peak-to-peak ripple voltage is:

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fr C)

Where:

  • Vr(pp) = Peak-to-peak ripple voltage.
  • IL = DC load current.
  • fr = Ripple frequency.
  • C = Filter capacitance in farads.

For Half-Wave Rectifier

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (fC)

For Full-Wave Rectifier

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (2fC)
For the same load current, capacitance and AC input frequency, a full-wave rectifier produces approximately half the peak-to-peak ripple voltage of a half-wave rectifier under the small-ripple approximation.

8. Factors Affecting Ripple Voltage

Parameter Effect on Ripple
Capacitance increases Ripple decreases
Load current increases Ripple increases
Ripple frequency increases Ripple decreases
Load resistance increases Load current decreases and ripple generally decreases
A larger capacitor generally provides better smoothing, but it also increases charging surge current and may require a rectifier and transformer with higher current ratings.

9. Approximate Ripple Factor with Capacitor Filter

For a capacitor-input filter with relatively small ripple, the approximate ripple factor for a full-wave rectifier is:

γ ≈ 1 / (4√3 f RLC)

For a half-wave rectifier, the corresponding approximation is:

γ ≈ 1 / (2√3 f RLC)

Where:

  • f = AC supply frequency in hertz.
  • RL = Load resistance in ohms.
  • C = Filter capacitance in farads.
These formulas assume a capacitor-input filter, a resistive load and relatively small ripple. They are approximate design equations, not exact results for every power supply.

10. Numerical Example 1: Ripple Voltage

A full-wave rectifier operates at 50 Hz and supplies a load current of 100 mA. A 1000 µF capacitor is connected across the load. Calculate the approximate peak-to-peak ripple voltage.

Given Data

  • IL = 100 mA = 0.1 A
  • f = 50 Hz
  • C = 1000 µF = 0.001 F
  • fr = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz

Formula

Vr(pp) ≈ IL / (frC)

Calculation

Vr(pp) = 0.1 / (100 × 0.001)

= 0.1 / 0.1

= 1 V
Answer: Approximate peak-to-peak ripple voltage = 1 V.

11. Numerical Example 2: Capacitor Selection

A full-wave rectifier supplies a load current of 200 mA. The AC supply frequency is 50 Hz. Calculate the minimum approximate capacitance required to limit peak-to-peak ripple voltage to 2 V.

Given Data

  • IL = 0.2 A
  • f = 50 Hz
  • fr = 100 Hz
  • Vr(pp) = 2 V

Formula

C ≈ IL / (fr Vr(pp))

Calculation

C = 0.2 / (100 × 2)

C = 0.001 F

C = 1000 µF
Answer: The approximate minimum capacitance is 1000 µF. In practical design, select a suitable standard capacitor value with appropriate voltage and ripple-current ratings, then verify the actual ripple under load.

12. Advantages of Capacitor Filter

  • Simple circuit construction.
  • Low cost and easy availability.
  • Reduces output ripple voltage.
  • Provides smoother DC output.
  • Suitable for many low- and medium-current power supplies.
  • Can be combined with voltage regulators.

13. Disadvantages of Capacitor Filter

  • Charging current pulses can be high.
  • Ripple increases when load current increases.
  • Large capacitance may be physically bulky and expensive.
  • Output voltage changes with load conditions.
  • Capacitor voltage rating must be selected carefully.
  • It does not provide precise voltage regulation by itself.

14. Practical Applications

  • Linear DC power supplies.
  • Embedded controller power supplies.
  • Battery charger circuits.
  • Small electronic adapters.
  • Audio amplifier power supplies.
  • Industrial control electronics.
  • Rectifier and regulator circuits.

15. Practical Design Considerations

  • Choose capacitance based on load current and acceptable ripple.
  • Select a capacitor with a voltage rating above the maximum expected DC voltage.
  • Check the capacitor's ripple-current rating.
  • Observe electrolytic capacitor polarity.
  • Consider rectifier surge-current capability.
  • Check transformer current and voltage ratings.
  • Use a voltage regulator if a stable DC output is required.
Safety: Large capacitors can retain dangerous charge after the power supply is switched off. Disconnect the supply and discharge capacitors using an appropriate resistor before handling the circuit.

18. Lesson Summary

  • A filter reduces unwanted AC ripple in rectifier output.
  • A capacitor filter is connected in parallel with the load.
  • The capacitor charges near rectified voltage peaks.
  • It discharges through the load between successive peaks.
  • Higher capacitance generally reduces ripple.
  • Higher load current generally increases ripple.
  • Full-wave rectifiers have twice the supply ripple frequency.
  • A voltage regulator is used when a stable DC output is required.
