2. Why Are LC and Pi Filters Required?

A capacitor filter reduces the ripple voltage at a rectifier output. However, applications requiring cleaner DC may need additional filtering.

Inductors and capacitors have complementary characteristics:

Inductor (L)

  • Opposes changes in current.
  • Stores energy in a magnetic field.
  • Has higher reactance at higher frequencies.
  • Can reduce AC ripple current while allowing DC current.

Capacitor (C)

  • Opposes changes in voltage.
  • Stores energy in an electric field.
  • Has lower reactance at higher frequencies.
  • Provides a low-impedance path for AC ripple components.
Inductive reactance: XL = 2πfL

Capacitive reactance: XC = 1/(2πfC)

Here, f is the frequency of the AC component being considered. In a rectifier filter, this is generally the ripple frequency rather than the original mains frequency.

3. Inductor-Input Filter (Choke Filter)

An inductor-input filter places a choke in series between the rectifier and the load. The choke opposes rapid changes in current and reduces the ripple current delivered to the load.

Working principle

  1. The rectifier produces pulsating DC.
  2. The inductor is connected in series with the load.
  3. When current attempts to increase, the inductor generates a voltage opposing the change.
  4. When current attempts to decrease, the inductor releases stored magnetic energy and opposes the decrease.
  5. The load current becomes smoother.
VL = L × (di/dt)

The inductor's voltage depends on the rate of change of current. It does not block DC in an ideal steady-state circuit, but its winding resistance causes a real DC voltage drop.

Important practical characteristics

  • Choke-input filters are commonly used where smoother current and lower peak charging currents are important.
  • Inductor size, winding resistance and magnetic saturation affect performance.
  • Choke-input operation can depend on maintaining sufficient load current.
  • At very light load, the output behaviour may change.
An inductor-input filter is different from a capacitor-input filter. In a choke-input supply, the inductor is the first major filter element after the rectifier. In a capacitor-input supply, a capacitor is placed first.

4. LC Filter Circuit

An LC filter combines a series inductor and a shunt capacitor. The inductor reduces ripple current, while the capacitor provides a low-impedance path for remaining AC ripple.

Operation

  • The series inductor opposes ripple-current variations.
  • The shunt capacitor bypasses part of the remaining ripple current.
  • The load receives a smoother DC voltage.

Frequency behaviour

XL = 2πfL
XC = 1/(2πfC)

As ripple frequency increases, inductive reactance increases and capacitive reactance decreases. These characteristics help attenuate higher-frequency ripple components.

Key idea: The inductor resists ripple current in series, while the capacitor provides a shunt path for AC ripple.

5. Pi (π) Filter Circuit

A π filter is named after the shape of its circuit arrangement. A common configuration is C-L-C: a capacitor connected across the input, followed by a series inductor and another capacitor across the output.

How the π filter works

  1. First capacitor C1: Charges near the rectifier voltage peaks and reduces a large part of the low-frequency ripple.
  2. Series inductor L: Opposes ripple-current changes passing toward the output.
  3. Second capacitor C2: Provides a shunt path for remaining ripple and smooths the output voltage.

The arrangement resembles the Greek letter π, giving the circuit its name.

A π filter is generally a capacitor-input filter. It can provide strong ripple reduction, but the first capacitor can cause high peak charging current in the rectifier and transformer. Component ratings and source impedance must be considered.

6. Comparison of Filter Types

Filter type Configuration Main action Practical consideration
Capacitor filter C across load Stores energy and supports load voltage between peaks High peak charging current may occur
Choke-input filter Series L Reduces ripple-current variation Large inductor and minimum-load considerations
LC filter Series L, shunt C Inductor attenuates ripple current; capacitor bypasses ripple Inductor resistance, size and resonance matter
π filter C-L-C Two capacitor stages with a series choke between them Input-capacitor charging pulses and inductor rating matter

7. Reactance Calculations

Example 1: Inductive reactance

A 10 mH inductor is used in a full-wave rectifier supply operating at 50 Hz. Calculate its inductive reactance at the ripple frequency.

Given:

  • L = 10 mH = 0.01 H
  • Ripple frequency = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz
XL = 2πfL
= 2 × 3.1416 × 100 × 0.01
≈ 6.28 Ω

Example 2: Capacitive reactance

Calculate the reactance of a 1000 µF capacitor at 100 Hz.

C = 1000 µF = 0.001 F
XC = 1/(2πfC)
= 1/(2 × 3.1416 × 100 × 0.001)
≈ 1.59 Ω

Example 3: Compare reactance at two frequencies

For the same 10 mH inductor:

At 50 Hz: XL ≈ 3.14 Ω
At 100 Hz: XL ≈ 6.28 Ω
At 200 Hz: XL ≈ 12.57 Ω

Inductive reactance increases directly with frequency.

8. LC Filter Frequency Response Concept

An LC network can attenuate ripple components while passing the DC component. Its behaviour depends on the inductance, capacitance, load and circuit topology.

f0 = 1/(2π√LC)

This is the ideal undamped LC resonant frequency. It is not automatically the exact cutoff frequency or ripple attenuation frequency of a loaded rectifier filter.

In a practical filter, damping, winding resistance, capacitor ESR and load resistance affect the frequency response. Resonance can produce peaking if the network is insufficiently damped.

Worked example: Ideal resonant frequency

For L = 10 mH and C = 1000 µF:

L = 0.01 H
C = 0.001 F
f0 = 1/(2π√(0.01 × 0.001))
≈ 50.33 Hz

This calculation identifies the ideal LC resonant frequency. It should not be interpreted as a complete filter design result.

11. Practical Design Considerations

Inductor selection

  • Inductance value and tolerance.
  • Rated DC current and saturation current.
  • Winding resistance and copper loss.
  • Core material and operating frequency.
  • Temperature rise and physical size.
  • Insulation and voltage rating.

Capacitor selection

  • Capacitance and voltage rating.
  • ESR and ripple-current capability.
  • Temperature and lifetime rating.
  • Polarity and manufacturer derating requirements.

Other considerations

  • Transformer regulation and winding resistance.
  • Rectifier diode current and surge ratings.
  • Inrush current during startup.
  • Resonance and damping in LC networks.
  • Load variation and transient response.
  • Physical layout, wiring resistance and thermal management.

13. Laboratory Experiments

Experiment 1: Compare capacitor and LC filters

Objective: Compare output ripple with a capacitor filter and an LC filter.

Equipment: Isolated low-voltage AC source, rectifier, suitable capacitor, inductor, load resistor, multimeter and oscilloscope.

  1. Build a low-voltage rectifier with a capacitor filter.
  2. Measure output DC voltage and ripple.
  3. Power down and safely discharge the capacitor.
  4. Connect a suitable series inductor and shunt capacitor to form an LC filter.
  5. Repeat the measurements under the same load conditions.
  6. Compare the measured ripple and voltage drop.

Expected observation: The LC network can reduce ripple further, depending on its component values and loading.

Experiment 2: Construct a π filter

  1. Connect the first capacitor across the rectifier output.
  2. Connect a suitable inductor in series with the positive rail.
  3. Connect the second capacitor across the output load.
  4. Check all capacitor polarities and component ratings.
  5. Measure the output ripple with an oscilloscope.

Expected observation: The C-L-C network provides additional ripple attenuation compared with a single capacitor stage in suitable operating conditions.

Experiment 3: Observe frequency response

  1. Use a safe, isolated low-voltage test arrangement.
  2. Change the frequency of a small AC test signal within component limits.
  3. Measure the input and output ripple amplitudes.
  4. Calculate the attenuation ratio at each frequency.
  5. Compare the result with the ideal reactance calculations.
Safety: Perform these experiments using isolated low-voltage supplies. Inductors and capacitors can store energy. Switch off power, discharge capacitors through an appropriate resistor and verify voltage before changing connections. Never probe a live mains circuit using an ordinary grounded oscilloscope connection.

15. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

  1. What is an LC filter?
    A filter consisting of an inductor and capacitor used to reduce ripple in a DC power supply.
  2. What is a choke?
    An inductor used in a power supply to oppose changes in current.
  3. Why is an inductor connected in series?
    To oppose ripple-current changes in the load path.
  4. Why is a capacitor connected in parallel?
    To provide a low-impedance path for AC ripple components.
  5. What is a π filter?
    A filter commonly arranged as capacitor-inductor-capacitor, or C-L-C.
  6. Why is it called a π filter?
    Its circuit arrangement resembles the Greek letter π.
  7. What is the function of the first capacitor in a π filter?
    It reduces a substantial portion of the rectifier ripple by charging near voltage peaks.
  8. What is the function of the inductor in a π filter?
    It reduces ripple-current variations between the two capacitor stages.
  9. What is the function of the second capacitor?
    It bypasses remaining ripple and smooths the output.
  10. What is inductive reactance?
    The opposition an inductor presents to AC, given by XL = 2πfL.
  11. What is capacitive reactance?
    The opposition a capacitor presents to AC, given by XC = 1/(2πfC).
  12. What happens to inductive reactance when frequency increases?
    It increases proportionally with frequency.
  13. What happens to capacitive reactance when frequency increases?
    It decreases.
  14. What is the ideal resonant frequency of an LC network?
    f0 = 1/(2π√LC).
  15. Why can resonance be a problem?
    It can produce unwanted voltage or current peaking if the network is insufficiently damped.
  16. What is core saturation?
    A magnetic operating condition where increasing current produces a much smaller increase in magnetic flux density.
  17. Why is inductor winding resistance important?
    It causes DC voltage drop and copper power loss.
  18. What is the main disadvantage of a large choke?
    It can be heavy, bulky, expensive and have winding losses.
  19. Why can a π filter produce high startup current?
    The first capacitor initially draws charging current through the rectifier and transformer.
  20. Which filter is best?
    There is no universally best filter. Selection depends on ripple, load, cost, size, current, voltage and transient requirements.

17. Key Technical Takeaways

  • Inductors oppose changes in current; capacitors oppose changes in voltage.
  • An inductor-input filter uses a series choke to smooth load current.
  • An LC filter combines a series inductor and shunt capacitor.
  • A π filter commonly uses a C-L-C arrangement.
  • Inductive reactance increases with frequency.
  • Capacitive reactance decreases with frequency.
  • The ideal LC resonant frequency is 1/(2π√LC).
  • Real filter performance depends on source impedance, loading, ESR, winding resistance and damping.
  • Component current, voltage, thermal and saturation ratings must be checked.
