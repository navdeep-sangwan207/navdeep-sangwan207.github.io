2. Introduction to Clippers

A clipper is a nonlinear wave-shaping circuit that removes a selected portion of an input waveform without significantly affecting the remaining portion.

Diodes are commonly used as switching elements in clipper circuits. The clipping level depends on the diode orientation, reference voltage and circuit configuration.

2.1 Applications

  • Input voltage protection
  • Signal amplitude limiting
  • Noise suppression
  • Waveform shaping
  • Communication circuits
  • Transient voltage limiting

2.2 Ideal Diode Model

ConditionIdeal Diode Behavior
Forward biasedShort circuit, approximately 0 V drop
Reverse biasedOpen circuit, zero current

For practical silicon diode calculations, use an approximate forward voltage of 0.7 V when appropriate. Actual forward voltage depends on current and temperature.

3. Series Diode Clippers

In a series clipper, the diode is connected in series with the load. Depending on diode orientation, one portion of the input signal is blocked.

3.1 Positive Series Clipper

A positive series clipper is arranged so that the positive half-cycle is blocked while the negative half-cycle is passed.

The exact output depends on the diode orientation and measurement reference. Analyze the diode state during each half-cycle rather than relying only on the circuit name.

3.2 Negative Series Clipper

Reversing the diode orientation changes which half-cycle is conducted. A negative series clipper can block the negative half-cycle and pass the positive half-cycle.

3.3 Series Clipper Operation

Input ConditionDiode StateOutput
Forward-biasing polarityONSignal appears across load, subject to diode drop
Reverse-biasing polarityOFFOutput is approximately zero in the ideal series circuit

4. Parallel (Shunt) Diode Clippers

In a parallel clipper, the diode is connected across the load. When the diode conducts, it shunts or limits the output voltage. A series resistor is normally required to limit current.

4.1 Positive Shunt Clipper

A diode connected across the output can conduct during the positive half-cycle and limit the positive output excursion.

4.2 Negative Shunt Clipper

Reversing the diode orientation causes conduction during the negative half-cycle and limits the negative excursion.

4.3 Comparison

ParameterSeries ClipperShunt Clipper
Diode placementSeries with loadParallel with load
Blocking actionDiode interrupts signal pathDiode diverts current
Current limitingDepends on circuit and source resistanceSeries resistor is important
Typical useWaveform selectionAmplitude limiting

5. Biased Clippers

A biased clipper uses a DC reference source in addition to a diode. It clips the waveform at a selected voltage rather than at zero.

5.1 Positive Biased Clipper

A positive clipping threshold can be set using a DC reference. For a practical silicon diode, the approximate threshold may be:

VClip ≈ VReference + VD

The exact sign and threshold depend on the orientation of the diode and reference source.

5.2 Negative Biased Clipper

A negative reference can establish a negative clipping level. For a practical diode, the threshold includes its forward drop.

5.3 Dual Biased Clipper

A dual biased clipper limits both positive and negative peaks. It is useful when a signal must remain within a specified voltage window.

Upper clipping level ≈ +V1 + VD
Lower clipping level ≈ −V2 − VD

These equations describe a common orientation of the reference sources and diodes. Always derive the actual thresholds from the specific circuit polarity.

7. Introduction to Clampers

A clamper is a wave-shaping circuit that shifts an entire waveform upward or downward by adding a DC level. Ideally, the peak-to-peak amplitude remains unchanged.

7.1 Main Components

  • Diode
  • Capacitor
  • Resistor

7.2 Positive Clamper

A positive clamper shifts the waveform upward so that its negative peak is approximately clamped to zero volts for an ideal diode and suitable time constant.

7.3 Negative Clamper

A negative clamper shifts the waveform downward so that its positive peak is approximately clamped to zero volts under ideal conditions.

7.4 Clamper Operation

  1. During one half-cycle, the diode conducts.
  2. The capacitor charges to approximately the input peak, subject to diode drop.
  3. During the opposite half-cycle, the diode is OFF.
  4. The capacitor voltage adds to or subtracts from the input.
  5. The waveform shifts to a new DC level.

7.5 Clamper Time Constant

τ = RC

For good clamping, the RC time constant should generally be much larger than the input signal period:

RC >> T

A large time constant helps the capacitor retain its charge over a cycle. If RC is too small, the output waveform may tilt or droop.

10. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Positive Clipping Level

A biased clipper has a 3 V reference source and a silicon diode. Estimate the clipping threshold for the orientation where the reference and diode drop add.

Show Solution
VClip ≈ 3 + 0.7 = 3.7 V

Problem 2: Negative Clipping Level

A negative biased clipper has a 4 V reference and a silicon diode. Find the approximate negative clipping threshold for the orientation where the magnitudes add.

Show Solution
VClip ≈ −(4 + 0.7) = −4.7 V

Problem 3: Clamper Time Constant

A clamper uses R = 100 kΩ and C = 10 µF. Calculate its time constant.

Show Solution
τ = RC
τ = 100000 × 10 × 10⁻⁶
τ = 1 second

Problem 4: Signal Period

A clamper receives a 1 kHz signal. Find the signal period.

Show Solution
T = 1/f = 1/1000 = 1 ms

For a 1 second time constant, RC/T = 1000, which is much greater than 1.

Problem 5: Output of an Ideal Clamper

An ideal sine wave has a peak amplitude of 5 V. It is shifted upward by 5 V. Find its maximum and minimum output values.

Show Solution
Maximum = +5 + 5 = +10 V
Minimum = −5 + 5 = 0 V

11. Practical Laboratory Experiments

Experiment 1: Series Clipper

Objective: Observe the clipping of one half-cycle of an AC signal.

Components: Silicon diode, resistor, function generator, oscilloscope and breadboard.

  1. Construct a series clipper with the selected diode orientation.
  2. Apply a low-voltage sine-wave input.
  3. Observe input and output simultaneously.
  4. Reverse the diode orientation.
  5. Compare the output waveforms.

Experiment 2: Biased Clipper

Objective: Study how a DC reference changes the clipping level.

  1. Build a biased clipper.
  2. Apply a sine-wave input.
  3. Change the DC reference voltage.
  4. Observe the change in clipping threshold.
  5. Compare measured and calculated thresholds.

Experiment 3: Positive Clamper

Objective: Shift an AC waveform in the positive direction.

  1. Connect a diode, capacitor and resistor in the positive clamper configuration.
  2. Apply a low-frequency sine wave.
  3. Observe input and output waveforms.
  4. Measure the DC shift.
  5. Change the capacitor value and observe droop.

Experiment 4: Negative Clamper

Objective: Shift the waveform in the negative direction.

  1. Reverse the diode orientation as required.
  2. Apply the input waveform.
  3. Observe the output DC level.
  4. Compare with the positive clamper.
Use an isolated, low-voltage signal source for these experiments. Never connect a breadboard clipper or clamper directly to mains. Observe oscilloscope grounding requirements and component ratings.

15. Lecture Summary

  • Clippers remove selected portions of input waveforms.
  • Series and shunt clippers differ in diode placement.
  • Biased clippers use a DC reference to set the clipping threshold.
  • Dual clippers limit both positive and negative peaks.
  • Clampers shift the DC level of an AC waveform.
  • Positive and negative clampers shift the waveform in opposite directions.
  • The RC time constant determines how well the capacitor retains charge.
  • Oscilloscope waveform analysis is an essential practical skill.
