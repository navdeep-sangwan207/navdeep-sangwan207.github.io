2. Why Do We Need the Diode Equation?

A semiconductor diode is a nonlinear electronic device. Unlike an ideal resistor, its current does not increase linearly with applied voltage.

When a forward voltage is applied to a PN junction, the current generally increases exponentially over a suitable operating range.

The relationship between the voltage across a diode and the current flowing through it is described approximately by the Shockley diode equation.

Shockley Diode Equation:

ID = IS [ eVD/(nVT) − 1 ]

Where:

  • ID = diode current
  • IS = reverse saturation current
  • VD = voltage across diode
  • n = ideality factor
  • VT = thermal voltage
  • e = mathematical exponential constant
Important: The Shockley equation is an idealized model. Real diodes exhibit series resistance, high-current effects, leakage, breakdown, recombination effects and other nonideal behavior.

3. Thermal Voltage (VT)

Thermal voltage is a fundamental semiconductor parameter associated with thermal energy and electric charge.

VT = kT/q

  • k = Boltzmann constant = 1.380649 × 10−23 J/K
  • T = absolute temperature in Kelvin
  • q = electron charge = 1.602176634 × 10−19 C

At approximately 25°C:

VT ≈ 25.69 mV

At approximately 27°C (300 K), engineers commonly use:

VT ≈ 25.85 mV

Conversion of Temperature

T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15

Worked Example

Calculate thermal voltage at 50°C.

Step 1: Convert to Kelvin.

T = 50 + 273.15 T = 323.15 K

Step 2: Calculate thermal voltage.

Vt = kT/q Vt = (1.380649 × 10^-23 × 323.15) / (1.602176634 × 10^-19) Vt ≈ 0.02785 V
Answer: Thermal voltage at 50°C is approximately 27.85 mV.

4. Ideality Factor (n)

The ideality factor, also known as the emission coefficient, describes how closely the current-voltage behavior of a real diode follows the ideal Shockley model.

It appears in the exponent of the diode equation.

ID = IS [ eVD/(nVT) − 1 ]

Ideality Factor Typical Interpretation
n ≈ 1 Diffusion-dominated behavior in an idealized junction model
n ≈ 2 Recombination-dominated behavior in an idealized junction model
1 < n < 2 Mixed or nonideal behavior in many practical devices
Note: These values are approximate interpretations, not universal rules. Ideality factor depends on device construction, temperature, bias region and measurement conditions.

Effect of Ideality Factor

For a given forward voltage, thermal voltage and saturation current, increasing n generally reduces the exponential current predicted by the Shockley equation.

5. Reverse Saturation Current (IS)

Reverse saturation current is a parameter in the Shockley model representing the small current associated with minority-carrier transport in the idealized PN junction.

IS = reverse saturation current

  • It is usually very small in silicon diodes.
  • It depends strongly on temperature.
  • It is affected by semiconductor material and junction characteristics.
  • It is a parameter in the Shockley equation.
  • It is not the same as the complete leakage current measured in every real diode.

Reverse Bias Behavior

For a sufficiently negative diode voltage, before breakdown and under the ideal Shockley model:

ID ≈ −IS

Actual reverse current can be affected by surface leakage, generation mechanisms, temperature, junction structure and applied reverse voltage.

Practical distinction: Reverse saturation current in an ideal equation and reverse leakage current in a datasheet are related concepts but are not necessarily identical measurements.

6. Understanding the Shockley Diode Equation

ID = IS [ eVD/(nVT) − 1 ]

Forward Bias

When VD is positive and sufficiently greater than nVT, the exponential term becomes dominant.

ID ≈ ISeVD/(nVT)

The −1 term becomes negligible in this operating region.

Reverse Bias

For negative VD sufficiently below zero, but before breakdown:

ID ≈ −IS

Assumptions and Limitations

  • The junction is modeled using an idealized PN junction.
  • Temperature is uniform and constant during the calculation.
  • Series resistance is neglected.
  • High-current effects are neglected.
  • Breakdown behavior is not modeled.
  • Idealized transport mechanisms are assumed.

7. Worked Numerical: Calculating Diode Current

Consider a diode with the following parameters:

ParameterValue
IS1 nA
VD0.60 V
n1
VT25.85 mV

Calculate the diode current.

Step 1: Write the equation

ID = IS [ eVD/(nVT) − 1 ]

Step 2: Substitute values

ID = 1 × 10^-9 [ exp(0.60 / 0.02585) - 1 ]

Step 3: Calculate

0.60 / 0.02585 ≈ 23.21 exp(23.21) ≈ 1.20 × 10^10 ID ≈ 1 × 10^-9 × 1.20 × 10^10 ID ≈ 12.0 A
Model limitation: This calculated current is far beyond the range in which the ideal Shockley equation alone can accurately predict the current of a typical small signal diode. Series resistance, thermal effects and device limits become significant. The result is an ideal-model mathematical prediction, not a safe operating current.

8. Static Resistance and Dynamic Resistance

A diode is nonlinear, so its resistance depends on its operating point.

8.1 Static Resistance (DC Resistance)

Static resistance is the ratio of the voltage across a diode to the current flowing through it at a particular operating point.

RDC = VD/ID

Worked Example

Suppose a diode has:

  • VD = 0.65 V
  • ID = 10 mA
RDC = VD / ID RDC = 0.65 / 0.010 RDC = 65 Ω
Answer: Static resistance = 65 Ω.

8.2 Dynamic Resistance (Small-Signal Resistance)

Dynamic resistance is the incremental resistance of a diode at a specific operating point.

rd = dVD/dID

For the ideal Shockley equation in the forward region, when the −1 term is negligible and other effects are neglected:

rd ≈ nVT/ID

Worked Example

Calculate dynamic resistance for:

  • n = 1
  • VT = 25.85 mV
  • ID = 2 mA
rd = nVT / ID rd = 0.02585 / 0.002 rd = 12.925 Ω
Answer: Dynamic resistance ≈ 12.93 Ω.

Static vs Dynamic Resistance

Static Resistance Dynamic Resistance
V/I at operating point dV/dI at operating point
Ratio of DC values Incremental slope
Used in DC analysis Used in small-signal analysis
May vary significantly with operating point Depends on the local slope of the V-I curve

11. Temperature Effects on Diode Parameters

Temperature affects the diode equation through thermal voltage and saturation current, and it also affects real device behavior.

11.1 Thermal Voltage

VT = kT/q

Thermal voltage increases linearly with absolute temperature.

11.2 Reverse Saturation Current

Reverse saturation current generally increases strongly with temperature in silicon junction devices. The exact temperature dependence varies with the device and its operating conditions.

11.3 Forward Voltage

For a silicon diode operating at a fixed current in a typical operating region, forward voltage generally decreases as temperature increases.

Engineering note: The often-quoted approximate temperature coefficient of −2 mV/°C applies to many silicon PN junctions over certain operating conditions. It is not a universal constant and depends on the device and current.

11.4 Temperature and Reliability

  • Temperature can increase leakage current.
  • Thermal runaway may occur in circuits where heating increases current and the resulting heat further increases temperature.
  • Power dissipation must be considered.
  • Heat sinking and current limiting may be required.

12. Practical Circuit: Diode with a Series Resistor

A resistor is commonly used to limit the current through a diode in a simple circuit.

Worked Circuit Numerical

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 5 V
  • Series resistance = 1 kΩ
  • Approximate diode voltage = 0.7 V

Calculate current through the diode.

I = (VS − VD)/R
I = (5 - 0.7) / 1000 I = 4.3 / 1000 I = 0.0043 A I = 4.3 mA
Answer: The approximate circuit current is 4.3 mA.
The constant 0.7 V is a simplified silicon diode approximation. The actual voltage depends on current, temperature, device type and other operating conditions.

13. Practical Laboratory Experiment

Objective

To observe diode current and voltage and understand the operating point using a series resistor.

Required Components

  • Silicon diode
  • 1 kΩ resistor
  • DC power supply
  • Digital multimeter
  • Breadboard
  • Connecting wires

Procedure

  1. Connect the diode in series with the resistor and DC supply.
  2. Begin with a low supply voltage.
  3. Measure the voltage across the diode.
  4. Measure the voltage across the resistor.
  5. Calculate the current using resistor voltage divided by resistor resistance.
  6. Increase the supply voltage in small steps within the safe operating limits.
  7. Record the diode voltage and current at each step.
  8. Plot diode current against diode voltage.
  9. Compare the measured behavior with the idealized diode equation.

Observation Table

Expected Result

As supply voltage increases, diode current generally increases significantly after forward conduction begins. The series resistor limits current.

15. Placement Interview Questions

Q1. What is the Shockley diode equation?

It is an idealized equation describing the current-voltage relationship of a PN junction diode.

Q2. Write the diode equation.

ID = IS[eVD/(nVT) − 1].

Q3. What is thermal voltage?

Thermal voltage is kT/q. At approximately 25°C, it is around 25.69 mV.

Q4. What is the ideality factor?

It is a parameter that describes the deviation of diode behavior from the ideal exponential model.

Q5. What is reverse saturation current?

It is the small current parameter associated with minority-carrier transport in the idealized diode model.

Q6. What happens to reverse saturation current as temperature increases?

It generally increases significantly with temperature.

Q7. What is static resistance?

Static resistance is the ratio of diode voltage to diode current at an operating point.

Q8. What is dynamic resistance?

Dynamic resistance is the incremental voltage-to-current ratio at a particular operating point.

Q9. Why is dynamic resistance important?

It is useful for small-signal analysis of circuits containing diodes.

Q10. What is the difference between static and dynamic resistance?

Static resistance uses V/I, while dynamic resistance uses the local derivative dV/dI.

Q11. Why is a series resistor used with a diode?

It limits current and helps prevent excessive current and damage.

Q12. Why is the Shockley equation not sufficient at high currents?

Because series resistance, self-heating, high-level injection and other nonideal effects can become important.

Q13. What is the approximate dynamic resistance of an ideal diode at 1 mA and 25°C with n = 1?

rd ≈ 0.02585 / 0.001 rd ≈ 25.85 Ω

Q14. How does ideality factor affect forward current?

For a given forward voltage, temperature and saturation current, a larger ideality factor generally reduces the predicted exponential current.

Q15. What is the difference between diode leakage current and reverse saturation current?

Reverse saturation current is a parameter of the idealized model, while measured leakage current can include other mechanisms.

Q16. What is the effect of temperature on forward voltage?

At fixed current, forward voltage generally decreases with increasing temperature for many silicon PN junction diodes over typical operating ranges.

Q17. What is the thermal voltage at 300 K?

VT ≈ 25.85 mV

Q18. How is diode current measured in a circuit?

It can be measured using a properly connected ammeter in series, or calculated using the voltage across a known series resistor.

Q19. What is the power dissipated in a diode?

PD ≈ VDID

For DC operating conditions, this is the approximate electrical power dissipated in the diode.

Q20. Why must diode datasheets be consulted?

They specify voltage, current, power, temperature and other limits that must be respected for reliable operation.

16. Placement-Oriented Numerical Practice

Problem 1

A diode has VD = 0.6 V and ID = 5 mA. Calculate static resistance.

Show Answer
RDC = 0.6 / 0.005 RDC = 120 Ω

Problem 2

Calculate dynamic resistance at 2 mA for n = 1 and VT = 25.85 mV.

Show Answer
rd = 0.02585 / 0.002 rd = 12.925 Ω

Problem 3

Calculate thermal voltage at 80°C.

Show Answer
T = 80 + 273.15 T = 353.15 K VT = kT/q VT ≈ 30.44 mV

Problem 4

A diode carries 8 mA at a voltage of 0.72 V. Calculate DC power dissipation.

Show Answer
P = V × I P = 0.72 × 0.008 P = 0.00576 W P = 5.76 mW

Problem 5

A 5 V supply is connected to a diode with an approximate forward voltage of 0.7 V through a 470 Ω resistor. Calculate current.

Show Answer
I = (5 - 0.7) / 470 I = 4.3 / 470 I ≈ 9.15 mA

Problem 6

For a Shockley diode with n = 2, VT = 25.85 mV and ID = 1 mA, calculate approximate dynamic resistance.

Show Answer
rd ≈ nVT / ID rd ≈ (2 × 0.02585) / 0.001 rd ≈ 51.7 Ω
