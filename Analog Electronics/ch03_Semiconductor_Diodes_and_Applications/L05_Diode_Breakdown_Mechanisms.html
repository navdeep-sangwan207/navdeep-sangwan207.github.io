2. Introduction to Diode Breakdown

A PN junction diode is normally operated in forward bias or in reverse bias below its breakdown region.

When a sufficiently large reverse voltage is applied across a PN junction, the junction can enter a breakdown region in which reverse current increases rapidly.

In breakdown, the diode is not necessarily damaged. It can operate safely in the breakdown region if its current, power dissipation and temperature are controlled and if the device is designed and rated for that operation.

Important: An ordinary diode may be damaged by excessive reverse current or power dissipation. Breakdown is not automatically a safe operating condition.

3. PN Junction Under Reverse Bias

When a PN junction is reverse biased:

  • The P-region is connected to the negative terminal.
  • The N-region is connected to the positive terminal.
  • The depletion region generally widens.
  • The electric field across the junction increases.
  • Majority-carrier injection across the junction is suppressed.
  • A small reverse current may flow due to carrier generation and other leakage mechanisms.

Reverse-Bias Diagram

As reverse voltage increases, the electric field within the depletion region increases. At sufficiently high field conditions, breakdown mechanisms can produce a large reverse current.

4. Zener Breakdown

Definition

Zener breakdown is a reverse-breakdown mechanism in a heavily doped semiconductor junction, primarily associated with quantum-mechanical tunneling of electrons across the energy barrier under a strong electric field.

Mechanism

  1. The PN junction is reverse biased.
  2. Heavy doping creates a relatively narrow depletion region.
  3. Reverse voltage produces a strong electric field across the junction.
  4. The field enables electrons to tunnel between energy states across the junction.
  5. The resulting tunneling current increases significantly.

Conceptual Illustration

The illustration is conceptual. Actual Zener tunneling is described through the semiconductor band structure and electric field, not through literal electron movement along a drawn path.

Characteristics of Zener Breakdown

  • Associated primarily with tunneling.
  • Occurs in heavily doped junctions.
  • Depletion region is relatively narrow.
  • Strong electric field is present.
  • Can occur at comparatively low reverse breakdown voltages.
  • Used in voltage-reference and voltage-regulation circuits.
The term "Zener diode" is commonly used for voltage-regulator diodes. In many practical devices, particularly at intermediate breakdown voltages, Zener tunneling and avalanche mechanisms both contribute.

5. Avalanche Breakdown

Definition

Avalanche breakdown occurs when carriers accelerated by a strong electric field gain sufficient energy to cause impact ionization, generating additional electron-hole pairs.

Mechanism

  1. The PN junction is reverse biased.
  2. The electric field within the depletion region increases.
  3. Carriers gain energy from the field.
  4. Energetic carriers cause impact ionization.
  5. Additional electron-hole pairs are generated.
  6. New carriers may also gain energy and cause further impact ionization.
  7. Carrier multiplication leads to a rapidly increasing reverse current.

Conceptual Avalanche Illustration

Characteristics of Avalanche Breakdown

  • Associated with impact ionization.
  • Typically favored by comparatively wider depletion regions.
  • Carriers gain energy from the electric field.
  • Additional carriers are generated by impact ionization.
  • Can occur at comparatively higher reverse breakdown voltages.
  • Used in avalanche diodes and avalanche-rated semiconductor devices.
Avalanche breakdown is not restricted to a single voltage range. The dominant mechanism depends on junction doping, structure, temperature and electric-field distribution.

6. Zener Breakdown vs Avalanche Breakdown

Parameter Zener Breakdown Avalanche Breakdown
Primary mechanism Quantum-mechanical tunneling Impact ionization
Doping Typically heavily doped junction Typically comparatively lighter doping than tunneling-dominated junctions
Depletion width Relatively narrow Relatively wider
Electric field Strong field enables tunneling Field accelerates carriers for impact ionization
Typical voltage range Often associated with lower breakdown voltages Often associated with higher breakdown voltages
Carrier generation Electron tunneling across the junction Impact ionization creates electron-hole pairs
Temperature coefficient Often negative Often positive
Applications Voltage references, regulation Avalanche protection, breakdown devices, specialized circuits
Temperature coefficient: Tunneling-dominated breakdown voltages often decrease with temperature, while avalanche-dominated breakdown voltages often increase. Devices near the transition region may exhibit a small overall temperature coefficient due to the combination of both effects.

7. Doping and Depletion Width

The depletion-region width is strongly influenced by the doping concentration of the semiconductor regions and the applied junction voltage.

In a heavily doped junction, the depletion region is generally narrower. A strong electric field can therefore develop across a relatively small distance.

In a comparatively lightly doped junction, the depletion region is generally wider.

Engineering Interpretation

  • Doping concentration affects the depletion width.
  • Depletion width affects electric-field distribution.
  • Electric field influences the breakdown mechanism.
  • Junction design affects breakdown voltage.
  • Breakdown characteristics must be evaluated using datasheets and circuit design requirements.

8. Reverse V-I Characteristics During Breakdown

In reverse bias, current remains relatively small over a portion of the reverse-voltage range. When breakdown begins, current increases rapidly.

The breakdown region is characterized by a comparatively small change in diode voltage for a large change in current, over an appropriate rated operating range.

Key Observations

  • Reverse current is comparatively small before breakdown in a typical diode characteristic.
  • Breakdown begins when the reverse-voltage magnitude reaches the breakdown region.
  • Current can increase rapidly.
  • Voltage may remain comparatively stable over a suitable operating range in a breakdown-rated device.
  • Current must be limited externally or by the circuit design.

10. Breakdown Voltage, Current and Power

10.1 Breakdown Voltage (VBR)

Breakdown voltage is the reverse voltage at which a specified breakdown-related current or condition is reached, according to the device's measurement definition.

The exact definition may differ between datasheets.

10.2 Breakdown Current

Breakdown current is the reverse current flowing through the diode during breakdown operation.

It must be controlled to keep the device within its safe operating limits.

10.3 Power Dissipation

PD = |VD| × |ID|

In reverse breakdown, the magnitude of reverse voltage multiplied by the magnitude of reverse current gives the approximate electrical power dissipated.

Worked Numerical

A breakdown-rated diode operates at:

  • Reverse voltage = 6 V
  • Reverse current = 20 mA

Calculate the power dissipation.

P = V × I P = 6 × 0.020 P = 0.12 W P = 120 mW
Answer: Power dissipation = 120 mW.
Always verify the device's power rating, thermal conditions, operating current and datasheet limits. A mathematical result does not automatically mean the device can safely operate at that point.

11. Practical Circuit: Reverse Breakdown with Current Limiting

A breakdown-rated diode is commonly used in reverse bias with a series resistor to limit current.

Current Calculation

For a breakdown diode connected in reverse with a series resistor, assuming it is in breakdown and its voltage is approximately constant:

I ≈ (VS − VBR)/R

Worked Example

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 12 V
  • Breakdown voltage = 5.6 V
  • Series resistor = 1 kΩ
I = (VS - VBR) / R I = (12 - 5.6) / 1000 I = 6.4 / 1000 I = 0.0064 A I = 6.4 mA
Answer: The approximate current is 6.4 mA, assuming the diode is in breakdown and its voltage is approximately 5.6 V at that current.

Power Calculations

Diode power:

PZ = VBR × I PZ = 5.6 × 0.0064 PZ = 35.84 mW

Resistor power:

PR = I²R PR = (0.0064)² × 1000 PR = 0.04096 W PR ≈ 40.96 mW

Supply power:

PS = VS × I PS = 12 × 0.0064 PS = 76.8 mW
The supply power is approximately equal to the sum of resistor and diode power under these assumptions: 40.96 mW + 35.84 mW = 76.8 mW.

12. Temperature Effects on Breakdown

Temperature dependence is an important property of semiconductor breakdown mechanisms.

Mechanism Typical Temperature Trend
Zener tunneling dominated Breakdown voltage often decreases with increasing temperature
Avalanche dominated Breakdown voltage often increases with increasing temperature
Mixed mechanisms Temperature coefficient depends on the relative contribution of each mechanism

Why?

Zener tunneling: The temperature dependence of band structure and carrier distribution generally results in a negative temperature coefficient for tunneling-dominated breakdown.

Avalanche breakdown: Increasing temperature generally increases carrier scattering, affecting the energy required for impact ionization and commonly producing a positive breakdown-voltage temperature coefficient.

Many reference diodes near the transition between dominant mechanisms exhibit a relatively small temperature coefficient. The exact temperature behavior must be obtained from the device datasheet.

13. Practical Testing of Diodes

13.1 Multimeter Diode Mode

Diode mode is commonly used to test forward conduction and detect some short-circuit or open-circuit faults.

  • Forward direction generally produces a voltage reading.
  • Reverse direction on a typical ordinary diode may show OL.
  • Shorted diodes may show conduction in both directions.
  • Open diodes may show OL in both directions.
A multimeter diode test does not normally establish the breakdown voltage of a diode. Its test voltage may be far below the breakdown voltage.

13.2 Testing Breakdown Characteristics

Breakdown characteristics require suitable equipment, controlled reverse bias, current limiting and appropriate measurement procedures.

  • Use a breakdown-rated diode or test sample.
  • Use an appropriate current-limiting circuit.
  • Use a controlled supply or suitable test instrument.
  • Measure voltage and current under defined conditions.
  • Observe power and thermal limits.
  • Do not exceed the rated reverse voltage of an ordinary diode.
Do not perform breakdown testing on an ordinary diode without understanding its reverse-voltage rating, power rating, test setup and current limiting.

14. Troubleshooting Breakdown-Related Circuits

Fault Possible Cause Suggested Check
Excessive reverse current Insufficient series resistance, damaged diode, incorrect supply Check resistor, supply voltage and device condition
Output voltage is not regulated Diode not in breakdown, insufficient current, excessive load Check operating current and circuit calculations
Diode overheats Excessive current or power dissipation Calculate power and verify ratings
Breakdown voltage differs from expected Device tolerance, temperature, measurement conditions Check datasheet and test conditions
Diode appears shorted Thermal or electrical damage Test after removing from circuit if necessary
Unexpected leakage Temperature, surface leakage, damaged junction Inspect and test under controlled conditions
Incorrect breakdown current Incorrect resistor, supply or measurement Verify circuit values and measurements

15. Placement Interview Questions

Q1. What is diode breakdown?

Diode breakdown is a reverse-bias phenomenon in which current increases rapidly after a breakdown condition is reached.

Q2. What is Zener breakdown?

Zener breakdown is a reverse-breakdown mechanism primarily associated with quantum-mechanical tunneling in a strongly electrically stressed, heavily doped junction.

Q3. What is avalanche breakdown?

Avalanche breakdown occurs through impact ionization and carrier multiplication in a strong electric field.

Q4. What is the main difference between Zener and avalanche breakdown?

Zener breakdown is primarily associated with tunneling, while avalanche breakdown is primarily associated with impact ionization.

Q5. Why is a Zener junction heavily doped?

Heavy doping generally produces a narrow depletion region, facilitating tunneling under a strong electric field.

Q6. Why is avalanche breakdown associated with impact ionization?

Carriers gain energy from the electric field and generate additional carriers through impact ionization.

Q7. What is breakdown voltage?

It is the reverse voltage associated with a specified breakdown condition, as defined for the device.

Q8. Can breakdown damage a diode?

Yes, if current, power or temperature limits are exceeded. Breakdown-rated devices can operate in their breakdown region under specified conditions.

Q9. Why is a resistor used with a breakdown diode?

It limits current and helps control power dissipation.

Q10. What is the depletion region?

It is the region near a PN junction depleted of mobile majority carriers and containing the space-charge distribution.

Q11. How does doping affect depletion width?

Higher doping generally reduces depletion width, while lower doping generally increases it, subject to junction structure and bias.

Q12. What is the temperature coefficient of Zener breakdown?

Tunneling-dominated breakdown typically has a negative temperature coefficient.

Q13. What is the temperature coefficient of avalanche breakdown?

Avalanche-dominated breakdown typically has a positive temperature coefficient.

Q14. Why are some reference diodes temperature compensated?

Because combining mechanisms with different temperature coefficients can reduce the net temperature dependence over a specified range.

Q15. Is a Zener diode always based only on Zener tunneling?

No. The name is commonly used for breakdown reference diodes, and avalanche may also contribute, particularly at intermediate breakdown voltages.

Q16. Can an ordinary diode be tested for breakdown using a multimeter?

Usually not with a standard diode-mode test because the test voltage may not reach the diode's breakdown voltage.

Q17. What is impact ionization?

It is a process in which an energetic carrier creates additional electron-hole pairs through collision with the semiconductor lattice.

Q18. What is carrier multiplication?

It is the increase in carrier population due to processes such as impact ionization.

Q19. Why must breakdown current be limited?

Excessive current can cause overheating, excessive power dissipation and device damage.

Q20. What is the power equation for a reverse-biased breakdown diode?

P ≈ |VBR| × |IBR|

16. Placement-Oriented Numerical Practice

Problem 1: Breakdown Power

A breakdown diode operates at 8 V and 15 mA. Calculate the power dissipation.

Show Answer
P = V × I P = 8 × 0.015 P = 0.12 W P = 120 mW

Problem 2: Series Resistor

A 10 V supply is connected to a 4.7 V breakdown diode through a 1 kΩ resistor. Assuming breakdown operation and a constant diode voltage, calculate current.

Show Answer
I = (10 - 4.7) / 1000 I = 5.3 / 1000 I = 5.3 mA

Problem 3: Resistor Power

Calculate the resistor power in Problem 2.

Show Answer
PR = I²R PR = (0.0053)² × 1000 PR = 0.02809 W PR ≈ 28.09 mW

Problem 4: Breakdown Diode Power

Calculate breakdown diode power for a 4.7 V diode carrying 5.3 mA.

Show Answer
PZ = VZ × IZ PZ = 4.7 × 0.0053 PZ = 0.02491 W PZ ≈ 24.91 mW

Problem 5: Current Limiting

A 15 V supply is connected to a 6.2 V breakdown diode. The required nominal current is 10 mA. Calculate the approximate series resistor.

Show Answer
R = (VS - VZ) / I R = (15 - 6.2) / 0.010 R = 8.8 / 0.010 R = 880 Ω

Problem 6: Resistor Selection

For Problem 5, select a nearby standard resistor value of 910 Ω and calculate the approximate current.

Show Answer
I = (15 - 6.2) / 910 I = 8.8 / 910 I ≈ 9.67 mA

Problem 7: Maximum Resistor Power

For Problem 6, calculate the resistor power.

Show Answer
PR = I²R PR = (0.00967)² × 910 PR ≈ 0.0851 W PR ≈ 85.1 mW

Problem 8: Power Check

For Problem 6, calculate the diode power.

Show Answer
PZ = VZ × IZ PZ = 6.2 × 0.00967 PZ ≈ 0.05995 W PZ ≈ 60 mW
