2. Diode as an Electronic Switch

2.1 Introduction

A diode can operate as an electronic switch because its conductivity depends on the applied voltage polarity. It allows current primarily in the forward direction and blocks current in the reverse direction.

3. Diode Switching Characteristics

3.1 Forward Recovery

When a diode changes from the OFF state to the ON state, its forward voltage does not necessarily reach its steady-state value instantaneously. The transient settling behavior is associated with forward recovery.

3.2 Reverse Recovery

When a conducting PN diode is suddenly reverse biased, stored minority carriers must be removed or recombine before the diode fully blocks reverse current. During this interval, reverse current flows.

3.3 Reverse Recovery Time (trr)

Reverse recovery time is the interval associated with the reverse current transient after the diode is switched from forward conduction to reverse bias.

3.4 Important Parameters

ParameterMeaning
trrReverse recovery time
IRRMPeak reverse recovery current
QrrReverse recovery charge
IFForward current
VRReverse voltage
Qrr = ∫ |IR(t)| dt

Reverse recovery charge is the magnitude of charge associated with the reverse recovery current waveform.

3.5 Soft and Abrupt Recovery

  • Soft recovery: Reverse current decays gradually.
  • Abrupt recovery: Reverse current falls relatively sharply.

Recovery behavior can influence voltage overshoot, ringing, electromagnetic interference and switching losses.

5. Flyback Diode

5.1 Introduction

A flyback diode is connected across an inductive load such as a relay coil, solenoid or DC motor. It provides a path for the inductor current when the driving switch turns OFF.

5.2 Why Is It Required?

An inductor opposes sudden changes in current. When the circuit is switched OFF, the inductor generates a voltage that attempts to maintain current flow. Without a suitable current path, this voltage can become large enough to damage a transistor or MOSFET.

VL = L × (di/dt)

5.3 Flyback Diode Circuit

In a conventional low-side switched DC relay circuit, the flyback diode is connected in reverse bias across the coil during normal energization. Its cathode is connected to the positive coil supply and its anode to the transistor-side coil terminal.

5.4 Operation

  1. When the transistor turns ON, coil current flows.
  2. The flyback diode remains reverse biased.
  3. When the transistor turns OFF, coil current attempts to continue.
  4. The coil voltage reverses polarity.
  5. The flyback diode becomes forward biased.
  6. Current circulates through the coil and diode, decaying over time.

5.5 Inductor Energy

E = ½ LI²

Example:

  • Inductance = 100 mH
  • Initial coil current = 100 mA
E = 0.5 × 0.1 × (0.1)²
E = 0.0005 J = 0.5 mJ

5.6 Flyback Diode Selection

  • Forward current capability must support coil current.
  • Reverse voltage rating must exceed the normal supply voltage with suitable margin.
  • Recovery characteristics should suit the switching frequency.
  • Surge and thermal ratings must be checked.
A simple flyback diode can slow relay release because it limits the coil's reverse voltage. Where fast release is needed, a properly designed TVS or diode-Zener clamp may be more suitable.

7. Reverse Polarity Protection

7.1 Purpose

Reverse polarity protection prevents damage when a DC supply is accidentally connected with the wrong polarity.

7.2 Series Diode Protection

A diode is placed in series with the supply. Under correct polarity it conducts; under reverse polarity it blocks current.

7.3 Advantages and Disadvantages

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Simple circuitForward voltage loss
Low component countPower dissipation
Blocks reverse supplyMay be unsuitable for high-current applications

7.4 MOSFET-Based Protection

A P-channel or N-channel MOSFET can be arranged as an ideal-diode style reverse-polarity protection circuit. Its low ON-state resistance can reduce voltage drop compared with a series diode.

The MOSFET's body diode and gate-source voltage limits must be considered during design. Dedicated ideal-diode controllers are also used in demanding applications.

7.5 Applications

  • Battery-powered circuits
  • Automotive electronics
  • Embedded systems
  • DC adapters
  • Portable electronic devices

8. TVS Diode (Transient Voltage Suppression)

8.1 Introduction

A TVS diode is designed to protect electronic circuits against short-duration voltage transients. It responds rapidly to overvoltage events and diverts surge current away from the protected circuit.

8.2 Working Principle

  1. During normal operation, the TVS remains non-conductive or has very low leakage.
  2. A transient raises the voltage beyond its breakdown region.
  3. The TVS conducts strongly.
  4. It clamps the protected node to a limited voltage.
  5. After the transient, it returns to its normal state if it has not been damaged.

8.3 Unidirectional and Bidirectional TVS

TypeBehaviorApplications
UnidirectionalProtects one polarity of transient with a diode-like reverse clampDC supply lines
BidirectionalClamps transients of both polaritiesAC and signal lines

8.6 TVS Selection

  • Choose VRWM above the maximum normal operating voltage.
  • Check VBR and VC against the protected device's voltage limits.
  • Check peak pulse current and pulse power rating.
  • Consider pulse waveform and repetition rate.
  • Use short, low-inductance PCB connections.
  • Consider placement close to the connector or transient entry point.
A TVS diode does not guarantee protection against every surge. The source impedance, pulse energy, PCB layout, fuse and protected device limits all matter.

8.7 Applications

  • USB and communication interfaces
  • Automotive power lines
  • Industrial control equipment
  • Sensor interfaces
  • Power supply inputs
  • ESD and surge protection

10. Other Diode Protection Applications

10.1 Inductive Load Protection

Relays, solenoids and motors generate inductive kickback when their current is interrupted. A flyback diode or a suitable clamp network protects the switching transistor.

10.2 Input Overvoltage Clamping

Zener and TVS diodes can limit voltage excursions. Zener regulators are generally intended for continuous regulation within their power ratings; TVS devices are designed primarily for transient suppression.

10.3 Signal-Line Protection

Protection diodes can clamp input signals to safe voltage rails. The clamp current must be limited, and the protection device must be selected for the interface's capacitance, bandwidth and surge requirements.

10.4 Flyback Diode vs TVS Diode

11. Practical Laboratory Experiments

Experiment 1: Diode Switching

Objective: Observe diode ON and OFF behavior with a changing input.

  1. Connect a diode and a current-limiting resistor.
  2. Apply a low-voltage square wave.
  3. Observe input and output waveforms.
  4. Measure the forward and reverse states.

Experiment 2: Flyback Diode Protection

Objective: Study the effect of a flyback diode across a relay coil.

  1. Connect a low-voltage relay coil to a suitable transistor driver.
  2. Connect a flyback diode across the coil with correct polarity.
  3. Switch the transistor OFF.
  4. Observe the switching-node voltage with suitable equipment.
  5. Compare behavior with a properly designed protected test circuit.

Experiment 3: Reverse Polarity Protection

Objective: Verify series diode protection.

  1. Build a low-voltage series diode circuit.
  2. Apply correct supply polarity and measure output.
  3. Remove power before reversing supply polarity.
  4. Measure the protected output again.

Experiment 4: TVS Diode Clamping

Objective: Observe transient voltage clamping.

  1. Use a suitable current-limited pulse source and TVS device.
  2. Connect the TVS across the protected test line.
  3. Apply a controlled transient within device ratings.
  4. Observe the clamping waveform.
  5. Compare with the device datasheet.
Do not deliberately generate uncontrolled high-voltage surges. Use an appropriately rated pulse generator and laboratory protection equipment. Never perform these tests directly on mains.

13. Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Inductor Stored Energy

An inductor of 200 mH carries 0.5 A. Calculate stored energy.

Show Solution
E = ½LI²
= 0.5 × 0.2 × 0.5²
= 0.025 J = 25 mJ

Problem 2: Approximate Reverse Recovery Charge

A diode has an approximately triangular reverse current pulse with peak magnitude 2 A and duration 1 µs. Estimate Qrr.

Show Solution
Qrr ≈ ½ × 2 × 1 µs
= 1 µC

Problem 3: Flyback Current Decay

A 100 mH coil carries 200 mA. A flyback diode provides an approximately constant 0.7 V clamp. Estimate the time for the current to decay to zero.

Show Solution
t ≈ LI / V
= 0.1 × 0.2 / 0.7
≈ 0.0286 s = 28.6 ms

Problem 4: Series Diode Power

A silicon protection diode has a forward drop of 0.7 V and carries 500 mA. Calculate its approximate power dissipation.

Show Solution
P = VF × I
= 0.7 × 0.5
= 0.35 W

Problem 5: TVS Selection Concept

A DC circuit operates at a maximum normal voltage of 12 V. Explain why a TVS stand-off voltage must be selected above the maximum normal voltage.

Show Solution

The TVS must remain in its non-conducting operating region during normal operation. Its stand-off voltage is therefore selected above the maximum expected normal voltage, with appropriate allowance for supply tolerance and transients. The clamping voltage must also remain compatible with the protected circuit's maximum voltage rating.
