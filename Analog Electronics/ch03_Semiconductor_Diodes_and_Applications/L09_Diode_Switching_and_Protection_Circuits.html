Chapter 3: Semiconductor Diodes and Applications

Lesson 9: Diode Switching and Protection Circuits

Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Explain diode operation as an electronic switch.

1. Introduction to Diode Switching

A semiconductor diode can operate as an electronic switch because it conducts current in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias under normal operating conditions.

Operating Condition Diode State Ideal Equivalent
Forward biased ON Closed switch
Reverse biased OFF Open switch
Placement Focus: A diode is not an ideal switch. Its forward voltage, leakage current, junction capacitance and switching time affect real circuit operation.

2. Diode as an Electronic Switch

Forward Bias – ON State

When the anode is sufficiently positive relative to the cathode, the diode conducts current.

  • The depletion region becomes narrower.
  • The junction barrier is reduced.
  • Current flows through the diode.
  • A practical silicon diode has a forward voltage drop.

Reverse Bias – OFF State

When the anode is negative relative to the cathode, the diode normally blocks current.

  • The depletion region becomes wider.
  • Only a small reverse leakage current flows.
  • The diode blocks normal load current.
       Diode Switching Concept

       Forward Bias       Reverse Bias
          ON                  OFF

       Anode +             Anode -
          |                   |
        --|>|--             --|>|--
          |                   |
       Current             Leakage only
       flows               (normally)

3. Diode Switching Characteristics

When a diode changes from ON to OFF or OFF to ON, its current and voltage do not change instantaneously. The transition requires a finite amount of time.

Important Switching Parameters

Parameter Description
Forward Recovery Time Time required for the forward voltage to settle after switching into conduction.
Reverse Recovery Time (trr) Time required for reverse current to return to a specified low value after forward conduction.
Junction Capacitance Capacitance associated with the semiconductor junction.
Reverse Leakage Current Small current flowing under reverse bias.

Reverse Recovery

In a PN junction diode, charge carriers stored during forward conduction must be removed before the diode can fully block reverse voltage.

During this transition, a temporary reverse current flows. This phenomenon is called reverse recovery.

Reverse Recovery Time:

trr = ta + tb

Here, ta and tb represent the storage and decay portions used in a common reverse recovery measurement.

A smaller reverse recovery time is desirable in many high-frequency switching applications.

4. Fast Recovery and Schottky Diodes

Different diode technologies are selected according to switching frequency, voltage rating, current rating and conduction losses.

Diode Type Important Characteristic Typical Use
General-purpose PN diode Suitable for ordinary rectification. Low-frequency power supplies.
Fast-recovery diode Shorter reverse recovery time. Switch-mode power converters.
Ultrafast diode Very short reverse recovery time. High-frequency switching circuits.
Schottky diode Majority-carrier conduction and negligible minority-carrier storage. Low-voltage, high-speed rectification.
A Schottky diode can have higher reverse leakage and lower reverse-voltage capability than some PN diodes. Selection must consider the complete datasheet.

5. Freewheeling Diode (Flyback Diode)

A freewheeling diode provides a path for inductive current when the supply or switching device is turned OFF.

Inductive loads such as relays, motors and solenoids store energy in their magnetic fields. When the current path is interrupted, the inductor can generate a large voltage spike. 

        Relay / Inductive Load Protection

             +V
              |
          +---+------+
          |          |
         Coil       |<|  Flyback Diode
          |          |
          +----+-----+
               |
          Switching Device
               |
              GND

Working Principle

  • When the switch is ON, current flows through the coil.
  • The flyback diode is normally reverse biased.
  • When the switch turns OFF, the coil generates a voltage that attempts to maintain current.
  • The diode becomes forward biased and provides a recirculating current path.
  • The inductive energy is dissipated through the coil and protection circuit.
Inductor Energy:

E = 1/2 × L × I²

Where:

  • L = Inductance in henries.
  • I = Current immediately before switching OFF.

Applications

  • Relay driver circuits.
  • Solenoid control.
  • DC motor switching.
  • Electromagnetic actuators.
  • Embedded controller output circuits.
A simple flyback diode can slow the release of a relay or solenoid because it allows current to decay gradually. Faster-release designs may use a TVS diode or other higher-voltage clamp arrangement.

6. Reverse Polarity Protection

Reverse polarity protection prevents damage when a DC power supply is accidentally connected with the wrong polarity.

Series Diode Protection

A diode is connected in series with the positive supply. It conducts when the supply polarity is correct and blocks current when the supply is reversed. 

       Series Reverse Polarity Protection

       +V Supply
           |
          |>|  Protection Diode
           |
           +------ Load
           |
          GND

Advantages

  • Simple circuit.
  • Provides basic reverse polarity protection.
  • Easy to implement.

Limitation

The series diode introduces a forward voltage drop and corresponding power loss.

PD = VF × I

Schottky Diode or MOSFET Protection

A Schottky diode can reduce the forward voltage loss. For higher-current applications, a correctly designed MOSFET ideal-diode circuit can provide lower conduction losses.

7. Input Voltage Clamping Protection

A clamping circuit limits a voltage excursion to protect sensitive electronic inputs.

Diodes can be connected to supply rails or reference voltages to restrict the input voltage range. 

       Conceptual Input Clamp

              +VCC
                |
             Clamp Diode
                |
       Input ---+------ Protected Input
                |
             Clamp Diode
                |
               GND

In a practical circuit, the clamp diodes conduct when the input exceeds the permitted voltage range. A series resistor or other current-limiting element is usually needed to control clamp current.

The input must not be driven beyond the protection circuit's current and voltage ratings. Ordinary signal clamp diodes are not a substitute for properly selected TVS protection against high-energy surges.

8. TVS Diode Protection

A Transient Voltage Suppressor (TVS) diode protects electronic circuits from short-duration voltage transients caused by ESD, switching and surge events.

Working Principle

  • Under normal operating voltage, the TVS remains in its non-conducting region.
  • When a transient exceeds its breakdown region, it conducts strongly.
  • It diverts transient current and limits the voltage across the protected circuit.
  • After the transient disappears, it returns to its normal state if it has not been damaged.

Important Selection Parameters

Parameter Meaning
VRWM Maximum working peak reverse voltage.
VBR Breakdown voltage under specified test conditions.
VC Clamping voltage at a specified peak pulse current.
IPP Peak pulse current rating.

Applications

  • UART and communication interfaces.
  • CAN bus protection.
  • USB port protection.
  • Automotive electronics.
  • Power supply input protection.
Embedded Systems Relevance: External interfaces can be exposed to ESD and electrical transients. TVS protection is commonly used near connectors to protect microcontrollers and communication transceivers.

9. Overvoltage and Transient Protection

Electronic circuits can experience overvoltage conditions due to inductive switching, electrostatic discharge, lightning-induced surges and wiring faults.

Protection Method Purpose
TVS Diode Fast transient and ESD suppression.
Zener Diode Voltage reference and low-power voltage limiting.
Flyback Diode Suppresses inductive switching voltage across a load.
Series Diode Basic reverse polarity protection.
Clamp Diodes Limit signal voltage to defined rails or references.

10. Numerical Problems

Example 1: Diode Power Loss

A silicon diode carries 500 mA with a forward voltage drop of 0.7 V. Calculate the power dissipated.

P = VF × I

P = 0.7 × 0.5

P = 0.35 W
Answer: Diode power dissipation = 0.35 W.

Example 2: Flyback Diode Energy

An inductive load has an inductance of 100 mH and carries 200 mA immediately before switching OFF. Calculate its stored magnetic energy.

E = 1/2 × L × I²

E = 0.5 × 0.1 × (0.2)²

E = 0.002 J
Answer: Stored energy = 2 mJ.

11. Practical Design Considerations

  • Select a diode with adequate forward current rating.
  • Verify maximum reverse voltage.
  • Check reverse recovery time for switching applications.
  • Check power dissipation and thermal limits.
  • Consider reverse leakage at the expected temperature.
  • Use suitable flyback protection for inductive loads.
  • Choose TVS clamping voltage compatible with the protected circuit.
  • Consider PCB layout and transient-current return paths.
A protection diode must be selected for the actual fault waveform, energy, source impedance and protected circuit voltage limits. A voltage rating alone is not sufficient.

12. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. How does a diode work as a switch?

It conducts in forward bias and blocks normal current in reverse bias.

Q2. What is reverse recovery time?

It is the time required for a PN junction diode to recover its reverse-blocking capability after forward conduction.

Q3. What is a freewheeling diode?

It provides a path for inductive current when the switching device is turned OFF.

Q4. Why is a flyback diode connected across a relay coil?

It limits the voltage spike generated by the coil when current is interrupted.

Q5. What happens if a relay coil has no flyback protection?

The inductive voltage spike may damage the switching transistor or other connected components.

Q6. What is reverse polarity protection?

It prevents damage when a DC supply is connected with incorrect polarity.

Q7. What is a TVS diode?

A TVS diode is used to suppress short-duration voltage transients.

Q8. Why are fast recovery diodes used in SMPS?

Their shorter reverse recovery time reduces switching losses and unwanted current during high-frequency operation.

Q9. What is the difference between a Zener diode and a TVS diode?

Zener diodes are commonly used for voltage references and regulation, while TVS diodes are designed to handle transient suppression events.

Q10. Why is a Schottky diode useful in switching circuits?

It has low forward voltage and avoids the usual minority-carrier storage associated with PN junction reverse recovery.
