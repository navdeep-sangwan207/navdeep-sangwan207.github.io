2. Identifying a Diode

2.1 Diode Terminals

A conventional diode has two terminals:

  • Anode: Current enters the diode through the anode under conventional forward-current operation.
  • Cathode: Current leaves the diode under conventional forward-current operation.

2.2 Physical Identification

For many axial rectifier diodes, a band or stripe marks the cathode. Always confirm the manufacturer's package drawing, especially for SMD and unusual packages.

3. Testing a Diode Using a Digital Multimeter

3.1 Diode Test Mode

A digital multimeter in diode-test mode applies a small test current and displays the voltage drop across the device.

3.2 Testing Procedure

  1. Switch OFF the circuit power.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely.
  3. Set the multimeter to diode-test mode.
  4. Connect the red probe to the anode.
  5. Connect the black probe to the cathode.
  6. Record the forward voltage reading.
  7. Reverse the probes and record the reverse reading.

3.3 Expected Readings

ConditionTypical ResultInterpretation
Red to anode, black to cathodeForward voltageNormal forward conduction
Red to cathode, black to anodeOL on many metersNormal reverse blocking
Low reading in both directionsNear zeroPossible short circuit
OL in both directionsOpen circuitPossible open diode or poor contact

Typical silicon PN diode forward voltage is approximately 0.5–0.8 V under common test conditions. Schottky diode readings are often lower. Actual values depend on device current, temperature and meter design.

3.4 Why Test Out of Circuit?

Other components connected in parallel or through semiconductor junctions can create alternative current paths. This may produce misleading multimeter readings.

When an in-circuit result is ambiguous, isolate at least one diode terminal before making a final judgment.

Never use resistance or diode-test mode on an energized circuit. Confirm that capacitors are discharged before testing.

5. Understanding a Diode Datasheet

A datasheet contains the electrical, thermal, mechanical and reliability information needed to select a diode for an actual circuit.

5.1 Important Parameters

IFSMNon-repetitive peak forward surge currentShort-duration surge capability
VFForward voltage at specified currentConduction loss

trrReverse recovery timeSwitching performance

TJMaximum junction temperatureReliability and operating limit
RθJAJunction-to-ambient thermal resistanceTemperature estimation

5.2 Example: 1N4007

The 1N4007 is a widely used general-purpose rectifier diode. Its commonly specified repetitive peak reverse voltage is 1000 V. Always consult the exact manufacturer's datasheet for current, surge, thermal and package limits.

5.3 Datasheet Selection Checklist

  • Reverse-voltage rating with adequate margin.
  • Average and peak forward current requirements.
  • Forward voltage at the actual operating current.
  • Reverse leakage at the maximum expected temperature.
  • Reverse recovery characteristics for switching circuits.
  • Power dissipation and thermal resistance.
  • Package, mounting and PCB footprint compatibility.
  • Surge and repetitive pulse ratings.

5.4 Important Design Distinction

A maximum voltage or current rating is not necessarily a recommended continuous operating point. Ratings depend on specified test conditions, mounting, temperature and waveform.

6. Practical Diode Circuit: Half-Wave Rectifier

6.1 Circuit Diagram

6.2 Operation

  • During the positive half-cycle, the diode conducts.
  • During the negative half-cycle, the diode blocks.
  • The load receives a pulsating unidirectional waveform.

6.3 Important Equations for an Ideal Diode and Resistive Load

VDC = Vm / π
VRMS = Vm / 2
Ripple frequency = Supply frequency

For a practical diode, the forward drop and source impedance reduce the actual output. A filter capacitor also changes the waveform and requires separate analysis.

6.4 Numerical Example

An ideal half-wave rectifier receives a sinusoidal input with a peak voltage of 20 V. Calculate average output voltage.

VDC = Vm / π
= 20 / 3.1416
= 6.37 V

7. Practical Diode Circuit: Bridge Rectifier

7.1 Operation

A bridge rectifier uses four diodes. Two diodes conduct during each half-cycle, producing full-wave rectification across the load.

7.2 Ideal Full-Wave Equations

VDC = 2Vm / π
VRMS = Vm / √2
Ripple frequency = 2 × Supply frequency

7.3 Practical Consideration

Two diode forward drops are present in the conducting path. Therefore, the approximate peak load voltage is:

Vpeak,load ≈ Vm - 2VF

The exact waveform and average output depend on load, source impedance, diode characteristics and filtering.

A Zener regulator is operating correctly only when its current remains within the specified operating range and the Zener, resistor and load ratings are respected.

11. Component Replacement and Selection

11.1 Can Any Diode Replace Another?

No. Two diodes with the same package or similar appearance may have very different electrical and thermal specifications.

11.2 Replacement Checklist

  • Equal or higher suitable reverse-voltage rating.
  • Equal or higher suitable forward-current rating.
  • Compatible surge-current rating.
  • Appropriate forward voltage and power dissipation.
  • Suitable reverse recovery behavior.
  • Compatible package and PCB footprint.
  • Acceptable thermal characteristics.

11.3 Example: 1N4001 vs 1N4007

Both are members of the 1N400x general-purpose rectifier family. The 1N4007 has a much higher repetitive peak reverse voltage rating than the 1N4001. However, voltage rating alone does not establish complete interchangeability: current, surge, thermal, package and circuit requirements must also be checked.

Never select a replacement solely by matching the part's physical size or appearance. Consult the manufacturer's datasheet and the circuit's operating conditions.

12.1 Oscilloscope Measurements

An oscilloscope is useful for observing:

  • Rectifier output ripple.
  • Diode switching transients.
  • Reverse recovery current (with a suitable current-measurement setup).
  • Flyback voltage waveforms.
  • TVS clamping behavior.
Oscilloscope ground clips are commonly connected to protective earth. Incorrect grounding can short a circuit node. Use appropriate differential probes or isolated measurement equipment when required. Do not connect a grounded probe directly to hazardous mains circuitry.

13. Laboratory Exercises

Experiment 1: Diode Identification and Testing

Objective: Identify diode terminals and verify forward/reverse behavior.

Equipment: Digital multimeter and suitable low-voltage diode.

  1. Identify the cathode marking.
  2. Test forward voltage using diode mode.
  3. Reverse the probes and record the reading.
  4. Compare results with the datasheet.

Experiment 2: Half-Wave Rectifier

Objective: Observe half-wave rectification.

  1. Build a low-voltage isolated AC rectifier circuit.
  2. Observe input and output waveforms.
  3. Measure average output voltage.
  4. Compare measured and calculated results.

Experiment 3: Bridge Rectifier

Objective: Verify full-wave rectification.

  1. Connect four diodes in bridge configuration.
  2. Apply a suitable isolated AC input.
  3. Observe the load waveform.
  4. Compare ripple frequency with the input frequency.

Experiment 4: Zener Voltage Regulation

Objective: Verify Zener regulation under different loads.

  1. Build a current-limited Zener regulator.
  2. Measure output voltage without overload.
  3. Vary the load resistance within the design limits.
  4. Measure series and Zener currents.
  5. Verify power dissipation.

Experiment 5: Flyback Protection

Objective: Observe inductive-load switching protection.

  1. Use a low-voltage relay and suitable transistor driver.
  2. Install a correctly rated flyback diode.
  3. Switch the relay and observe the switching node.
  4. Check the transistor voltage remains within its safe operating limits.

14. Placement-Oriented Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Forward Power Dissipation

A diode carries 1.2 A with a forward drop of 0.8 V. Calculate its power dissipation.

Show Solution
P = VF × IF
= 0.8 × 1.2
= 0.96 W

Problem 2: Rectifier Output

An ideal half-wave rectifier has an input peak voltage of 15 V. Calculate its average output voltage.

Show Solution
VDC = Vm / π
= 15 / 3.1416
= 4.77 V

Problem 3: Zener Current

A 5.1 V Zener regulator has a 470 Ω series resistor and 12 V input. The load draws 5 mA. Calculate Zener current.

Show Solution
IR = (12 - 5.1) / 470
= 14.68 mA

IZ = IR - IL
= 14.68 - 5
= 9.68 mA

Problem 4: Zener Power

Using the previous result, calculate Zener power.

Show Solution
PZ = VZ × IZ
= 5.1 × 0.00968
≈ 49.4 mW

Problem 5: Bridge Rectifier Peak Output

An AC source has a peak voltage of 18 V. Each conducting silicon diode has a forward drop of 0.7 V. Estimate the peak load voltage.

Show Solution
Vpeak = Vm - 2VF
= 18 - 1.4
= 16.6 V

15. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. How do you identify the cathode of an axial diode?

For many axial rectifier diodes, a band or stripe identifies the cathode. Confirm the package drawing.

Q2. How do you test a diode using a multimeter?

Use diode-test mode. A healthy diode normally shows a forward voltage in one direction and an open indication in the other.

Q3. What does OL indicate in diode mode?

It indicates that the meter cannot establish a conducting path under its test conditions. It does not alone prove that the component is faulty.

Q4. What is a shorted diode?

A diode that conducts with very low resistance in both directions under the test conditions.

Q5. What is an open diode?

A diode that fails to conduct in either direction under the test conditions.

Q6. Why can in-circuit diode testing be inaccurate?

Parallel components and alternative current paths can affect the meter reading.

Q7. What is VRRM?

Maximum repetitive peak reverse voltage specified by the manufacturer.

Q8. What is IFSM?

Maximum specified non-repetitive peak forward surge current under defined conditions.

Q9. Why is reverse leakage important?

It contributes to standby loss and can increase significantly with temperature.

Q10. Why does a rectifier diode overheat?

Possible reasons include excessive forward current, high conduction loss, reverse stress, poor thermal conditions or repetitive surges.

Q11. Why does a bridge rectifier have two diode drops?

Two diodes are in the conducting path during each half-cycle.

Q12. How do you identify a faulty bridge rectifier?

Test each junction with the circuit isolated and compare the input and output waveforms.

Q13. What happens if a filter capacitor fails open?

Output ripple generally increases significantly.

Q14. What happens if a Zener diode fails short?

The regulated output can collapse and the series resistor may experience excessive current and heating.

Q15. What happens if a Zener diode fails open?

The output may rise toward the unregulated value, depending on the circuit and load.

Q16. Why is a flyback diode used with a relay?

It provides a current path for the coil after switch-off and limits the inductive voltage spike.

Q17. Why can a flyback diode slow relay release?

Its low clamp voltage allows the coil current to decay relatively slowly.

Q18. What is the purpose of a TVS diode?

It suppresses short-duration voltage transients by conducting strongly during an overvoltage event.

Q19. What is the difference between VRWM and VC?

VRWM is the stand-off voltage for normal operation; VC is the clamping voltage at a specified pulse current.

Q20. What should be checked before replacing a diode?

Voltage, current, surge, recovery, forward drop, leakage, power, thermal and package ratings.

16. Practical Troubleshooting Interview Scenarios

Scenario 1: A DC power supply has no output.

Expected approach: Check the input supply and fuse, inspect the rectifier, test the diodes, check filter capacitors and verify PCB connections.

Scenario 2: A rectifier output is lower than expected.

Expected approach: Measure AC input, diode drops, load current and capacitor ripple. Check for faulty diodes or excessive loading.

Scenario 3: A relay driver transistor repeatedly fails.

Expected approach: Inspect the flyback path, switching-node transient, transistor voltage rating, current rating and layout.

Scenario 4: A Zener regulator works without load but fails under load.

Expected approach: Calculate the minimum available series current, load current and required minimum Zener current. Verify the design at worst-case input and load conditions.

Scenario 5: A diode becomes hot after replacement.

Expected approach: Confirm correct type and orientation, measure current and forward voltage, calculate dissipation and check thermal mounting.
