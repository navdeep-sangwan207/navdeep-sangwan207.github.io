1. Light Emitting Diode (LED)

1.1 Introduction

A Light Emitting Diode (LED) is a semiconductor device that converts electrical energy into light energy when forward biased. The phenomenon is called electroluminescence.

1.2 Working Principle

  1. The LED is forward biased.
  2. Electrons move from the N-region towards the junction.
  3. Holes move from the P-region towards the junction.
  4. Electrons and holes recombine.
  5. Energy is released in the form of photons.

1.4 LED Circuit

1.5 LED Current Limiting Resistor

R = (VSupply − VLED) / ILED

Example:

  • Supply voltage = 5 V
  • LED forward voltage = 2 V
  • Desired current = 10 mA
R = (5 − 2) / 0.01 = 300 Ω

A standard 330 Ω resistor can be selected.

ILED = (5 − 2) / 330 ≈ 9.09 mA
PR = I²R ≈ 27.3 mW

A resistor rated at 1/4 W is suitable for this illustrative circuit.

Never connect a typical LED directly to a voltage source without appropriate current limiting or a current-controlled driver.

1.8 PWM LED Brightness Control

Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) controls LED brightness by varying the duty cycle of the signal.

Duty Cycle = (Ton / T) × 100%

Increasing the duty cycle increases the average electrical power delivered to the LED, and typically increases perceived brightness.

2. Photodiode

2.1 Introduction

A photodiode converts incident light into an electrical current. It is commonly operated in reverse bias for fast and linear photodetection, although it can also operate without external bias.

2.2 Working Principle

  1. Light photons enter the semiconductor.
  2. Photons with sufficient energy generate electron-hole pairs.
  3. The electric field in the depletion region separates carriers.
  4. Photocurrent is generated.
  5. The photocurrent changes with incident light intensity.

2.3 Photodiode Operating Modes

ModeBiasCharacteristics
PhotovoltaicZero external biasLow noise, generates photovoltage
PhotoconductiveReverse biasFast response, lower junction capacitance

2.4 Photodiode Circuit

A photodiode can be connected to a resistor to produce a voltage from photocurrent. A transimpedance amplifier (TIA) is commonly used for accurate current-to-voltage conversion.

2.5 Photodiode Applications

  • Optical communication receivers
  • Light sensing
  • Automatic lighting systems
  • Barcode readers
  • Optical encoders
  • Smoke detectors
  • Infrared sensing
  • Industrial automation

3. Solar Cell

3.1 Introduction

A solar cell is a semiconductor device that converts light energy directly into electrical energy using the photovoltaic effect.

3.2 Working Principle

  1. Sunlight enters the semiconductor.
  2. Photons generate electron-hole pairs.
  3. Internal electric field separates charge carriers.
  4. Electrons and holes move in different directions.
  5. A potential difference develops.
  6. Connected external load receives electrical power.

3.3 Solar Cell Parameters

ParameterMeaning
VocOpen-circuit voltage
IscShort-circuit current
VmppVoltage at maximum power point
ImppCurrent at maximum power point
PmaxMaximum electrical power
Fill FactorMeasure of I-V curve quality
EfficiencyElectrical power output / incident power

3.4 Solar Cell Calculations

Pmax = Vmpp × Impp
FF = (Vmpp × Impp) / (Voc × Isc)
Efficiency = (Pmax / Pin) × 100%

Example:

  • Voc = 0.60 V
  • Isc = 0.80 A
  • Vmpp = 0.48 V
  • Impp = 0.70 A
  • Incident power = 2 W
Pmax = 0.48 × 0.70 = 0.336 W
FF = 0.336 / (0.60 × 0.80) = 0.70 = 70%
Efficiency = (0.336 / 2) × 100 = 16.8%

4. Schottky Diode

4.1 Introduction

A Schottky diode is formed by a metal-semiconductor junction rather than a conventional P-N junction. It is often called a Schottky barrier diode.

4.2 Working Principle

The metal-semiconductor interface forms a potential barrier. Current conduction is primarily associated with majority carriers, which gives Schottky diodes their characteristic fast switching behavior.

4.3 Characteristics

FeatureTypical Behavior
Forward voltageOften approximately 0.2–0.5 V under specified conditions
SwitchingVery fast; no conventional minority-carrier storage recovery
Reverse leakageOften higher than comparable silicon P-N diodes
Reverse voltageDepends on device; often lower than some standard rectifiers
Carrier typePrimarily majority-carrier conduction

4.4 Comparison with PN Junction Diode

ParameterPN DiodeSchottky Diode
JunctionP-NMetal-semiconductor
Forward dropTypically higher for siliconOften lower
Reverse recoveryMay involve stored chargeVery little minority-carrier storage
Reverse leakageOften lowerOften higher

4.5 Schottky Diode Applications

  • SMPS rectification
  • DC-DC converters
  • High-frequency circuits
  • Reverse polarity protection
  • Voltage clamping
  • Logic circuits
Schottky diodes are not universally superior. Reverse leakage, temperature, voltage rating and power dissipation must be considered.

5. PIN Diode

5.1 Introduction

A PIN diode contains three semiconductor regions:

  • P-region
  • Intrinsic or lightly doped I-region
  • N-region

5.2 Structure

5.3 Working Principle

The intrinsic region increases the effective depletion-region width. Under forward bias, injected carriers establish a stored charge that can make the device behave like a low-resistance element at RF. Under reverse bias, the depletion region supports RF signals with a relatively high resistance and low capacitance.

5.4 PIN Diode in RF Circuits

Bias ConditionRF Behavior (simplified)
Forward biasLow RF resistance, can act as a switch ON
Reverse biasHigh RF resistance, can act as a switch OFF
Zero biasBehavior depends on device and circuit configuration

5.5 Applications

  • RF switches
  • RF attenuators
  • Microwave circuits
  • Transmit/receive switching
  • RF protection circuits
  • High-frequency modulation circuits
PIN diode RF operation depends on bias networks, stored charge, frequency, parasitic capacitance, package and circuit design. The simplified ON/OFF description is not a universal model.

6. Varactor Diode (Varicap Diode)

6.1 Introduction

A varactor diode is a PN junction diode designed to operate primarily in reverse bias, where its junction capacitance varies with applied reverse voltage.

6.2 Working Principle

  1. The diode is reverse biased.
  2. The depletion region widens with increasing reverse voltage.
  3. The junction capacitance decreases.
  4. Capacitance variation is used for electronic frequency tuning.

6.3 Junction Capacitance

Cj = εA / W
Where:
  • Cj = junction capacitance
  • ε = semiconductor permittivity
  • A = junction area
  • W = depletion-region width

As reverse bias increases, depletion width W generally increases and junction capacitance decreases.

6.4 Capacitance-Voltage Relationship

Cj = C0 / (1 + VR / Φ)^m

Where:

  • C0 = capacitance at zero reverse bias in the model
  • VR = reverse-bias voltage magnitude
  • Φ = model parameter representing a characteristic voltage
  • m = junction grading coefficient

This is a simplified junction-capacitance model. Actual varactor behavior depends on the device structure and datasheet.

6.5 Varactor Applications

  • Voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs)
  • Radio frequency tuning
  • Frequency synthesizers
  • Electronic tuning circuits
  • FM modulation
  • Phase-locked loops

11. Numerical Practice

Problem 1

A red LED has a forward voltage of 2 V. It is connected to a 5 V supply through a 470 Ω resistor. Calculate the current.

Show Solution
I = (5 − 2) / 470
I = 6.38 mA

Problem 2

A solar cell produces 0.45 V and 0.6 A at its maximum power point. Calculate maximum power.

Show Solution
Pmax = 0.45 × 0.6 = 0.27 W

Problem 3

A solar cell has Voc = 0.65 V, Isc = 1 A and maximum power of 0.39 W. Calculate fill factor.

Show Solution
FF = 0.39 / (0.65 × 1)
FF = 0.60 = 60%

Problem 4

A 12 V supply drives a 3 V LED at a desired current of 20 mA. Calculate the required resistor.

Show Solution
R = (12 − 3) / 0.02
R = 450 Ω

Problem 5

A solar panel generates 50 W from an incident solar power of 250 W. Calculate conversion efficiency.

Show Solution
η = (50 / 250) × 100
η = 20%
