2. What Is a Push-Pull Amplifier?

A push-pull amplifier uses two active devices that handle alternate portions of an input waveform. Their outputs combine to reproduce the complete amplified signal across the load.

Basic principle:
  • One device handles the positive half-cycle.
  • The other device handles the negative half-cycle.
  • The load receives both halves of the amplified waveform.

Push-pull output stages are commonly implemented using Class B or Class AB biasing. The push-pull arrangement describes how the devices cooperate; the amplifier class describes their conduction behavior.

3. Transformer-Coupled Push-Pull Amplifier

A traditional transformer-coupled push-pull stage uses a centre-tapped primary winding and two transistors. The two transistors are driven by signals that are 180° out of phase.

Operation

  1. The phase splitter produces two equal-amplitude signals with opposite phase.
  2. The first transistor conducts during one half-cycle.
  3. The second transistor conducts during the alternate half-cycle.
  4. The transformer combines the magnetic effects of the two primary halves.
  5. The secondary winding transfers the amplified AC signal to the load.

Important transformer relationship

The load reflected to the complete primary winding is:

RL' = (Np/Ns)² × RL

For a centre-tapped primary, the turns ratio must be defined carefully: use the number of turns in the conducting primary half when calculating the load reflected to that half winding.

4. Phase Splitter

A phase splitter provides two output signals with equal amplitudes and a phase difference of 180°. This is required by many transformer-coupled push-pull stages.

Examples of phase-splitting circuits include transformer phase splitters, common-emitter/common-collector transistor arrangements and differential amplifier stages.

5. Complementary Symmetry Push-Pull Amplifier

A complementary symmetry amplifier uses an NPN and a PNP transistor to drive the same load. It can avoid the need for an output transformer.

Advantages

  • No output transformer is required in a typical complementary emitter-follower output stage.
  • Smaller size and lower weight compared with transformer-coupled stages.
  • Suitable for integrated audio power amplifiers.
  • Can be designed for Class B or Class AB operation.

Limitations

  • Requires suitable complementary devices and driver circuitry.
  • Thermal tracking and bias control are important.
  • Output transistors must withstand load current and voltage stress.
  • Protection is needed against short circuits and excessive temperature.

7. Power and Efficiency of an Ideal Class B Push-Pull Stage

For a sinusoidal output across a resistive load:

Pout = Vm²/(2RL)

Peak load current:

Im = Vm/RL

For symmetrical supply rails ±VCC:

PDC = 2VCCIm/π

Efficiency:

η = (π/4)(Vm/VCC) × 100%

At the ideal maximum output swing, Vm approaches VCC and efficiency approaches 78.5%.

9. Transformer Impedance Matching Calculator

For an ideal transformer, load impedance reflected to the primary is proportional to the square of the turns ratio.

RL' = (Np/Ns)² × RL

For a centre-tapped push-pull transformer, use the turns of the active primary half when calculating the impedance reflected to that half. Do not automatically use the full primary turns for each transistor.

10. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Output Power

A push-pull amplifier delivers 12 V peak across an 8 Ω load.

Pout = Vm²/(2RL) = 144/16 = 9 W

Problem 2: Peak Load Current

Im = Vm/RL = 12/8 = 1.5 A

Problem 3: DC Input Power

For a symmetrical ±15 V supply:

PDC = 2 × 15 × 1.5/π ≈ 14.32 W

Problem 4: Efficiency

η = 9/14.32 × 100 ≈ 62.85%

Problem 5: Transformer Impedance

An ideal transformer has Np/Ns = 10 and the secondary load is 8 Ω.

RL' = 10² × 8 = 800 Ω

12. Practical Design and Protection

  • Use a suitable driver stage to provide adequate base current.
  • Set the required Class B or Class AB bias.
  • Use emitter resistors to improve current sharing and thermal stability.
  • Provide heat sinks sized for worst-case dissipation.
  • Use current limiting and short-circuit protection.
  • Check transistor SOA, maximum junction temperature and collector voltage.
  • Consider output inductors and Zobel networks where required for stability with reactive loads.
  • Check supply decoupling and grounding to prevent oscillation.
  • In transformer-coupled circuits, check winding polarity, insulation and core saturation.

13. Practical Lab: Push-Pull Amplifier

Objective

Observe complementary operation and calculate the output power of a push-pull amplifier.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage push-pull amplifier trainer or simulator.
  • Complementary NPN and PNP transistor models.
  • Function generator.
  • Oscilloscope and multimeter.
  • Suitable resistive load.

Procedure

  1. Open a complementary push-pull amplifier simulation.
  2. Apply a low-amplitude sinusoidal input.
  3. Observe the NPN and PNP collector/emitter currents.
  4. Observe the combined output across the load.
  5. Increase input amplitude gradually and inspect clipping.
  6. Adjust bias if the circuit is configured for Class AB operation.
  7. Measure RMS output voltage and calculate output power.
  8. Record supply current and estimate efficiency.

Expected result

The two output devices should handle alternate portions of the signal. The load receives the complete amplified waveform. Proper biasing reduces crossover distortion.

Use a current-limited low-voltage supply. Never connect an experimental amplifier output directly to mains or an unknown load.

17. Summary

  • Push-pull amplifiers use two devices to handle alternate signal portions.
  • Transformer-coupled stages commonly use a centre-tapped primary and phase splitter.
  • Complementary symmetry stages use NPN and PNP devices and usually avoid an output transformer.
  • Push-pull stages commonly operate in Class B or Class AB.
  • Ideal Class B maximum efficiency is approximately 78.5%.
  • Thermal stability, bias, device ratings and protection are critical in practical designs.
