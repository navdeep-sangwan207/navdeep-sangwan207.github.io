2. Why Is Heat Dissipation Important?

A power amplifier delivers electrical power to a load, but some of the supplied electrical energy is converted into heat inside its output devices and other components.

Excessive junction temperature can cause:

  • Reduced transistor reliability and lifetime.
  • Parameter drift, including changes in current gain and leakage current.
  • Thermal runaway in susceptible circuits.
  • Device failure or permanent damage.
  • Activation of thermal shutdown protection in integrated amplifiers.
Design principle: A power amplifier must be designed not only to deliver the required output power, but also to dissipate the resulting heat safely under worst-case operating conditions.

3. Power Amplifier Efficiency

Efficiency is the ratio of useful AC output power delivered to the load to the DC power drawn from the power supply.

η = (Pout / PDC) × 100%

Where:

  • Pout = useful AC power delivered to the load.
  • PDC = total DC input power drawn from the supply.
  • η = conversion efficiency.

Power dissipated as heat

For a simplified amplifier power balance:

Ploss = PDC − Pout

This represents the total power not delivered to the load. In a complete circuit, it can include losses in output transistors, driver stages, resistors, transformer windings and other components.

4. Output Power Calculations

For a sinusoidal output across a resistive load:

Vrms = Vpeak / √2

Pout = Vrms² / RL

Pout = Vpeak² / (2RL)

For a sinusoidal load current:

Pout = Irms² × RL

For a non-sinusoidal waveform, use the actual RMS voltage and current and calculate average real power. The simple sine-wave formula should not be applied blindly to distorted or reactive loads.

6. Class B Push-Pull Power and Device Dissipation

For an ideal symmetrical Class B amplifier with supply rails +VCC and −VCC:

Pout = Vm² / (2RL)

Im = Vm / RL

PDC = 2VCCIm / π

The total power dissipated in the output stage is:

Pdevice,total = PDC − Pout

For a perfectly symmetrical ideal stage, the average dissipation is shared equally between the two output devices:

Pdevice,each = (PDC − Pout) / 2

This is an average over a complete signal cycle. The instantaneous transistor dissipation varies throughout the waveform.

7. Why Maximum Dissipation Does Not Occur at Maximum Output

In an ideal Class B stage, transistor dissipation is not necessarily greatest at maximum output power. At very low output, the output devices dissipate little power in the ideal model. As output increases, device dissipation rises and then falls near the maximum output swing.

For one transistor in an ideal symmetrical Class B stage, average dissipation can be expressed as:

Pdevice = (VCC × Im / π) × (1 − (πVm / 4VCC))

With Im = Vm/RL, this model predicts maximum average dissipation at:

Vm = 2VCC / π ≈ 0.637VCC

This is an idealized resistive-load model. Real devices also experience switching transients, reactive load stress, supply variation and temperature effects.

8. Thermal Resistance

Thermal resistance describes how difficult it is for heat to flow from one point to another. It is measured in °C/W or K/W.

Temperature rise = Power dissipation × Thermal resistance

TJ = TA + PD × RθJA

Where:

  • TJ = transistor junction temperature.
  • TA = ambient temperature.
  • PD = power dissipated in the device.
  • RθJA = junction-to-ambient thermal resistance.

Thermal path in a heat-sink-mounted transistor

RθJA = RθJC + RθCS + RθSA

  • RθJC = junction-to-case thermal resistance.
  • RθCS = case-to-sink thermal resistance.
  • RθSA = heat-sink-to-ambient thermal resistance.

For an isolated package without a separate heat sink, use the manufacturer's specified RθJA under the stated mounting and airflow conditions.

9. Heat Sink Design

To maintain junction temperature below the chosen design limit:

RθJA ≤ (TJ,target − TA) / PD

For a heat-sink-mounted transistor:

RθSA ≤ (TJ,target − TA)/PD − RθJC − RθCS

A practical design should include margin for elevated ambient temperature, component tolerance, restricted airflow, mounting variation and long-term reliability.

A lower thermal resistance means a more effective heat-transfer path. A larger heat sink, better airflow or improved thermal interface can reduce temperature rise.

13. Thermal Runaway

Thermal runaway occurs when a rise in device temperature causes an increase in current or power dissipation that creates further heating. This can form a positive feedback loop.

  1. Junction temperature increases.
  2. Device leakage current and other temperature-dependent parameters change.
  3. Current or dissipation may increase, depending on the circuit.
  4. Further temperature rise can occur.

Methods to reduce thermal runaway risk

  • Use suitable bias stabilization.
  • Use emitter resistors in BJT output stages.
  • Use thermally coupled bias compensation where appropriate.
  • Provide adequate heat sinking.
  • Monitor quiescent current and junction temperature.
  • Use thermal shutdown and overcurrent protection where appropriate.

14. Safe Operating Area (SOA)

The safe operating area specifies combinations of collector-emitter voltage, collector current, pulse duration and temperature at which a transistor can operate without damage.

Checking only the maximum voltage and maximum current ratings separately is insufficient. A device may exceed its SOA even when neither individual absolute maximum rating is exceeded.

Important checks

  • Maximum collector-emitter or drain-source voltage.
  • Maximum continuous and pulsed current.
  • Power dissipation at the actual case temperature.
  • Second breakdown limitations in power BJTs.
  • Transient thermal impedance for pulsed operation.
  • Load-line trajectory and reactive-load conditions.
Always consult the manufacturer's SOA graph and derating curves. Absolute maximum ratings are not recommended normal operating points.

15. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Amplifier Efficiency

An amplifier draws 60 W DC and delivers 36 W AC to its load.

η = 36/60 × 100 = 60%

Ploss = 60 − 36 = 24 W

Problem 2: Output Power

An amplifier provides 8 V RMS across a 4 Ω load.

Pout = 8²/4 = 16 W

Problem 3: Junction Temperature

A transistor dissipates 5 W. Its ambient temperature is 35°C and RθJA is 12°C/W.

TJ = 35 + (5 × 12) = 95°C

Problem 4: Heat Sink Requirement

Given TJ,target = 120°C, TA = 40°C, PD = 10 W, RθJC = 2°C/W and RθCS = 0.5°C/W:

RθSA ≤ (120 − 40)/10 − 2 − 0.5

RθSA ≤ 5.5°C/W

Choose a heat sink with a rated thermal resistance below 5.5°C/W under the actual mounting and airflow conditions, with suitable design margin.

16. Practical Lab: Power and Thermal Measurement

Objective

Calculate amplifier efficiency and estimate output transistor temperature from measured power and thermal resistance.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage power amplifier trainer or circuit simulator.
  • Current-limited DC supply.
  • Oscilloscope and digital multimeter.
  • Resistive dummy load rated for the expected power.
  • Temperature sensor or thermal camera, if available.

Procedure

  1. Inspect the amplifier supply and load ratings.
  2. Apply a low-amplitude sine wave.
  3. Measure supply voltage and current.
  4. Calculate total DC input power.
  5. Measure output RMS voltage across the resistive load.
  6. Calculate output power using Pout = Vrms²/RL.
  7. Calculate efficiency and total power loss.
  8. Estimate junction temperature using the device's thermal resistance data.
  9. Compare the estimate with the manufacturer's maximum junction temperature and SOA.

Expected result

The measured output power should be lower than the DC input power. The difference represents total circuit losses. The estimated junction temperature must remain within the chosen design limit.

Do not touch a powered heat sink or transistor package to estimate temperature. Use suitable measurement equipment and allow components to cool before handling.

20. Summary

  • Efficiency is useful AC output power divided by DC input power.
  • Power not delivered to the load becomes heat or other circuit losses.
  • Class A, B, AB and C stages have different efficiency and thermal characteristics.
  • Junction temperature depends on power dissipation and thermal resistance.
  • Heat-sink selection requires the complete junction-to-ambient thermal path.
  • SOA, transient stress and thermal runaway must be considered in practical designs.
