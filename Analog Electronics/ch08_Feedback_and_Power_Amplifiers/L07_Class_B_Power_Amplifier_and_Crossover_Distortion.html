Lesson 7: Class A Power Amplifier

Operating Principle, Biasing, Load Line, Efficiency and Practical Applications

1. Introduction to Class A Power Amplifier

A Class A power amplifier is a transistor amplifier in which the active device conducts for the complete 360° of the input signal cycle.

The transistor is biased in its active region so that it remains conducting during both positive and negative half-cycles of the input signal.

Class A amplifiers are known for their relatively low distortion and simple circuit operation. However, they have low efficiency because the transistor continuously consumes power, even when no input signal is applied.

Important characteristics

  • Conduction angle: 360°.
  • Transistor remains in the active region throughout the signal cycle during normal operation.
  • Continuous collector current flows even with zero input signal.
  • Low distortion when correctly designed.
  • High power dissipation and relatively low efficiency.

2. Operating Principle

The transistor is provided with a suitable DC bias voltage to establish a quiescent operating point, also called the Q-point.

When an AC input signal is applied:

  • During the positive input half-cycle, base current and collector current generally increase, causing the collector voltage to decrease in a common-emitter circuit.
  • During the negative input half-cycle, base current and collector current decrease, causing the collector voltage to increase.
  • The transistor continues conducting during both half-cycles.

The amplified output signal is obtained through the output coupling network or transformer, depending on the circuit configuration.

Waveform concept: The transistor conducts throughout the entire input cycle. In a common-emitter amplifier, the collector voltage output is amplified and inverted relative to the input.

3. Quiescent Operating Point (Q-Point)

The Q-point is the DC operating condition of a transistor when no AC input signal is applied.

It is generally represented by:

Q-point parameters

Collector current: \(I_{CQ}\)

Collector-emitter voltage: \(V_{CEQ}\)

For symmetrical output voltage swing in a simple resistive-load Class A circuit, the Q-point is normally selected near the middle of the DC load line. The exact optimum depends on the circuit, supply voltage, load and bias arrangement.

DC load line

For a common-emitter circuit with a collector resistor and grounded emitter:

\[ V_{CE}=V_{CC}-I_C R_C \]

At cutoff: \(I_C=0\), so \(V_{CE}=V_{CC}\).

At the ideal load-line saturation intercept: \(V_{CE}\approx0\), so \(I_C\approx V_{CC}/R_C\).

Important: The load-line graph is conceptual. The actual saturation voltage is not exactly zero, and the AC load line can differ from the DC load line because of the external load and coupling arrangement.

4. Class A Amplifier Circuit Configurations

4.1 Series-fed resistive-load Class A amplifier

In this configuration, the collector resistor acts as the load. A coupling capacitor connects the external load to the collector while blocking DC from the load.

Working: The resistor \(R_C\) carries the transistor's DC collector current. The output coupling capacitor passes the AC output signal to \(R_L\), while preventing the collector's DC voltage from appearing across the load.

A practical circuit also needs a base-bias network to establish the required Q-point. It is omitted from this simplified conceptual diagram.

4.2 Transformer-coupled Class A amplifier

In this configuration, a transformer primary winding is connected between the supply and transistor collector. The secondary winding supplies the AC output to the load.

Working: The transformer transfers the AC signal to the load and provides impedance matching. Ideally, the DC supply current flows through the primary winding, but no DC current flows through the secondary load.

The diagram is conceptual. A complete design must include suitable base bias, transformer winding polarity, load matching and transistor ratings.

5. Efficiency of Class A Power Amplifier

Efficiency is the ratio of useful AC output power delivered to the load to the DC power drawn from the supply.

\[ \eta=\frac{P_{out}}{P_{DC}}\times100\% \] \[ P_{DC}=V_{CC}I_{CQ} \] \[ P_{out}=\frac{V_{rms}^{2}}{R_L} \]

5.1 Series-fed Class A amplifier

For an ideal resistive collector load and symmetrical sinusoidal output swing, the maximum theoretical efficiency is 25%.

\[ P_{out(max)}=\frac{V_{CC}I_{CQ}}{4} \] \[ \eta_{max}=\frac{1}{4}\times100=25\% \]

5.2 Transformer-coupled Class A amplifier

An ideal transformer can transfer AC power to the load without a DC voltage drop across a collector load resistor. The maximum theoretical efficiency is 50% for ideal sinusoidal operation.

\[ P_{out(max)}=\frac{V_{CC}I_{CQ}}{2} \] \[ \eta_{max}=\frac{1}{2}\times100=50\% \]
Configuration Maximum theoretical efficiency Main reason
Series-fed Class A 25% DC power is continuously dissipated in the resistive collector load and transistor.
Transformer-coupled Class A 50% Transformer transfers AC power without a DC drop across a collector resistor.
These are ideal theoretical limits. Real-world efficiency is lower due to transistor losses, transformer losses, bias-network power and other circuit imperfections.

6. Important Power Calculations

Sinusoidal output signal

\[ V_{rms}=\frac{V_{peak}}{\sqrt{2}} \] \[ I_{rms}=\frac{I_{peak}}{\sqrt{2}} \] \[ P_{out}=\frac{V_{rms}^{2}}{R_L} \]

Example 1: Calculate output power

Given:

  • Load resistance = 8 Ω
  • Output RMS voltage = 4 V

Solution:

\[ P_{out}=\frac{4^2}{8} \] \[ P_{out}=\frac{16}{8}=\boxed{2W} \]

Answer: Output power is 2 W.

Example 2: Calculate amplifier efficiency

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 12 V
  • Quiescent collector current = 0.5 A
  • AC output power = 2 W

Step 1: Calculate DC input power.

\[ P_{DC}=12\times0.5=6W \]

Step 2: Calculate efficiency.

\[ \eta=\frac{2}{6}\times100 \] \[ \boxed{\eta=33.33\%} \]

Answer: The amplifier efficiency is approximately 33.33%. This is below the ideal 50% transformer-coupled Class A limit.

Example 3: Calculate transistor power dissipation at no signal

Given: \(V_{CEQ}=6V\), \(I_{CQ}=0.5A\).

\[ P_{transistor}=V_{CEQ}I_{CQ} \] \[ P_{transistor}=6\times0.5=\boxed{3W} \]

At no signal, the transistor dissipates approximately 3 W, ignoring other circuit losses. A suitable heat sink may be required.

7. Advantages of Class A Amplifier

  • Low distortion when correctly biased and operated within its linear range.
  • Simple circuit design.
  • Good linearity.
  • No crossover distortion because the transistor conducts throughout the cycle.
  • Suitable for low-noise and high-fidelity small-signal or low-power stages.

8. Disadvantages of Class A Amplifier

  • Low efficiency compared with Class B, AB and D amplifiers.
  • Continuous power consumption even without an input signal.
  • High heat generation.
  • Large heat sink may be necessary.
  • Not economical for high-power battery-operated systems.

9. Applications

  • Low-distortion audio amplifier stages.
  • Small-signal audio preamplifiers.
  • High-linearity amplifier stages.
  • Selected instrumentation and signal-conditioning circuits.
  • Specialized RF and analog circuits where linearity is important.

10. Practical Design and Troubleshooting

Important design considerations

  • Select a transistor with adequate voltage, current and power ratings.
  • Establish a stable Q-point using an appropriate bias network.
  • Check the DC and AC load lines before choosing the operating point.
  • Calculate transistor power dissipation under no-signal and maximum-signal conditions.
  • Choose a suitable heat sink and verify the transistor's safe operating area (SOA).
  • Use coupling capacitors with suitable capacitance and voltage ratings.
  • Check transformer primary and secondary ratings in transformer-coupled designs.

Thermal calculation

\[ T_j=T_a+P_D R_{\theta JA} \]

Where:

  • \(T_j\): Junction temperature
  • \(T_a\): Ambient temperature
  • \(P_D\): Device power dissipation
  • \(R_{\theta JA}\): Junction-to-ambient thermal resistance

Common faults

Problem Possible cause Checking method
No output Incorrect bias, faulty transistor, open coupling capacitor Check DC voltages and signal path.
Output clipping Incorrect Q-point or excessive input amplitude Observe the output waveform on an oscilloscope.
Excessive heating High quiescent current, incorrect bias or inadequate heat sink Measure collector current and calculate dissipation.
Distorted output Improper bias, overload or component failure Compare input and output waveforms.
Low output power Incorrect load, weak supply or transformer mismatch Measure supply voltage and output power.
Lab safety: Disconnect power before changing circuit connections. Discharge capacitors safely, use current-limited low-voltage supplies, and avoid touching hot transistor heat sinks.

11. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a Class A power amplifier?

A transistor amplifier in which the active device conducts for the complete 360° of the input cycle.

Q2. What is the conduction angle of Class A?

360 degrees.

Q3. Why does a Class A amplifier have low efficiency?

Because the transistor draws continuous quiescent current and dissipates power even when there is no input signal.

Q4. What is the maximum theoretical efficiency of a series-fed Class A amplifier?

25% for an ideal resistive-load sinusoidal amplifier.

Q5. What is the maximum theoretical efficiency of a transformer-coupled Class A amplifier?

50% under ideal sinusoidal operating conditions.

Q6. What is a Q-point?

The DC operating point of a transistor with no AC input signal.

Q7. Why is the transistor biased in the active region?

To allow linear amplification of the complete input waveform without switching off during either half-cycle.

Q8. Does Class A suffer from crossover distortion?

No. The transistor conducts throughout the complete cycle, so the crossover distortion associated with push-pull Class B operation does not occur.

Q9. What is the purpose of an output transformer?

It transfers AC power to the load and can provide impedance matching while preventing DC supply current from flowing through the load.

Q10. Why is a heat sink required?

To transfer heat away from the transistor and keep its junction temperature within safe limits.

13. Lesson Summary

  • Class A amplifiers conduct for 360° of the input cycle.
  • The transistor remains in the active region during normal signal operation.
  • The Q-point determines the no-signal operating condition.
  • Series-fed Class A has a maximum theoretical efficiency of 25%.
  • Transformer-coupled Class A has a maximum theoretical efficiency of 50%.
  • Continuous current causes heat generation even without an input signal.
  • Class A is useful where linearity and low distortion are more important than efficiency.
