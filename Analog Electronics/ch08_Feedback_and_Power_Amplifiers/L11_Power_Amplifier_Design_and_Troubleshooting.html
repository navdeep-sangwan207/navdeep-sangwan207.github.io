2. Power Amplifier Design Workflow

A practical power amplifier should be designed in stages. Each stage has electrical, thermal and reliability constraints.

4. Calculate Required Output Voltage and Current

For a resistive load and sinusoidal output:

Pout = Vrms² / RL

Vrms = √(Pout × RL)

Irms = Vrms / RL

Vpeak = √2 × Vrms

Ipeak = √2 × Irms

These values establish the minimum output capability before allowing for transistor voltage drops, supply sag, headroom and design margin.

6. Output Transistor Selection

Check more than the headline maximum ratings. Select devices using the manufacturer's data sheet.

  • Maximum collector-emitter voltage or drain-source voltage.
  • Continuous and peak current capability.
  • Power dissipation at the actual case temperature.
  • Safe operating area under DC and pulsed conditions.
  • Gain or transconductance at the intended operating current.
  • Frequency response and switching behavior.
  • Thermal resistance and maximum junction temperature.
  • Complementary device matching, where required.
A transistor's absolute maximum current and voltage ratings must not be treated as simultaneously available operating limits. Check the SOA graph for the intended load line and temperature.

7. Driver and Bias Design

Driver stage

The driver provides the voltage and current required to control the output devices. It must deliver sufficient base current for BJTs or gate charge for MOSFETs at the intended signal frequency and slew rate.

Bias network

Class AB stages use a bias network to establish a small quiescent conduction current. This reduces crossover distortion while avoiding excessive idle dissipation.

  • Use suitable bias spreader or diode compensation.
  • Consider thermal coupling between the bias element and output devices.
  • Use emitter/source resistors where appropriate for current sharing and stability.
  • Verify quiescent current after the circuit reaches thermal equilibrium.

8. Feedback and Stability

Negative feedback can improve closed-loop gain accuracy, reduce distortion and control frequency response, provided the amplifier remains stable.

Af = A / (1 + Aβ)

Practical stability checks include:

  • Loop gain and phase margin over the required frequency range.
  • Compensation capacitor and driver-stage bandwidth.
  • PCB layout and high-current return paths.
  • Power supply decoupling near output and driver stages.
  • Reactive load behavior, including speaker cables and capacitive loads.
A power amplifier that oscillates at high frequency can overheat even when no audible oscillation is present. Verify the output with an oscilloscope using suitable probing technique.

10. Power Supply and PCB Layout

  • Choose a supply with sufficient voltage, current and transient capability.
  • Provide local decoupling capacitors close to driver and output stages.
  • Keep high-current load paths short and appropriately wide.
  • Separate sensitive input circuitry from high-current switching paths.
  • Use a deliberate grounding strategy to reduce hum and unwanted feedback.
  • Provide adequate creepage, clearance and insulation where required.
  • Ensure heat sink and device mounting meet electrical isolation requirements.

14. Systematic Troubleshooting Procedure

  1. Confirm the reported symptom and inspect the circuit for visible damage.
  2. Check the power supply rails, polarity and current consumption.
  3. Check DC conditions at the input, driver and output stages.
  4. Apply a low-level test signal and trace it through each stage.
  5. Measure output waveform, clipping and DC offset.
  6. Check bias current and device temperature.
  7. Inspect feedback, decoupling and grounding.
  8. Test protection circuits and verify the load condition.
  9. Change one suspected fault at a time and repeat the measurement.
  10. Record the final readings and confirm operation across the required load and frequency range.

16. Practical Lab: Low-Voltage Amplifier Testing

Objective

Measure the output power, gain, clipping behavior and thermal response of a low-voltage power amplifier.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage amplifier trainer or simulator.
  • Current-limited DC supply.
  • Function generator.
  • Oscilloscope and digital multimeter.
  • Rated resistive dummy load.
  • Temperature sensor, if available.

Procedure

  1. Verify the supply voltage and polarity before powering the circuit.
  2. Check the quiescent output voltage and current.
  3. Apply a low-amplitude sine wave within the amplifier's bandwidth.
  4. Measure input and output peak-to-peak voltage.
  5. Calculate voltage gain.
  6. Increase the input gradually and identify the onset of clipping.
  7. Measure output RMS voltage and calculate power into the known load.
  8. Monitor supply current and device temperature.
  9. Inspect the waveform for crossover distortion, clipping and oscillation.
  10. Verify that protection operates correctly under approved test conditions.

Expected result

The amplifier should reproduce an amplified output within its specified range. The output waveform should remain stable, device temperature should remain within the design limit, and protection should operate as intended.

Use only a low-voltage, current-limited trainer or simulator. Do not deliberately short a power amplifier output or exceed transistor ratings during testing.

19. Summary

  • Power amplifier design starts with measurable electrical and thermal specifications.
  • Output power determines the required load voltage and current.
  • Topology and output devices must satisfy bandwidth, distortion, voltage, current and SOA constraints.
  • Bias, driver, feedback, thermal management and protection are essential design blocks.
  • Systematic measurements help identify faults without unnecessary component replacement.
  • Final validation must include load, frequency, temperature and protection checks.
