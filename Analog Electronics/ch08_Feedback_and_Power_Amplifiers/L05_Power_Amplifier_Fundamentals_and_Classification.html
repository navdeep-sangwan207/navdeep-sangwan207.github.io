2. What Is a Power Amplifier?

A power amplifier is an electronic circuit designed to deliver significant power to a load. It increases the power available to a load such as a loudspeaker, motor driver stage or other output device.

Power amplifiers are generally used as the output stages of electronic systems.

In a practical audio system, the preamplifier handles small signals, the driver stage supplies sufficient voltage/current drive, and the power output stage delivers power to the speaker.

A power amplifier does not create energy. It controls energy drawn from its DC supply and transfers a portion of it to the load.

4. Important Power Amplifier Parameters

A. Input power

For a resistive input:

Pin = Vin(rms) × Iin(rms)

B. Output power

For a sinusoidal signal delivered to a resistive load:

Pout = Vout(rms) × Iout(rms)

Pout = Vout(rms)²/RL = Iout(rms)²RL

C. Power gain

Ap = Pout/Pin

Power gain in decibels:

Ap(dB) = 10 log10(Pout/Pin)

D. Efficiency

Efficiency is the ratio of useful AC output power delivered to the load to the DC power drawn from the supply.

η = (Pout(ac)/PDC) × 100%

E. Power dissipation

For a simplified steady-state stage:

Pdevice ≈ PDC − Pout(ac)

In a real circuit, account for all output devices, driver losses, bias networks and other supply loads when calculating total heat dissipation.

5. Conduction Angle

The conduction angle is the portion of one complete input-signal cycle during which an output device conducts appreciable current.

Conceptual conduction illustrations. Class AB conducts for more than 180° but less than 360° per device.

6. Class A Power Amplifier

In a Class A amplifier, the output device conducts throughout the complete input cycle.

  • Conduction angle: 360°.
  • Low crossover distortion.
  • Simple linear operation.
  • High standing current and significant power dissipation.
  • Low efficiency compared with switching-oriented classes.

Ideal maximum efficiency depends on the circuit arrangement:

Class A configurationIdeal maximum efficiency
Resistive or series-fed load25%
Transformer-coupled load50%

These limits apply to idealized sinusoidal Class A output-stage models under their specified operating conditions.

7. Class B Power Amplifier

In a Class B push-pull stage, one output device conducts during the positive half-cycle and the other during the negative half-cycle.

  • Each device conducts for approximately 180°.
  • Quiescent current is ideally zero.
  • Efficiency is higher than Class A.
  • A practical transistor implementation may exhibit crossover distortion near zero crossing.

Ideal maximum efficiency for a sinusoidal push-pull Class B stage:

ηmax = π/4 × 100% ≈ 78.5%

8. Class AB Power Amplifier

Class AB operation biases both output devices slightly above cutoff. Each device conducts for more than 180° but less than 360°.

  • Reduces crossover distortion compared with Class B.
  • Has higher efficiency potential than Class A.
  • Requires careful bias control and thermal compensation.
  • Commonly used in audio power amplifiers.

9. Class C Power Amplifier

In Class C operation, the output device conducts for less than half the input cycle. The resulting current pulses contain strong harmonic components.

  • High efficiency can be achieved with a tuned resonant load.
  • Not suitable for direct linear audio amplification.
  • Common in tuned RF power amplifier stages.
  • The resonant network reconstructs the desired fundamental-frequency output.

11. Power Amplifier Heat Dissipation

Power devices convert part of the DC supply energy into heat. Excessive junction temperature can cause parameter shifts, thermal runaway or device failure.

Tj = Ta + Pd × RθJA

where:

  • Tj = junction temperature
  • Ta = ambient temperature
  • Pd = power dissipated by the device
  • RθJA = junction-to-ambient thermal resistance

When a heat sink is used, the thermal path can be modeled as:

Tj = Ta + Pd(RθJC + RθCS + RθSA)

where JC, CS and SA represent junction-to-case, case-to-sink and sink-to-ambient thermal resistances.

14. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Output Power

A power amplifier supplies 8 V RMS to an 8 Ω resistive load. Calculate the output power.

Pout = V²/R = 8²/8 = 8 W

Problem 2: Efficiency

An amplifier delivers 40 W AC output power and draws 60 W DC input power.

η = (40/60) × 100 = 66.67%

Problem 3: Power Gain in dB

Input power is 0.5 W and output power is 25 W.

Ap = 25/0.5 = 50

Ap(dB) = 10 log10(50) ≈ 16.99 dB

Problem 4: Power Dissipation

A simplified amplifier draws 80 W DC power and delivers 50 W to the load.

Pdevice,total ≈ 80 − 50 = 30 W

The total output-stage dissipation is approximately 30 W, assuming other circuit consumption is included in the stated DC power and the output power is the only useful output.

15. Practical Design Considerations

  • Select output transistors with suitable voltage, current and power ratings.
  • Check the safe operating area (SOA), not only the maximum ratings.
  • Provide adequate heat sinking and airflow.
  • Use suitable emitter/source resistors where required for current sharing and stability.
  • Protect the output against short circuits and excessive load current.
  • Check supply decoupling and PCB current paths.
  • Consider load impedance and reactive load behavior.
  • Measure output distortion, temperature and efficiency under realistic operating conditions.

16. Practical Lab: Power Amplifier Performance

Objective

Measure output power and efficiency of a low-voltage power amplifier or simulation model.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage amplifier evaluation circuit or simulator.
  • Function generator or simulated sine-wave source.
  • Oscilloscope or waveform viewer.
  • Suitable rated resistive load.
  • Multimeter and current measurement equipment.

Procedure

  1. Verify the supply voltage and load resistance.
  2. Apply a low-amplitude sine-wave signal.
  3. Measure the output voltage across the resistive load.
  4. Calculate RMS output power using V²/R.
  5. Measure DC supply voltage and current.
  6. Calculate DC input power using VDC × IDC.
  7. Calculate efficiency.
  8. Increase the input gradually and observe waveform clipping and temperature changes.
  9. Record the results and compare them with theoretical expectations.

Expected result

The measured output power, supply power and efficiency should be consistent with the amplifier's operating class and circuit design.

Use a low-voltage current-limited supply and a load with an adequate power rating. Never connect an experimental amplifier directly to mains voltage.

20. Summary

  • Power amplifiers deliver useful power to a load.
  • Efficiency is the ratio of AC output power to DC supply input power.
  • Conduction angle determines the basic operating class.
  • Class A conducts for 360°.
  • Class B conducts for approximately 180° per output device.
  • Class AB conducts for more than 180° but less than 360°.
  • Class C conducts for less than 180° and is commonly used with tuned RF networks.
  • Thermal management and SOA are essential in practical output stages.
