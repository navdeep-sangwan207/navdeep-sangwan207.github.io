Lesson 5: Barkhausen Criterion and Oscillation

Understand oscillator fundamentals, positive feedback, loop gain, phase conditions and commonly used oscillator circuits.

1. Introduction to Oscillators

An oscillator is an electronic circuit that generates a periodic output signal without requiring a periodic input signal. It converts DC power from its supply into an AC waveform.

Oscillators are widely used in electronic systems for generating clock signals, timing signals, carrier signals and test signals.

Examples of Oscillator Applications

  • Clock generation in microcontrollers and processors.
  • Frequency generation in communication systems.
  • Local oscillators in radio receivers.
  • Signal generators used in electronics laboratories.
  • Timing circuits and digital systems.
  • PWM and control-system reference signals.

2. Basic Principle of Oscillation

An oscillator uses an amplifier and a feedback network. A portion of the output is returned to the input with the correct magnitude and phase to sustain the output signal.

Unlike an ordinary amplifier, an oscillator does not need a continuous external AC input signal to maintain oscillation. A small initial disturbance, such as circuit noise or a switching transient, can initiate oscillation.

Conceptual amplifier-feedback arrangement used to explain oscillator operation.

3. Positive Feedback in Oscillators

Positive feedback occurs when the feedback signal reinforces the input signal. In an oscillator, the feedback network returns a signal with the phase required to sustain oscillation.

The amplifier provides the energy needed to maintain the output signal, while the feedback network selects the desired frequency and phase.

An oscillator is not simply an amplifier with arbitrary positive feedback. Its frequency-selective network and loop conditions must support sustained periodic operation.

4. Barkhausen Criterion

The Barkhausen criterion describes the ideal loop conditions for sustained sinusoidal oscillation in a linear feedback model.

It has two main conditions:

Condition 1: Magnitude Condition

|Aβ| = 1

The magnitude of the total loop gain must be unity at the oscillation frequency.

Condition 2: Phase Condition

Total phase shift = 0° or 360° × n

Where n is an integer.

The feedback signal must return in phase with the signal at the input of the amplifier.
Barkhausen criterion: |Aβ| = 1 and total loop phase shift = 0° (or an integer multiple of 360°), using the positive-feedback loop convention.

5. Understanding Loop Gain

Loop gain is the product of amplifier gain and feedback factor.

Loop Gain = Aβ

A = Amplifier gain
β = Feedback factor

The loop gain determines whether an initial signal decreases, grows or remains constant as it circulates around the feedback loop.

Loop Gain Magnitude Typical Behaviour
|Aβ| < 1 Oscillation tends to decay.
|Aβ| = 1 Ideal condition for sustained oscillation.
|Aβ| > 1 Oscillation amplitude tends to grow initially.
These descriptions assume the correct phase condition is also satisfied. In practical oscillators, nonlinear effects or amplitude-control circuits limit growth and establish a steady output amplitude.

6. Startup and Sustained Oscillation

A. Startup Condition

At startup, the loop gain magnitude is commonly designed to be slightly greater than unity at the intended frequency. This allows a small initial disturbance to grow.

Startup condition:

|Aβ| > 1

At the intended oscillation frequency.

B. Sustained Oscillation

As the output amplitude increases, nonlinear effects or automatic amplitude-control circuits reduce the effective loop gain until it approaches unity.

Steady-state condition:

|Aβ| ≈ 1

This prevents the output amplitude from increasing indefinitely.

7. Types of Electronic Oscillators

Oscillators can be classified according to the frequency-selective network and the waveform they generate.

8. RC Phase-Shift Oscillator

An RC phase-shift oscillator uses an amplifier and a resistor-capacitor feedback network.

In a common three-section RC network, each section contributes phase shift. At the oscillation frequency, the RC network provides approximately 180° phase shift, while an inverting amplifier provides another 180°.

Functional representation of the RC phase-shift oscillator.

Frequency Equation

For three identical RC sections under the standard ideal assumptions:

f = 1 / (2πRC√6)

Where:
  • R = Resistance of each RC section
  • C = Capacitance of each RC section

Characteristics

  • Uses an RC feedback network.
  • Suitable for low-frequency sinusoidal signals.
  • Three RC sections provide approximately 180° phase shift.
  • The inverting amplifier provides another 180°.
  • Minimum amplifier gain is approximately 29 for the ideal equal-section network.

9. Wien Bridge Oscillator

A Wien bridge oscillator uses a lead-lag RC network to select the oscillation frequency. It is commonly used to generate low-distortion sine waves.

Functional representation of a Wien bridge oscillator.

Frequency Equation

For equal resistance and capacitance values in the standard Wien bridge network:

f = 1 / (2πRC)

Characteristics

  • Produces a sinusoidal output.
  • Provides approximately zero phase shift at the selected frequency.
  • The feedback network attenuation is 1/3 at the oscillation frequency.
  • The amplifier gain must be approximately 3 for ideal sustained operation.
  • Amplitude stabilization is commonly used to control distortion.

10. Hartley Oscillator

A Hartley oscillator is an LC oscillator that uses an inductive divider in its resonant feedback network. It is commonly used for radio-frequency signal generation.

Functional representation of a Hartley oscillator.

f = 1 / (2π√(Leq C))

Leq = L1 + L2 + 2M

For tightly coupled inductors in a series-aiding arrangement.

Here L1 and L2 are the inductances and M is their mutual inductance. The exact equivalent inductance depends on winding polarity and coupling.

11. Colpitts Oscillator

A Colpitts oscillator is an LC oscillator that uses a capacitive divider for feedback.

Functional representation of a Colpitts oscillator.

f = 1 / (2π√(Leq Ceq))

Ceq = (C1 × C2) / (C1 + C2)

Characteristics

  • Uses two capacitors as a capacitive divider.
  • Uses an inductor in the resonant tank circuit.
  • Suitable for high-frequency oscillation.
  • Commonly used in RF circuits.

12. Crystal Oscillator

A crystal oscillator uses the piezoelectric effect of a quartz crystal to produce a highly stable frequency.

When an alternating voltage is applied to a quartz crystal, it mechanically vibrates at frequencies determined by its physical properties and electrical loading.

Simplified crystal oscillator feedback concept.

Characteristics

  • High frequency stability.
  • High Q factor compared with many ordinary LC networks.
  • Widely used in microcontrollers and digital electronics.
  • Common clock frequencies include 8 MHz, 12 MHz, 16 MHz and 20 MHz.
Embedded Systems Connection: Microcontrollers such as the LPC2129 use an external crystal or oscillator source to provide a stable clock reference. The processor clock is derived using the device's clock generation circuitry.

14. Numerical Example: RC Phase-Shift Oscillator

Calculate the oscillation frequency of a three-section RC phase-shift oscillator when each resistor is 10 kΩ and each capacitor is 0.01 μF.

Given:

  • R = 10 kΩ = 10,000 Ω
  • C = 0.01 μF = 10 × 10−9 F
f = 1 / (2πRC√6)

f = 1 / (2 × 3.1416 × 10,000 × 10 × 10−9 × √6)

f ≈ 649 Hz

15. Practical Oscillator Troubleshooting

Problem Possible Cause Checking Method
No output Incorrect supply, wrong feedback connection or insufficient gain Check supply and circuit connections
Unstable frequency Component tolerance, temperature or poor layout Measure frequency over time
Low amplitude Insufficient loop gain or excessive loading Check amplifier gain and load
Distorted output Amplifier clipping or poor amplitude control Observe waveform on oscilloscope
Wrong frequency Incorrect R, C, L or crystal value Measure component values and output frequency

Oscilloscope Testing

  1. Verify the oscillator supply voltage.
  2. Connect the oscilloscope probe using a suitable ground reference.
  3. Observe the output waveform.
  4. Measure frequency and peak-to-peak voltage.
  5. Check for clipping, noise and unwanted oscillation.
  6. Compare measured frequency with the calculated value.
When testing a real circuit, use the correct probe attenuation and avoid loading a high-impedance oscillator node. Observe equipment grounding and supply-voltage safety.

16. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is an oscillator?

An oscillator is an electronic circuit that generates a periodic signal without requiring a periodic external input signal.

Q2. What is the Barkhausen criterion?

It states that the loop-gain magnitude must be unity and the total loop phase shift must be an integer multiple of 360° for ideal sustained oscillation.

Q3. What is the startup condition?

The loop-gain magnitude is generally designed to be slightly greater than unity at the intended frequency.

Q4. What happens when loop gain is less than unity?

An initial oscillation generally decays if the phase condition is satisfied and the loop gain remains below unity.

Q5. Which oscillator is suitable for low-distortion sine waves?

Wien bridge oscillator.

Q6. Which oscillator uses an inductive divider?

Hartley oscillator.

Q7. Which oscillator uses a capacitive divider?

Colpitts oscillator.

Q8. Why is a crystal oscillator used in microcontrollers?

It provides a stable clock reference for timing and processor operation.

Q9. What is the frequency equation of a Wien bridge oscillator?

For equal R and C values, f = 1/(2πRC).

Q10. Why is amplitude stabilization required?

It prevents unlimited amplitude growth and helps maintain a stable, low-distortion output waveform.

18. Lesson Summary

  • An oscillator generates a periodic signal without a periodic external input.
  • Oscillators use an amplifier and a frequency-selective feedback network.
  • The Barkhausen criterion requires unity loop-gain magnitude and a total phase shift of 0° modulo 360°.
  • Startup generally requires loop-gain magnitude greater than unity.
  • Amplitude stabilization establishes steady-state oscillation.
  • RC phase-shift and Wien bridge oscillators use RC networks.
  • Hartley and Colpitts oscillators use LC networks.
  • Crystal oscillators provide high frequency stability.
  • Oscillators are essential in clocks, communication systems and signal generators.
