Lesson 4: Gain, Bandwidth and Stability

Understand the effect of negative feedback on amplifier gain, bandwidth, frequency response, distortion, noise and stability.

1. Introduction

An amplifier increases the amplitude of an input signal. Its performance is commonly evaluated using voltage gain, current gain, power gain, bandwidth, distortion and stability.

Negative feedback returns a portion of the output signal to the input with an opposing phase. It changes the overall gain and can improve several important amplifier characteristics.

2. Amplifier Gain

Gain is the ratio of output signal magnitude to input signal magnitude. It indicates how much an amplifier increases a signal.

A. Voltage Gain

Av = Vo / Vi

Where:
  • Av = Voltage gain
  • Vo = Output voltage
  • Vi = Input voltage

B. Current Gain

Ai = Io / Ii

Where Io is output current and Ii is input current.

C. Power Gain

Ap = Po / Pi

Where Po is output power and Pi is input power.

D. Gain in Decibels

Gain is often expressed in decibels (dB), particularly when analysing frequency response.

Voltage gain:
Av(dB) = 20 log10 |Vo / Vi|

Power gain:
Ap(dB) = 10 log10(Po / Pi)

3. Gain with Negative Feedback

Consider an amplifier with open-loop gain A and feedback factor β. The loop gain is the product Aβ.

Closed-loop gain:

Af = A / (1 + Aβ)

Where:
  • A = Open-loop gain
  • β = Feedback factor
  • Aβ = Loop gain
  • Af = Gain with negative feedback

When loop gain is sufficiently large, the closed-loop gain becomes less dependent on the exact open-loop gain.

Gain Desensitivity

Gain desensitivity describes how much negative feedback reduces the sensitivity of closed-loop gain to changes in open-loop gain.

Desensitivity factor = 1 + Aβ

Af = A / (1 + Aβ)

A larger loop gain generally produces better gain stabilization, provided the feedback loop remains stable.

4. Numerical Example: Gain Reduction

An amplifier has an open-loop gain of 100 and a feedback factor of 0.09. Calculate its closed-loop gain.

Given:

  • A = 100
  • β = 0.09

Step 1: Calculate loop gain.

Aβ = 100 × 0.09
Aβ = 9

Step 2: Calculate closed-loop gain.

Af = A / (1 + Aβ)

Af = 100 / (1 + 9)

Af = 10
Negative feedback reduces the gain from 100 to 10 in this example. The gain is now less sensitive to changes in open-loop gain.

5. Frequency Response of an Amplifier

An amplifier does not provide the same gain at every frequency. Its gain varies with the frequency of the input signal.

A typical amplifier has three frequency regions:

  • Low-frequency region: Gain decreases because of coupling and bypass capacitors.
  • Mid-frequency region: Gain is approximately constant.
  • High-frequency region: Gain decreases because of transistor capacitances, wiring capacitance and other parasitic effects.

Conceptual frequency response showing low-frequency and high-frequency gain roll-off.

6. Bandwidth

Bandwidth is the range of frequencies over which an amplifier provides its specified useful gain.

The lower and upper cutoff frequencies are commonly defined at the points where voltage gain falls to 0.707 of its midband value.

Bandwidth (BW) = fH − fL

Where:
  • fL = Lower cutoff frequency
  • fH = Upper cutoff frequency

Why 0.707?

At the half-power point, output power is half of its midband value. Since power is proportional to voltage squared for a fixed resistance:

Vcutoff / Vmid = √(1/2)

Vcutoff / Vmid ≈ 0.707

Gain reduction = −3 dB

7. Effect of Negative Feedback on Bandwidth

In a common single-pole amplifier model, negative feedback reduces midband gain while extending the lower and upper cutoff frequencies.

For a suitable dominant-pole model with approximately frequency-independent feedback factor:

Lower cutoff frequency with feedback:
fLf ≈ fL / (1 + Aβ)

Upper cutoff frequency with feedback:
fHf ≈ fH × (1 + Aβ)

Therefore, the bandwidth generally increases in this simplified model.

These cutoff-frequency equations are approximations for a suitable single-pole amplifier model. Real amplifiers can have multiple poles, parasitic effects and frequency-dependent feedback networks. Their actual bandwidth and stability must be evaluated from the complete frequency response.

8. Gain-Bandwidth Product

For a suitable single-dominant-pole amplifier, the gain-bandwidth product is approximately constant over the relevant operating range.

Gain × Bandwidth ≈ Constant

A1 × BW1 ≈ A2 × BW2

For example, if an amplifier provides a gain of 10 over a bandwidth of 100 kHz, a gain of 20 may reduce its bandwidth to approximately 50 kHz under the same simplified model.

Do not assume a constant gain-bandwidth product for every discrete amplifier. It is especially useful for understanding compensated operational amplifiers and dominant-pole systems.

9. Effect of Negative Feedback on Distortion

Nonlinear distortion occurs when an amplifier does not reproduce the input waveform accurately. The output waveform may contain additional harmonic components.

Negative feedback can reduce distortion by correcting part of the difference between the desired and actual output.

Distortion with feedback ≈ Distortion without feedback / (1 + Aβ)

This approximation assumes the amplifier operates in its linear range and the feedback loop has sufficient loop gain.

Example

If an amplifier has 10% distortion without feedback and its loop gain is 9:

Df = 10% / (1 + 9)

Df = 1%

This is an idealized estimate. Feedback cannot completely eliminate distortion caused by clipping, saturation or insufficient output-stage capability.

10. Effect of Negative Feedback on Noise

Noise is an unwanted electrical signal that affects the quality of the amplifier output.

Negative feedback can reduce the contribution of noise generated within the amplifier when that noise is inside the effective feedback loop.

Approximate internal noise reduction factor:

1 + Aβ

However, feedback does not remove all noise. Noise generated by the source, feedback resistors and components outside the effective loop may remain.

11. Stability of a Feedback Amplifier

Stability describes whether an amplifier maintains controlled operation without unwanted oscillations or excessive ringing.

A negative-feedback system can become unstable if its total loop phase shift and loop gain cause the feedback signal to reinforce the input instead of opposing it.

Loop Gain

T = Aβ

T is the loop gain.

Both magnitude and phase of loop gain vary with frequency. Stability therefore cannot be determined from the magnitude of Aβ alone.

12. Phase Shift and Oscillation Risk

Every amplifier introduces phase shift. Additional phase shifts may be introduced by capacitors, inductors and feedback networks.

At a frequency where the total loop phase shift reaches approximately 180° in an otherwise negative-feedback convention, the feedback can effectively become positive. If the loop gain magnitude is at least unity at that frequency, sustained oscillation or instability may occur.

The exact phase criterion depends on the sign convention used for the loop transfer function. Always evaluate the complete loop gain rather than relying on a phase angle in isolation.

13. Phase Margin and Gain Margin

A. Phase Margin (PM)

Phase margin measures how far the loop phase is from the critical −180° condition when loop-gain magnitude reaches unity (0 dB).

PM = 180° + Phase of T(jωgc)

Where ωgc is the gain-crossover frequency.

A larger positive phase margin generally indicates better relative stability and less ringing, although the required margin depends on the application.

B. Gain Margin (GM)

Gain margin measures how much the loop gain can increase before reaching the critical oscillation condition at the phase-crossover frequency.

GM = 1 / |T(jωpc)|

In decibels:
GM(dB) = −20 log10 |T(jωpc)|

Where ωpc is the phase-crossover frequency.

14. Bode Plot and Stability Analysis

A Bode plot shows the magnitude and phase of a system's frequency response against logarithmic frequency. It is widely used to evaluate feedback stability.

Conceptual Bode magnitude and phase plots. Actual curves depend on the amplifier and feedback network.

15. Practical Causes of Instability

  • Excessive phase shift in the feedback loop.
  • Unintended parasitic capacitance and inductance.
  • Long wiring and poor PCB layout.
  • Incorrect feedback component values.
  • Insufficient phase margin.
  • Heavy capacitive loading at the amplifier output.
  • Inappropriate compensation components.

16. Practical Methods to Improve Stability

  • Use appropriate frequency compensation.
  • Keep feedback paths short.
  • Use proper PCB grounding and layout techniques.
  • Decouple power-supply rails near the amplifier.
  • Check phase margin and gain margin.
  • Use output isolation or compensation when required by the datasheet.
  • Avoid excessive capacitive loading unless the amplifier supports it.

17. Comparison: Amplifier Without and With Negative Feedback

Parameter Without Feedback With Negative Feedback
Gain Generally higher Reduced and more controlled
Bandwidth Generally narrower in the single-pole model Generally wider in the single-pole model
Distortion Higher Can be reduced
Internal noise Not suppressed by feedback Some internal noise can be reduced
Gain stability More sensitive to parameter changes Generally improved
Stability No feedback-loop instability Requires loop stability analysis

18. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is amplifier gain?

Gain is the ratio of output signal magnitude to input signal magnitude.

Q2. What is closed-loop gain?

It is the amplifier gain when a feedback network is connected around the amplifier.

Q3. Write the negative-feedback gain equation.

Af = A / (1 + Aβ).

Q4. What is bandwidth?

Bandwidth is the difference between the upper and lower cutoff frequencies.

Q5. What is the −3 dB frequency?

It is the frequency at which power falls to half its reference midband value, corresponding to a voltage magnitude of approximately 0.707 for fixed resistance.

Q6. How does negative feedback affect bandwidth?

In a suitable single-pole model, it reduces gain and increases bandwidth.

Q7. What is phase margin?

It measures the separation from the critical −180° loop phase at the unity loop-gain frequency.

Q8. What is gain margin?

It measures how much loop gain can increase before reaching the critical oscillation condition at the phase-crossover frequency.

Q9. Can negative feedback cause oscillation?

Yes. If the loop phase and magnitude meet the instability condition, a nominally negative-feedback system can become unstable.

Q10. Does negative feedback remove all noise?

No. It can reduce some noise generated within the feedback loop, but source noise and other noise contributions remain.

20. Lesson Summary

  • Gain measures the ratio of output to input signal.
  • Negative-feedback gain is Af = A / (1 + Aβ).
  • Negative feedback improves gain desensitivity.
  • Bandwidth is fH − fL.
  • The −3 dB point corresponds to approximately 0.707 voltage gain.
  • Negative feedback can increase bandwidth in a suitable single-pole model.
  • Negative feedback can reduce distortion and some internal noise.
  • Stability depends on loop-gain magnitude and phase.
  • Phase margin and gain margin are important stability measures.
  • Poor layout, parasitic elements and excessive loading can cause instability.
