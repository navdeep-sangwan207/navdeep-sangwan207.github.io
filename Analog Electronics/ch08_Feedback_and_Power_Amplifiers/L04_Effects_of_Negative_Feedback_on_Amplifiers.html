Chapter 8: Feedback and Power Amplifiers

Lecture 4: Effects of Negative Feedback on Amplifiers

Gain Stability • Bandwidth • Distortion • Noise • Sensitivity • Impedance

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand how negative feedback changes amplifier gain.
  • Calculate closed-loop gain and desensitivity.
  • Explain gain stability and bandwidth improvement.
  • Understand the effects on distortion, noise and frequency response.
  • Analyze the effects of negative feedback on input and output resistance.
  • Recognize practical limitations such as noise, loading and instability.

2. Introduction

Negative feedback occurs when a portion of the output signal is returned to the input in such a way that it opposes the applied input signal.

The resulting error signal drives the amplifier:

Vi = Vs − Vf

where:

  • Vs = source input signal
  • Vf = feedback signal
  • Vi = amplifier error signal

3. Closed-Loop Gain Reduction

Let A be the open-loop voltage gain and β be the feedback factor.

The output is:

Vo = A(Vs − Vf)

Since Vf = βVo:

Vo = A(Vs − βVo)

Rearranging:

Vo(1 + Aβ) = AVs

Therefore, the closed-loop gain is:

Af = Vo/Vs = A/(1 + Aβ)

Loop gain: T = Aβ. The quantity 1 + Aβ is called the desensitivity factor for the standard negative-feedback model.

Worked example

An amplifier has open-loop gain A = 1000 and feedback factor β = 0.09.

T = 1000 × 0.09 = 90

Af = 1000/(1 + 90) = 10.99

The open-loop gain is 1000, but the closed-loop gain is approximately 10.99.

4. Effect 1: Gain Stability

Negative feedback reduces the sensitivity of closed-loop gain to changes in amplifier gain.

Af = A/(1 + Aβ)

For constant β, the relative sensitivity is:

S = (ΔAf/Af)/(ΔA/A) = 1/(1 + Aβ)

For high loop gain, S becomes small. Thus, changes in transistor parameters, temperature or supply conditions have a reduced effect on the overall closed-loop gain.

Worked example

Suppose Aβ = 99.

S = 1/(1 + 99) = 0.01

If open-loop gain changes by 10%, the approximate closed-loop gain change is:

0.01 × 10% = 0.1%

This result assumes the feedback factor remains constant and the amplifier stays in its linear operating region.

5. Effect 2: Bandwidth Improvement

Negative feedback generally reduces midband gain while extending the usable bandwidth in a simple dominant-pole amplifier model.

For a single-pole amplifier:

Af(s) = A0/(1 + A0β + s/ωH)

where A0 is the open-loop low-frequency gain and ωH is the original upper cutoff angular frequency.

The approximate new upper cutoff frequency is:

fHf ≈ fH(1 + A0β)

The lower cutoff frequency for a simple high-pass response is approximately reduced by the same factor:

fLf ≈ fL/(1 + A0β)

These relations illustrate the gain-bandwidth trade-off for a suitable single-pole model.

Illustrative frequency-response curves, not measured data. Actual amplifier response depends on its poles, zeros, loading and feedback network.

6. Effect 3: Reduction in Nonlinear Distortion

Nonlinear distortion occurs when an amplifier does not reproduce the input waveform proportionally. Negative feedback can reduce distortion by correcting part of the output error.

For a simplified model, the residual distortion is approximately:

Df ≈ D/(1 + Aβ)

where D is the distortion before feedback and Df is the distortion after feedback.

Example

If the original distortion is 5% and Aβ = 9:

Df ≈ 5%/10 = 0.5%

Conceptual comparison: feedback can reduce distortion generated within the amplifier, provided sufficient loop gain is available at the distortion frequencies.

7. Effect 4: Noise Reduction

Negative feedback can reduce some noise generated within the amplifier by reducing the closed-loop effect of disturbances that enter through the amplifier's internal forward path.

For a disturbance modeled as an additive signal at the amplifier output:

Noise contribution at output ≈ N/(1 + Aβ)

However, negative feedback does not automatically reduce every type of noise. Noise generated by the feedback resistors, source impedance or input stage may be affected differently.

Practical point: Always distinguish between amplifier-generated noise, source noise and feedback-network noise. The complete noise performance must be calculated or measured over the required bandwidth.

8. Effect 5: Input and Output Impedance

Impedance changes depend on the feedback topology.

TopologyInput resistanceOutput resistance
Voltage-seriesIncreasesDecreases
Voltage-shuntDecreasesDecreases
Current-seriesIncreasesIncreases
Current-shuntDecreasesIncreases

For an idealized model with loop gain T:

Series mixing: Rif ≈ Ri(1 + T)

Shunt mixing: Rif ≈ Ri/(1 + T)

Voltage sampling: Rof ≈ Ro/(1 + T)

Current sampling: Rof ≈ Ro(1 + T)

9. Effect 6: Gain-Bandwidth Trade-Off

Negative feedback trades excess open-loop gain for improved performance. In a simple single-pole amplifier, the product of closed-loop gain and upper bandwidth remains approximately constant:

Af × fHf ≈ A0 × fH

This is an idealized gain-bandwidth relationship. Real amplifiers may have multiple poles, zeros and slew-rate limitations.

12. Limitations of Negative Feedback

  • Closed-loop gain is lower than open-loop gain.
  • Feedback may cause instability or oscillation if phase margin is inadequate.
  • Feedback network components introduce tolerances and noise.
  • Large input signals may cause clipping even with feedback.
  • Bandwidth extension is limited by the amplifier's frequency response.
  • Slew-rate limitations cannot be removed by ordinary linear feedback.
  • Feedback cannot correct distortion or noise that lies outside the effective loop bandwidth.
Stability warning: A negative-feedback connection at low frequency may become effectively positive at some higher frequency due to phase shift. Compensation and stability analysis are essential in practical amplifier design.

13. Practical Lab: Measuring Feedback Effects

Objective

Compare the gain, bandwidth and distortion of an amplifier with and without negative feedback.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage amplifier circuit or simulator.
  • Function generator or simulated sine-wave source.
  • Oscilloscope or simulation waveform viewer.
  • Multimeter and suitable resistors.

Procedure

  1. Measure the amplifier gain without the feedback network, if the circuit allows safe operation in that configuration.
  2. Connect the designed negative-feedback network.
  3. Measure the new closed-loop gain.
  4. Sweep the input frequency and identify the lower and upper cutoff frequencies.
  5. Apply a suitable low-distortion sine-wave input and observe the output waveform.
  6. Compare distortion and clipping behavior at the same output amplitude where possible.
  7. Record gain, bandwidth and waveform observations.

Expected result

The closed-loop gain should be more stable and lower than the open-loop gain. In a suitable single-pole design, bandwidth generally increases as gain decreases.

Do not disconnect feedback from an amplifier that requires it for stable operation unless the test circuit is specifically designed for open-loop testing.

17. Summary

  • Negative feedback reduces closed-loop gain.
  • It improves gain stability and reduces sensitivity to open-loop gain variations.
  • It can extend bandwidth in suitable amplifier models.
  • It reduces some forms of nonlinear distortion and internally generated noise.
  • It changes input and output impedance according to feedback topology.
  • Insufficient phase margin can lead to oscillation.
  • Practical performance depends on amplifier bandwidth, loading and feedback-network design.
