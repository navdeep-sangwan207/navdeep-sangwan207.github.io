Lesson 4: Thermal Stability and Stability Factor

Understand temperature effects, thermal runaway, transistor stabilization and stability-factor calculations.

Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers

1. What is Thermal Stability?

Thermal stability is the ability of a transistor biasing circuit to maintain a reasonably constant operating point when temperature changes.

A BJT's collector current depends on several parameters, including base-emitter voltage, current gain and collector-base leakage current. These parameters can change with temperature.

If collector current increases excessively, the transistor may dissipate more power and become hotter. This can cause further increases in collector current.

Objective of thermal stabilization: Maintain the transistor's Q-point within safe limits despite temperature and device-parameter variations.

2. Effect of Temperature on BJT Parameters

Temperature affects the electrical characteristics of a BJT in several important ways.

2.1 Base-Emitter Voltage (VBE)

For a silicon BJT at approximately constant collector current, VBE generally decreases as junction temperature increases.

A commonly used approximate temperature coefficient is around −2 mV/°C, although the actual value depends on operating current and device characteristics.

2.2 Collector-Base Leakage Current (ICBO)

ICBO is the reverse leakage current of the collector-base junction when the emitter is open.

It increases significantly with temperature. Its exact temperature dependence varies with the semiconductor process and device.

2.3 Current Gain (β)

The DC current gain β can also change with temperature and operating current. Its variation is device-dependent.

Parameter Typical Temperature Effect Possible Consequence
VBE Decreases as temperature rises at constant IC Can increase collector current in a fixed-bias circuit
ICBO Increases with temperature Can contribute to increased collector current
β May increase or decrease depending on device and operating point Changes the relationship between IB and IC

3. What is Thermal Runaway?

Thermal runaway is a potentially destructive positive-feedback process in which increasing junction temperature causes increased transistor power dissipation, leading to further heating.

Thermal Runaway Process

  1. Junction temperature increases.
  2. Collector current may increase due to temperature-dependent parameters.
  3. Transistor power dissipation increases.
  4. Additional heat is generated.
  5. Junction temperature rises further.
  6. The transistor may be damaged if the process is not controlled.

Thermal Runaway Feedback Cycle

Thermal runaway can destroy a transistor. Proper bias stabilization, heat sinking and operation within the device's safe operating area are important.

4. Transistor Power Dissipation

The approximate DC power dissipated by a transistor under normal forward-active conditions is:

PD ≈ VCE × IC

Where:

  • PD = transistor power dissipation in watts.
  • VCE = collector-emitter voltage in volts.
  • IC = collector current in amperes.

Numerical Example

A transistor operates with VCE = 6 V and IC = 20 mA. Calculate its approximate power dissipation.

PD = VCE × IC

PD = 6 × 0.02

PD = 0.12 W
Answer: PD = 120 mW.

The transistor must be selected so that its power, voltage and current ratings are not exceeded under the actual thermal conditions.

5. What is Stability Factor?

The stability factor describes how sensitive the collector current is to changes in temperature-dependent transistor parameters.

A commonly used stability factor is defined with respect to collector-base leakage current:

S = ∂IC / ∂ICBO

In this definition, the derivative is taken while keeping the relevant bias-circuit and transistor parameters constant.

Interpretation: A smaller value of S means that collector current is less sensitive to changes in ICBO.

Other Stability Factors

Some textbooks also define sensitivity factors with respect to base-emitter voltage and current gain. These are different quantities and should not be confused with the leakage-current stability factor.

SVBE = ∂IC / ∂VBE

Sβ = ∂IC / ∂β

6. Stability Factor of Fixed Bias

For a fixed-bias transistor, the approximate collector current including collector-base leakage current is:

IC = βIB + (β + 1)ICBO

For a fixed base current IB, differentiate with respect to ICBO:

S = ∂IC / ∂ICBO

S = β + 1

Therefore, the stability factor of fixed bias is:

S = β + 1

For example, if β = 100:

S = 100 + 1 = 101

This indicates considerable sensitivity to leakage-current changes compared with a well-designed stabilized bias circuit.

7. Stability Factor of Voltage Divider Bias

Consider a voltage-divider bias circuit replaced by its Thevenin equivalent. It has:

  • Thevenin voltage VTH.
  • Thevenin resistance RTH.
  • Emitter resistance RE.

For the leakage-current stability analysis, use:

IC = βIB + (β + 1)ICBO

IE = IC + IB

VTH = IB RTH + VBE + IE RE

Treating VTH, RTH, RE, VBE and β as constants while differentiating with respect to ICBO gives:

S = (β + 1)(RTH + RE) / [RTH + (β + 1)RE]

This expression is for the common leakage-current stability-factor model of a voltage-divider bias circuit with emitter resistance.

Increasing emitter resistance relative to the Thevenin resistance generally reduces the leakage-current stability factor.

8. Comparison of Stability Factors

Biasing Method Leakage Stability Factor Relative Stability
Fixed Bias β + 1 Poor
Collector-to-Base Bias Lower than fixed bias under normal feedback conditions Improved
Voltage Divider Bias with RE (β + 1)(RTH + RE) / [RTH + (β + 1)RE] Generally good

Stability factor alone does not capture every temperature effect. A complete design must also consider VBE and beta variation, transistor power dissipation and thermal resistance.

9. Methods of Thermal Stabilization

9.1 Emitter Resistor Stabilization

An emitter resistor introduces negative feedback. If collector current increases, emitter voltage rises, reducing VBE and opposing the increase in current.

9.2 Heat Sink

A heat sink transfers heat from the transistor package to the surrounding air, reducing junction temperature for a given power dissipation.

9.3 Diode Compensation

A suitably selected forward-biased diode can be used in a bias network to provide a temperature-dependent voltage that approximately tracks the BJT base-emitter voltage.

9.4 Thermistor Compensation

A thermistor can be placed in a bias network so that its resistance changes with temperature and compensates for changes in transistor current.

9.5 Thermal Tracking

Compensation components can be mounted close to the transistor so they experience similar temperature changes. Their thermal characteristics should be selected appropriately.

10. Practical Thermal Design Considerations

  • Check maximum collector current and collector-emitter voltage.
  • Check the transistor's maximum power dissipation.
  • Use a suitable heat sink when required.
  • Check the safe operating area (SOA).
  • Allow for ambient temperature and thermal resistance.
  • Consider transistor parameter variations and component tolerances.
TJ ≈ TA + PD × θJA

Where:

  • TJ = junction temperature.
  • TA = ambient temperature.
  • PD = power dissipation.
  • θJA = junction-to-ambient thermal resistance.

This is a simplified steady-state thermal estimate. With a heat sink, use the relevant junction-to-case, interface and case-to-sink thermal resistances as well as the sink-to-ambient resistance.

11. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is thermal stability?

It is the ability of a transistor circuit to maintain a reasonably stable operating point despite temperature changes.

Q2. What is thermal runaway?

It is a positive-feedback process in which increasing temperature causes increased current and power dissipation, leading to further heating.

Q3. What happens to VBE when temperature increases?

At approximately constant collector current, VBE generally decreases as temperature increases.

Q4. Define stability factor.

The leakage-current stability factor is S = ∂IC/∂ICBO, with other relevant parameters held constant.

Q5. What is the stability factor of fixed bias?

S = β + 1.

Q6. How does an emitter resistor improve stability?

It provides negative feedback by opposing changes in emitter and collector current.

Q7. What is the purpose of a heat sink?

It transfers heat away from the transistor and helps maintain junction temperature within safe limits.

Q8. Does a heat sink replace proper bias stabilization?

No. A heat sink manages heat transfer, while bias stabilization controls the electrical operating point. Both may be required.

13. Lesson Summary

  • Temperature affects VBE, ICBO and beta.
  • Thermal runaway is a potentially destructive positive-feedback process.
  • Power dissipation is approximately VCE × IC in normal DC operation.
  • The leakage-current stability factor is S = ∂IC/∂ICBO.
  • Fixed-bias stability factor is β + 1.
  • Emitter resistance provides negative feedback.
  • Heat sinks and compensation networks help manage thermal effects.
Remember: Good bias stabilization prevents excessive changes in collector current, while proper thermal design keeps the transistor junction within its safe operating temperature.
