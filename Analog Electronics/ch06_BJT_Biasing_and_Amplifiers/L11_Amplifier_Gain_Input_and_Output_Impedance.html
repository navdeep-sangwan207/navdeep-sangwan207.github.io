Amplifier Gain, Input and Output Impedance

Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers | Lecture 11

1. Learning Objectives

  • Define voltage, current and power gain.
  • Calculate gain in linear form and decibels.
  • Understand amplifier input and output impedance.
  • Analyse source loading and output loading.
  • Understand impedance matching and maximum power transfer.
  • Calculate cascaded amplifier gain.
  • Measure amplifier performance using a function generator, oscilloscope and multimeter.

2. Amplifier as a Two-Port Network

An amplifier receives an input signal from a source and delivers an amplified signal to a load. Its behaviour can be studied using input and output ports.

The amplifier is characterised by its gain, input impedance and output impedance. These parameters determine how effectively it transfers a signal between a source and a load.

3. Voltage Gain

Voltage gain is the ratio of output voltage to input voltage.

Av = Vout / Vin

For example, if Vin = 20 mV and Vout = 2 V:

Av = 2 / 0.020 = 100

The sign of voltage gain describes phase relationship when using signed instantaneous or phasor voltage definitions.

  • Common-emitter amplifier: typically negative voltage gain, indicating approximately 180° phase inversion.
  • Common-base amplifier: typically positive voltage gain.
  • Common-collector amplifier: positive gain slightly below unity.

4. Current Gain

Ai = Iout / Iin

Current gain describes how much output current is delivered relative to input current. The exact result depends on the port-current definitions and whether current is measured at the transistor terminal or at the complete amplifier input.

For a common-emitter transistor, the transistor current gain is approximately:

β = IC / IB

For a common-collector transistor, the base-to-emitter current gain is approximately β + 1.

5. Power Gain

Power gain is the ratio of output power delivered to the load to input power accepted at the amplifier input.

Ap = Pout / Pin

For a resistive load and RMS sinusoidal quantities:

P = V²/R = I²R = VI

Therefore, if the input and output resistances differ:

Ap = (Vout²/Rout-load) / (Vin²/Rin-source)

For a simplified matched resistive input/output comparison:

Ap = Av × Ai
Do not assume power gain is always equal to voltage gain multiplied by current gain unless the voltage and current gain definitions use consistent input and output port quantities.

7. Input Impedance

Input impedance is the impedance presented by the amplifier to the signal source.

Zin = Vin / Iin

For a predominantly resistive midband input, it is often approximated by Rin.

A high input impedance is desirable when the source has a high output impedance because it reduces signal loading.

Practical effects

  • Low amplifier input impedance can reduce the voltage received from a sensor or signal source.
  • The bias resistors in a transistor amplifier contribute to its total input impedance.
  • Input coupling capacitors affect impedance at low frequencies.
  • Input impedance may vary with frequency.

8. Output Impedance

Output impedance represents the internal impedance seen looking back into the amplifier output with the input signal set to zero and independent sources treated according to the small-signal model.

Vout = Vopen × RL/(Rout + RL)

This is the Thevenin voltage-divider model for a resistive output impedance and load.

Low output impedance is generally desirable when driving a relatively low-resistance load because it reduces output voltage loss.

9. Source Loading Effect

A practical signal source has an internal resistance Rs. When connected to an amplifier input resistance Rin, the input voltage is determined by a voltage divider.

Vin = Vs × Rin/(Rs + Rin)

Example: Vs = 1 V, Rs = 10 kΩ and Rin = 10 kΩ.

Vin = 1 × 10/(10 + 10) = 0.5 V

Only half the open-circuit source voltage reaches the amplifier input.

10. Output Loading Effect

An amplifier's internal output resistance Rout and external load RL form a voltage divider.

Vload = Vopen × RL/(Rout + RL)

Example: Vopen = 5 V, Rout = 1 kΩ and RL = 1 kΩ.

Vload = 5 × 1/(1 + 1) = 2.5 V
A load much larger than the amplifier's output resistance receives a voltage closer to the open-circuit output voltage.

12. Impedance Matching

Impedance matching means selecting source, amplifier and load impedances to achieve a desired transfer of voltage, current or power.

Voltage transfer objective

For a voltage amplifier, a high input impedance relative to source resistance and a low output impedance relative to load resistance are usually desirable.

Maximum power transfer theorem

For a purely resistive Thevenin source, maximum power is delivered to the load when:

RL = Rsource

For a linear AC network with complex impedance, maximum average power transfer occurs when the load impedance is the complex conjugate of the Thevenin impedance:

ZL = ZTH*
Maximum power transfer is not always the objective in voltage-amplifier interconnections. It can reduce voltage transfer efficiency and cause unnecessary loading. Audio, RF and sensor circuits use different matching strategies depending on their design goals.

14. Cascaded Amplifier Gain

When multiple amplifier stages are connected in series, the overall gain is the product of individual stage gains, provided the gain of each stage includes the effect of its actual load.

Atotal = A1 × A2 × A3 × ... × An

For voltage gains expressed in decibels:

Atotal(dB) = A1(dB) + A2(dB) + ... + An(dB)

Worked example

Two stages have voltage gains 10 and 20.

Atotal = 10 × 20 = 200
Atotal(dB) = 20 log10(200) ≈ 46.02 dB

If the second stage loads the first, use the loaded gain of the first stage rather than its unloaded gain.

18. Measuring Amplifier Gain in the Laboratory

Equipment

  • Function generator.
  • Dual-channel oscilloscope.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Low-voltage current-limited DC supply.
  • Amplifier circuit and resistive loads.

Measurement procedure

  1. Build and verify the amplifier's DC bias conditions.
  2. Set the function generator to a small sinusoidal signal.
  3. Connect oscilloscope Channel 1 to the input and Channel 2 to the output.
  4. Use the same ground reference for both measurements.
  5. Measure input and output amplitudes using the same convention, such as peak-to-peak voltage.
  6. Calculate voltage gain as Vout/Vin.
  7. Observe phase difference and waveform distortion.
  8. Change the load resistance and record the output voltage.
  9. Estimate output resistance using open-circuit and loaded measurements, where appropriate.
Measurement consistency: Do not compare input peak-to-peak voltage with output RMS voltage. Use the same amplitude convention for both signals.

19. Practical Output Resistance Measurement

One practical method is to measure the amplifier output with no external load, then connect a known load. If the amplifier is represented by a Thevenin voltage source Vopen and series output resistance Rout:

Vload = Vopen × RL/(Rout + RL)

Rearranging:

Rout = RL × (Vopen/Vload − 1)

Example

Suppose Vopen = 4 V, RL = 1 kΩ and Vload = 2 V.

Rout = 1k × (4/2 − 1) = 1 kΩ

This method assumes the amplifier remains linear and its output does not change due to current limiting, thermal effects or bias shifts.

21. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. Define voltage gain.

It is the ratio of output voltage to input voltage, measured using consistent voltage conventions.

2. Define current gain.

It is the ratio of output current to input current using the stated port-current definitions.

3. Define power gain.

It is the ratio of output power to input power.

4. What is voltage gain in decibels?

20 log10 of the magnitude of the voltage ratio, when the voltage reference impedances are the same or the ratio is explicitly defined as a voltage gain.

5. What is power gain in decibels?

10 log10 of the output-to-input power ratio.

6. What is input impedance?

The impedance presented by the amplifier at its input terminals.

7. What is output impedance?

The impedance looking back into the amplifier output with the input signal set to zero.

8. Why is high input impedance useful?

It reduces loading of a high-impedance source.

9. Why is low output impedance useful?

It helps maintain output voltage when driving a load.

10. What is loading effect?

It is the change in signal voltage or performance caused by connecting an input or output impedance.

11. State the maximum power transfer condition for resistive circuits.

RL equals the Thevenin source resistance.

12. What is the maximum power transfer condition for AC circuits?

The load impedance equals the complex conjugate of the Thevenin impedance.

13. How are cascaded linear voltage gains combined?

Multiply their linear gains, accounting for interstage loading.

14. How are cascaded gains in dB combined?

Add the individual gains in decibels.

15. Why does a common-emitter stage invert phase?

An increase in base drive increases collector current and the collector resistor voltage drop, reducing collector output voltage.

16. Why is a common-collector stage used as a buffer?

It has high input impedance and low output impedance.

17. How can output resistance be measured?

Compare open-circuit and loaded output voltages using a known load resistance.

18. What causes amplifier gain to fall at low frequencies?

Coupling and bypass capacitor reactance becomes significant.

19. What causes high-frequency gain reduction?

Transistor junction capacitances, transit time, parasitic capacitances and layout effects.

20. Why must gain measurements use consistent RMS or peak conventions?

Otherwise the ratio can be incorrect by a scale factor.

22. Practice Problems

  1. An amplifier receives 20 mV and produces 1.5 V. Calculate voltage gain and gain in dB.
  2. Input current is 50 µA and output current is 5 mA. Calculate current gain.
  3. An amplifier accepts 2 mW and delivers 100 mW. Calculate power gain in linear form and dB.
  4. A source has Rs = 5 kΩ and the amplifier has Rin = 20 kΩ. If the source voltage is 2 V, calculate amplifier input voltage.
  5. An amplifier has Vopen = 8 V, Rout = 500 Ω and RL = 2 kΩ. Calculate the loaded output voltage.
  6. Two amplifier stages have gains 5 and 20. Calculate total gain and dB gain.
  7. An output resistance measurement gives Vopen = 6 V, Vload = 4 V and RL = 2 kΩ. Calculate Rout.
  8. Explain why maximum power transfer matching is not always used in voltage amplifier design.
