Common-Collector Amplifier (Emitter Follower)

Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers | Lecture 09

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the common-collector (CC) transistor configuration.
  • Explain why it is called an emitter follower.
  • Analyse the DC bias and small-signal operation.
  • Calculate voltage gain, current gain and power gain.
  • Understand high input impedance and low output impedance.
  • Apply emitter followers to impedance matching and buffer stages.
  • Interpret input and output waveforms using an interactive animation.

2. Introduction to Common-Collector Configuration

In a common-collector amplifier, the collector terminal is common to the input and output circuits for AC analysis. The input is applied between base and collector, and the output is taken between emitter and collector.

  • Input terminal: Base.
  • Output terminal: Emitter.
  • Common terminal: Collector.
  • Output voltage: Approximately follows the input voltage.
  • Phase relationship: Input and output are in phase.
Emitter follower: The emitter voltage follows the base voltage, typically remaining approximately one base-emitter junction drop below it for an NPN transistor operating in forward-active mode.

3. Common-Collector Circuit Diagram

Important: The collector is connected to the DC supply. Since an ideal DC supply is AC ground, the collector is common to the input and output in small-signal analysis.

4. Working Principle

  1. A voltage-divider network establishes the transistor's DC base bias.
  2. The NPN transistor operates in the forward-active region.
  3. When the base voltage increases, the base-emitter voltage initially tends to increase.
  4. Emitter current increases, raising the emitter voltage.
  5. The rising emitter voltage opposes the original increase in base-emitter voltage.
  6. This local negative feedback makes the emitter voltage follow the base voltage.
VE ≈ VB − VBE

For a silicon transistor, VBE is often approximated as 0.6–0.8 V for basic DC calculations. Its actual value depends on current, temperature and device characteristics.

5. DC Analysis

For a voltage-divider biased emitter follower, replace R1 and R2 by their Thevenin equivalent:

VTH = VCC × R2/(R1 + R2)
RTH = R1 || R2

For an NPN transistor with emitter resistor RE and no additional DC load path:

IB = (VTH − VBE)/[RTH + (β + 1)RE]
IE = (β + 1)IB
IC ≈ βIB
VE = IERE
VB ≈ VE + VBE
VC = VCC
VCE = VCC − VE

If a load is connected through a coupling capacitor, it normally does not affect the DC operating point. If the load has a DC connection, it must be included in the DC analysis.

6. Worked DC Bias Example

Given: VCC = 12 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, β = 100 and VBE = 0.7 V.

Step 1: Calculate the Thevenin equivalent.

VTH = 12 × 10/(47 + 10) = 2.105 V
RTH = 47 kΩ || 10 kΩ = 8.246 kΩ

Step 2: Calculate base current.

IB = (2.105 − 0.7)/[8.246k + 101 × 1k]
IB ≈ 12.87 µA

Step 3: Calculate emitter current.

IE = 101 × 12.87 µA ≈ 1.300 mA

Step 4: Calculate emitter and collector-emitter voltages.

VE = 1.300 mA × 1 kΩ = 1.300 V
VCE = 12 − 1.300 = 10.70 V
Estimated operating point: IE ≈ 1.30 mA and VCE ≈ 10.70 V. This is consistent with forward-active operation in the simplified model.

7. Small-Signal Analysis

For midband AC analysis:

  • DC voltage sources are treated as AC ground.
  • Coupling capacitors are approximated as short circuits if their reactance is sufficiently small.
  • The transistor is replaced by its hybrid-π or T-model equivalent.
  • The emitter resistor and AC load are included in the emitter-side impedance.

Define the effective emitter load:

RE' = RE || RL

For a base-driven emitter follower, neglecting ro and assuming the collector is at AC ground:

Av ≈ (β + 1)RE' / [rπ + (β + 1)RE']

Since rπ = β/gm, an alternative approximate expression is:

Av ≈ RE' / (re + RE')

where re ≈ 1/gm. These expressions assume the input voltage is measured directly at the transistor base. Source resistance and bias-network loading reduce the overall source-to-output gain.

8. Voltage Gain

The emitter follower has a voltage gain slightly less than unity under normal operating conditions.

Av = Vout/Vin
Av < 1

Although the voltage gain is less than one, the circuit can deliver substantial current gain and power gain.

For example, if the input changes by 100 mV and the emitter output changes by 95 mV:

Av = 95/100 = 0.95
The emitter follower is not primarily used to amplify voltage. It is used as a buffer to transfer a signal from a high-impedance source to a lower-impedance load.

9. Current Gain

Emitter current is the sum of base and collector currents:

IE = IC + IB

For a transistor in forward-active operation:

IE = (β + 1)IB

Therefore, the approximate transistor current gain from base current to emitter current is:

Ai ≈ IE/IB = β + 1

The exact current gain of a complete circuit depends on how input and output currents are defined and on the source and load impedances.

10. Input Impedance

The emitter follower has relatively high input impedance. A simplified base input resistance is:

Rin(base) ≈ rπ + (β + 1)RE'

Including the bias divider:

Rin(total) ≈ R1 || R2 || Rin(base)

The emitter-side load is reflected to the base multiplied approximately by β + 1. This is one of the main reasons the emitter follower is useful for buffering.

11. Output Impedance

The emitter follower has relatively low output impedance. A simplified estimate looking into the emitter is:

Rout ≈ re + (Rsource || R1 || R2)/ (β + 1)

Here Rsource is the signal generator's Thevenin resistance, and the estimate neglects ro and other parasitic effects. A more complete calculation uses the small-signal model with the actual bias network, transistor output resistance and load.

A low output impedance helps the circuit drive a load without a large reduction in output voltage.

14. Impedance Matching and Buffer Applications

An emitter follower is frequently placed between a signal source and a load when the source cannot drive the load directly.

Typical applications

  • Impedance matching between circuit stages.
  • Buffering high-impedance sensor outputs.
  • Driving relatively low-resistance loads from weak signal sources.
  • Output stages in analog circuits.
  • Level shifting and signal isolation in suitable circuit arrangements.
  • Buffer stages in audio and instrumentation circuits.

16. Worked Numerical Problem

Problem: An emitter follower has β = 100, IC = 2 mA, RE = 1 kΩ and RL = 10 kΩ. Assume VT = 26 mV. Estimate the voltage gain, neglecting ro.

Step 1: Calculate transconductance.

gm = IC/VT = 2 mA/26 mV = 76.92 mS

Step 2: Calculate small-signal emitter resistance.

re ≈ 1/gm = 13 Ω

Step 3: Calculate the effective emitter load.

RE' = RE || RL = 1k || 10k = 909.1 Ω

Step 4: Estimate voltage gain.

Av ≈ RE'/(re + RE')
Av ≈ 909.1/(13 + 909.1) = 0.986
The estimated voltage gain is approximately 0.986. The output is nearly equal in amplitude to the input and is in phase with it.

17. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Construct an NPN emitter follower and measure voltage gain and output impedance behaviour.

Components and equipment

  • BC547 or 2N2222 NPN transistor; verify the actual device pinout.
  • Current-limited low-voltage DC supply.
  • Bias resistors R1 and R2.
  • Emitter resistor RE and coupling capacitor Cin.
  • Function generator, oscilloscope and multimeter.
  • Selectable load resistors.

Procedure

  1. Build the voltage-divider biased emitter follower with power off.
  2. Apply the supply and measure VB, VE and VC.
  3. Calculate VBE and VCE; confirm forward-active operation.
  4. Apply a small sinusoidal signal to the base through Cin.
  5. Measure input and emitter output amplitudes.
  6. Calculate voltage gain and observe that there is no phase inversion.
  7. Connect different load resistances and record the output amplitude.
  8. Compare the loaded and unloaded output behaviour.

19. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a common-collector amplifier?

A BJT amplifier with the collector common to the input and output AC circuits.

2. Why is it called an emitter follower?

Because the emitter voltage follows the base voltage with a small base-emitter voltage difference.

3. What is the approximate voltage gain?

It is slightly less than unity under normal operating conditions.

4. Does the emitter follower invert phase?

No. Its input and output are approximately in phase.

5. What is the current gain?

The transistor base-to-emitter current gain is approximately β + 1.

6. Why is its input impedance high?

The emitter-side impedance is reflected to the base by approximately β + 1.

7. Why is its output impedance low?

Emitter feedback reduces the effective output resistance.

8. What is its main application?

Buffering and impedance matching.

9. Can an emitter follower amplify voltage?

It generally does not provide voltage gain greater than one, but it can provide current and power gain.

10. What is the purpose of RE?

It establishes emitter current and provides DC negative feedback.

11. What is the relationship between VB and VE?

For a forward-active NPN transistor, VE is approximately VB − VBE.

12. What happens if the load resistance decreases?

The effective emitter load decreases and the voltage gain may decrease.

13. Why is it suitable for sensor buffering?

Its high input impedance reduces loading on a high-impedance sensor.

14. Is the collector connected to AC ground?

In small-signal analysis, an ideal DC supply is AC ground, so the collector is the common terminal.

15. What is the approximate emitter small-signal resistance?

re ≈ VT/IE, or approximately 1/gm.

16. Does a coupling capacitor affect DC bias ideally?

No. An ideal coupling capacitor blocks steady-state DC.

17. Why is an emitter follower useful between amplifier stages?

It can reduce loading of the preceding stage while driving the next stage more effectively.

18. What limits the output swing?

Supply voltage, transistor cutoff and saturation limits, bias point and load current.
