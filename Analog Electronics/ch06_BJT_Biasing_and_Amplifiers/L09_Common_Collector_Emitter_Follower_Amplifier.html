Lesson 9: Multistage Amplifiers and Coupling Techniques

Understand cascaded amplifier stages, overall gain, stage loading, RC coupling, transformer coupling, direct coupling and tuned amplifiers.

1. Introduction to Multistage Amplifiers

A single transistor amplifier may not provide sufficient voltage gain, current gain or power gain for a practical application. To obtain the required amplification, two or more amplifier stages are connected in cascade. This arrangement is called a multistage amplifier.

The output of one amplifier stage is connected to the input of the next stage through a suitable coupling technique.

Why Are Multiple Stages Required?

  • To obtain higher overall voltage gain.
  • To increase the available signal level.
  • To provide suitable input and output impedance.
  • To drive a load that a single stage cannot drive effectively.
  • To combine voltage amplification with current or power amplification.

2. Cascaded Amplifier Configuration

In a cascaded amplifier, the output of the first stage drives the input of the second stage. Additional stages can be connected in the same manner.

3. Overall Gain of a Multistage Amplifier

The overall voltage gain is the product of the voltage gains of individual stages, provided the gains are evaluated under the actual loading conditions.

Overall voltage gain:

Av(total) = Av1 × Av2 × Av3 × ... × Avn

For two stages:

Av(total) = Av1 × Av2

Gain in Decibels

When gain is expressed in decibels, the individual stage gains are added instead of multiplied.

Av(dB) = 20 log10 |Av|

Av(total,dB) = Av1(dB) + Av2(dB) + ...

Numerical Example 1: Overall Voltage Gain

A two-stage amplifier has the following voltage gains:

  • First stage gain = 20
  • Second stage gain = 10

Solution:

Av(total) = 20 × 10 = 200

Overall gain in dB = 20 log10(200) ≈ 46.02 dB

Therefore, the overall voltage gain is 200, or approximately 46.02 dB.

Stage Loading Effect

The input impedance of the next stage loads the output of the previous stage. Consequently, the actual gain of a stage may be lower than its unloaded gain.

  • A low input impedance in the next stage can reduce the previous stage's output signal.
  • A buffer can reduce loading between stages.
  • Impedance matching may be important in particular applications.
Important: Always use the loaded voltage gain of each stage when calculating the overall gain of a practical multistage amplifier.

4. Coupling Techniques

Coupling is the method used to transfer an AC signal from one amplifier stage to another while providing the required DC operating conditions.

The major coupling techniques are:

  • RC coupling
  • Transformer coupling
  • Direct coupling
  • LC or tuned coupling

5. RC Coupled Amplifier

An RC coupled amplifier uses a capacitor to transfer the AC signal from one stage to the next. The coupling capacitor blocks DC while allowing the AC signal to pass.

Basic Arrangement

The diagram illustrates the signal path. A practical circuit also requires collector, base-bias and emitter components in each transistor stage.

Working Principle

  1. The first transistor amplifies the input AC signal.
  2. The coupling capacitor transfers the AC component to the next stage.
  3. The capacitor blocks the first stage's DC collector voltage from directly changing the next stage's base bias.
  4. The second transistor provides additional amplification.

Advantages

  • Simple circuit design.
  • Low cost and compact construction.
  • Good voltage gain over a useful mid-frequency range.
  • Widely used in audio voltage amplifiers.

Disadvantages

  • Gain decreases at very low frequencies due to coupling and bypass capacitors.
  • Gain decreases at high frequencies due to transistor and stray capacitances.
  • Impedance matching is not its strongest feature.
  • Not generally suitable for high-power output stages without an appropriate driver and output stage.

Applications

  • Audio preamplifiers.
  • Voltage amplification stages.
  • Microphone signal amplification.
  • General-purpose small-signal amplifiers.

6. Transformer Coupled Amplifier

A transformer coupled amplifier uses a transformer to transfer the AC signal between amplifier stages or from an amplifier to a load.

The transformer can provide impedance transformation, which helps transfer power efficiently when the source and load impedances require matching.

Transformer Impedance Ratio

V1/V2 = N1/N2

Z1/Z2 = (N1/N2)2

Here, N1 and N2 are the primary and secondary turns, while Z1 and Z2 are the corresponding impedances under ideal transformer conditions.

Advantages

  • Provides impedance transformation.
  • Can improve power transfer to a suitable load.
  • Provides galvanic isolation between windings.
  • Useful in certain audio output stages.

Disadvantages

  • Transformers can be bulky and expensive.
  • Frequency response is limited by transformer characteristics.
  • Core losses and winding resistance reduce efficiency.
  • Magnetic saturation can cause distortion.

Applications

  • Audio power amplifiers.
  • Impedance matching circuits.
  • Some RF and communication circuits.
  • Isolation and signal coupling applications.

7. Direct Coupled Amplifier

A direct coupled amplifier connects the output of one stage directly to the input of the next stage without a coupling capacitor or transformer.

Both AC and DC components can pass between stages. The DC operating point of one stage therefore affects the biasing conditions of the next stage.

Advantages

  • Can amplify signals down to DC and very low frequencies.
  • Does not require interstage coupling capacitors.
  • Useful in operational amplifier and differential amplifier circuits.
  • Suitable for many sensor and instrumentation applications.

Disadvantages

  • DC offset can be amplified along with the desired signal.
  • Temperature changes can cause operating-point drift.
  • Biasing and stability require careful design.

Applications

  • Operational amplifiers.
  • Instrumentation amplifiers.
  • Low-frequency signal conditioning.
  • Sensor interfaces and measurement systems.

8. LC or Tuned Coupled Amplifier

A tuned amplifier uses an LC resonant circuit to amplify signals over a selected frequency band. It is commonly used in radio frequency applications.

Resonant frequency:

fr = 1 / (2π√LC)

At resonance, the inductive and capacitive reactances are equal in magnitude. The tuned circuit provides frequency selectivity.

Applications

  • Radio-frequency amplifiers.
  • Radio receivers.
  • Communication transmitters and receivers.
  • Frequency-selective signal processing.

10. Coupling Capacitor and Cutoff Frequency

A coupling capacitor and the equivalent resistance seen by it form a high-pass network. The capacitor's reactance decreases as frequency increases.

Capacitive reactance:

XC = 1/(2πfC)

Approximate cutoff frequency:

fc = 1/(2πReqC)

Req is the equivalent resistance seen by the capacitor with independent sources set to zero for the small-signal analysis. Its value depends on the actual circuit and may involve the output resistance of the preceding stage and the input resistance of the following stage.

Numerical Example 2: Coupling Capacitor

Given:

  • Equivalent resistance = 10 kΩ
  • Coupling capacitance = 10 µF

Solution:

fc = 1/(2π × 10000 × 10 × 10-6)

fc ≈ 1.59 Hz

The approximate cutoff frequency associated with this coupling network is 1.59 Hz.

Design consideration: A multistage amplifier contains several coupling and bypass capacitors. Each can contribute to the low-frequency response. The overall lower cutoff frequency must be determined from the combined response, not assumed to equal every individual cutoff frequency.

11. Phase Relationship in Cascaded CE Amplifiers

A common-emitter amplifier produces approximately 180° phase inversion between its input and output signals in the mid-band region.

  • One CE stage: approximately 180° phase shift.
  • Two cascaded CE stages: approximately 360° total shift, so the output is in phase with the original input.
  • Three cascaded CE stages: approximately 540° total shift, equivalent to 180°.
For n identical inverting CE stages, the approximate phase shift is:

φ ≈ n × 180°

This is an idealized mid-band description. Actual phase shift varies with frequency because of coupling capacitors and transistor capacitances.

12. Practical Applications of Multistage Amplifiers

  • Audio systems: Preamplifier, driver and power amplifier stages.
  • Communication systems: Cascaded RF and intermediate-frequency amplification.
  • Instrumentation: Amplification of small sensor signals.
  • Embedded electronics: Signal conditioning before ADC input stages.
  • Industrial electronics: Amplification of low-level measurement signals.

13. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a multistage amplifier?

An amplifier formed by connecting two or more amplifier stages in cascade to obtain the required overall gain.

Q2. Why are amplifier stages connected in cascade?

To achieve higher gain and provide the required signal amplification, impedance characteristics and load-driving capability.

Q3. What is the overall voltage gain of two cascaded stages?

It is the product of the loaded voltage gains of the two stages: Av(total) = Av1 × Av2.

Q4. What is RC coupling?

A method of transferring an AC signal between amplifier stages using a coupling capacitor.

Q5. Why is a coupling capacitor used?

It transfers the AC signal while blocking DC, helping preserve the independent bias conditions of successive stages.

Q6. Which coupling technique is commonly used in audio voltage amplifiers?

RC coupling is commonly used because of its simple design, low cost and useful mid-band frequency response.

Q7. What is the main advantage of transformer coupling?

It provides impedance transformation and can improve power transfer to a suitable load.

Q8. Which coupling technique can amplify DC signals?

Direct coupling, because there is no interstage coupling capacitor or transformer blocking the DC component.

Q9. Where are tuned amplifiers used?

They are used in RF circuits where amplification is required over a selected frequency band.

Q10. What is stage loading?

Stage loading occurs when the input impedance of a following stage affects the output signal and gain of the preceding stage.

15. Lesson Summary

  • A multistage amplifier uses two or more cascaded amplifier stages.
  • Overall voltage gain is the product of the loaded stage gains.
  • Gain in decibels is additive across stages.
  • RC coupling is widely used for audio voltage amplification.
  • Transformer coupling provides impedance transformation.
  • Direct coupling is suitable for DC and very-low-frequency signals.
  • Tuned coupling provides frequency-selective amplification.
  • Stage loading can reduce the actual overall gain.
