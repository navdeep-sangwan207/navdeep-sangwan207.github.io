Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers

1. What is Voltage Divider Bias?

Voltage divider bias is a BJT biasing technique in which two resistors, R1 and R2, establish the base voltage of a transistor.

An emitter resistor RE is normally included to improve the thermal stability of the operating point.

A collector resistor RC is used to establish the collector voltage and determine the collector current together with the transistor's operating condition.

Base voltage is established by the R1-R2 voltage divider network.
Important: Voltage-divider bias with an emitter resistor is widely used because it provides good Q-point stability and reduces sensitivity to transistor beta variations.

2. Voltage Divider Bias Circuit

Consider an NPN transistor circuit containing:

  • R1: Upper voltage-divider resistor.
  • R2: Lower voltage-divider resistor.
  • RC: Collector resistor.
  • RE: Emitter resistor.
  • VCC: DC supply voltage.

Voltage Divider Biased NPN Transistor

Simplified circuit showing the voltage divider at the base and emitter stabilization resistor.

3. Working Principle

  1. R1 and R2 form a voltage divider across VCC.
  2. The divider establishes a base voltage VB.
  3. The base-emitter junction becomes forward biased.
  4. Emitter current flows through RE.
  5. Collector current is approximately equal to emitter current when beta is sufficiently large.
  6. RC develops a voltage drop and establishes the collector voltage.

The emitter voltage is approximately:

VE = VB − VBE

The emitter current is:

IE = VE / RE

For a transistor operating in the forward-active region with sufficiently large beta:

IC ≈ IE

4. Voltage Divider Equations

If the base current is small compared with the current through the divider network, the base voltage can be approximated using the ordinary voltage-divider equation.

VB ≈ VCC × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Then:

VE ≈ VB − VBE

IE ≈ VE / RE

IC ≈ IE

VC = VCC − IC × RC

VCE = VC − VE
These simplified equations assume that the divider current is sufficiently greater than base current. If base loading is significant, use the Thevenin equivalent method.

5. Thevenin Equivalent Method

Thevenin's theorem simplifies the voltage-divider network into one equivalent voltage source and one equivalent resistance as viewed from the base.

Step 1: Calculate Thevenin Voltage

VTH = VCC × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Step 2: Calculate Thevenin Resistance

RTH = R1 × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Step 3: Apply KVL to the Base-Emitter Loop

VTH = IB × RTH + VBE + IE × RE

IE = (β + 1)IB

Therefore:

IB = (VTH − VBE) / [RTH + (β + 1)RE]

Step 4: Calculate Collector Current

IC = β × IB

Step 5: Calculate Emitter and Collector Voltages

IE = (β + 1)IB

VE = IE × RE

VC = VCC − IC × RC

VCE = VC − VE
Placement tip: Remember the Thevenin equivalents: VTH = VCC × R2/(R1+R2) and RTH = R1 || R2.

6. Numerical Example Using Thevenin's Theorem

Consider the following circuit parameters:

  • VCC = 12 V
  • R1 = 47 kΩ
  • R2 = 10 kΩ
  • RC = 2.2 kΩ
  • RE = 1 kΩ
  • β = 100
  • VBE = 0.7 V

Step 1: Calculate VTH

VTH = 12 × 10 / (47 + 10)

VTH = 120 / 57

VTH ≈ 2.105 V

Step 2: Calculate RTH

RTH = (47 × 10) / (47 + 10) kΩ

RTH = 470 / 57 kΩ

RTH ≈ 8.246 kΩ

Step 3: Calculate Base Current

IB = (VTH − VBE) / [RTH + (β + 1)RE]

IB = (2.105 − 0.7) / [8.246 kΩ + 101 × 1 kΩ]

IB = 1.405 / 109.246 kΩ

IB ≈ 12.86 µA

Step 4: Calculate Collector Current

IC = β × IB

IC = 100 × 12.86 µA

IC ≈ 1.286 mA

Step 5: Calculate Emitter Current

IE = (β + 1) × IB

IE = 101 × 12.86 µA

IE ≈ 1.299 mA

Step 6: Calculate Emitter Voltage

VE = IE × RE

VE = 1.299 mA × 1 kΩ

VE ≈ 1.299 V

Step 7: Calculate Collector Voltage

VC = VCC − IC × RC

VC = 12 − (1.286 mA × 2.2 kΩ)

VC ≈ 9.171 V

Step 8: Calculate VCE

VCE = VC − VE

VCE = 9.171 − 1.299

VCE ≈ 7.872 V
Final Calculated Results:
VTH ≈ 2.105 V
RTH ≈ 8.246 kΩ
IB ≈ 12.86 µA
ICQ ≈ 1.286 mA
IE ≈ 1.299 mA
VE ≈ 1.299 V
VC ≈ 9.171 V
VCEQ ≈ 7.872 V

The calculated collector voltage is greater than the base voltage and the base voltage is greater than the emitter voltage, consistent with forward-active NPN operation.

7. How Emitter Resistance Improves Stability

The emitter resistor RE provides negative feedback that helps stabilize collector current.

Suppose collector current increases due to a temperature rise or transistor parameter variation.

  1. Emitter current increases.
  2. The voltage drop across RE increases.
  3. Emitter voltage VE rises.
  4. The base-emitter voltage VBE tends to decrease if VB remains approximately constant.
  5. Base current decreases.
  6. The original increase in collector current is opposed.
VE = IE × RE

VBE = VB − VE
This is negative feedback. It helps reduce the sensitivity of the operating point to temperature and transistor beta variations.

8. Conditions for Good Voltage Divider Bias

A common design guideline is to make the current through R2 several times larger than the base current.

Divider current through R2 ≈ 5 to 10 × IB

This is a practical rule of thumb, not a mandatory universal design value. The actual divider current depends on the required beta sensitivity, power consumption and resistor values.

A useful design approach is to select a divider sufficiently stiff that base loading has only a limited effect on the base voltage.

9. Advantages and Disadvantages

Advantages

  • Good operating-point stability.
  • Less sensitive to transistor beta variation.
  • Emitter resistor provides negative feedback.
  • Widely used in common-emitter amplifier circuits.
  • Suitable for practical discrete BJT amplifier designs.

Disadvantages

  • Uses more components than fixed bias.
  • Divider network consumes continuous current.
  • Emitter resistance can reduce AC voltage gain unless bypassed appropriately.
  • Accurate analysis may require Thevenin's theorem.

10. Practical Applications

  • Common-emitter voltage amplifiers.
  • Audio preamplifier circuits.
  • Sensor signal-conditioning stages.
  • Analog signal amplification.
  • Discrete transistor amplifier circuits.

11. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is voltage divider bias?

It is a BJT biasing technique in which two resistors establish the base voltage and an emitter resistor is commonly used for stabilization.

Q2. Why is voltage divider bias widely used?

It provides good Q-point stability and reduces sensitivity to transistor beta variations.

Q3. What is the purpose of the emitter resistor?

It provides negative feedback and improves thermal stability.

Q4. What is Thevenin voltage?

It is the open-circuit voltage of the equivalent voltage source seen from the base terminals.

Q5. What is Thevenin resistance?

It is the equivalent resistance seen from the base terminals when independent voltage sources are replaced by ideal short circuits.

Q6. Why should divider current be greater than base current?

A sufficiently large divider current reduces base loading and helps maintain a stable base voltage.

Q7. What is the difference between IC and IE?

IE = IC + IB. For sufficiently large beta, IC is approximately equal to IE.

An NPN transistor circuit has VCC = 15 V, R1 = 68 kΩ, R2 = 12 kΩ, RC = 3.3 kΩ, RE = 1.5 kΩ, β = 100 and VBE = 0.7 V.

Using the Thevenin method, calculate:

  1. VTH and RTH.
  2. Base current IB.
  3. Collector current IC.
  4. Emitter voltage VE.
  5. Collector-emitter voltage VCE.
Show Answer

VTH = 15 × 12 / (68 + 12) = 2.25 V

RTH = (68 × 12) / 80 = 10.2 kΩ

13. Lesson Summary

  • Voltage divider bias uses R1 and R2 to establish base voltage.
  • Emitter resistance RE provides negative feedback.
  • Thevenin's theorem simplifies base-network analysis.
  • Collector current is approximately equal to emitter current for sufficiently large beta.
  • Voltage divider bias provides good Q-point stability.
  • It is widely used in practical BJT amplifier circuits.
Remember: Voltage divider bias with emitter resistance is one of the most useful and widely applied BJT biasing arrangements.
