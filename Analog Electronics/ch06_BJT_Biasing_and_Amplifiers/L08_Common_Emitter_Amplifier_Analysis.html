Understand amplifier gain, decibel calculations, cutoff frequencies, bandwidth and frequency response.

Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers

1. Introduction to Amplifier Gain

An amplifier increases the amplitude of an input signal using energy supplied by its DC power source. The amount of amplification is measured using gain.

Gain is the ratio of output signal magnitude to input signal magnitude.

Depending on the quantity being measured, amplifier gain can be classified into:

  • Voltage gain.
  • Current gain.
  • Power gain.
Important: An amplifier does not create energy. It uses DC supply energy to produce an amplified output signal.

2. Voltage Gain

Voltage gain is the ratio of output voltage to input voltage.

Av = Vo / Vi

Where:

  • Av = Voltage gain.
  • Vo = Output AC voltage.
  • Vi = Input AC voltage.

For example, if an amplifier receives an input voltage of 20 mV and produces an output voltage of 2 V:

Av = 2 / 0.020

Av = 100

The voltage gain is 100.

2.1 Voltage Gain with Phase Inversion

In a common-emitter amplifier, the output voltage is approximately 180° out of phase with the input.

Av = −Vo / Vi

The negative sign indicates phase inversion. It does not mean that the amplifier has negative power gain.

3. Current Gain

Current gain is the ratio of output AC current to input AC current.

Ai = Io / Ii

Current gain depends on the amplifier configuration and the loading conditions.

  • CE: Generally provides substantial current gain.
  • CC: Provides high current gain.
  • CB: Current gain is approximately α, less than 1.

4. Power Gain

Power gain is the ratio of output AC power delivered to the load to input AC power.

Ap = Po / Pi

For resistive input and output loads:

Ap = (Vo × Io) / (Vi × Ii)

Ap = Av × Ai

This product relationship assumes voltage and current gains are defined using compatible input and output quantities.

5. Gain in Decibels (dB)

Decibel is a logarithmic unit used to express the ratio of two power levels or signal amplitudes.

5.1 Voltage Gain in Decibels

Av(dB) = 20 log10 |Vo / Vi|

5.2 Current Gain in Decibels

Ai(dB) = 20 log10 |Io / Ii|

5.3 Power Gain in Decibels

Ap(dB) = 10 log10 (Po / Pi)
Remember: The factor 20 is used for voltage and current ratios when the corresponding impedances are equal or when the ratio is being expressed as an amplitude gain convention. The factor 10 is used for power ratios.

5.4 Common Decibel Values

Amplitude Ratio Gain in dB
1 0 dB
2 6.02 dB
10 20 dB
100 40 dB
0.5 −6.02 dB
0.1 −20 dB

6. Numerical Example: Voltage Gain in dB

An amplifier has an input voltage of 50 mV and an output voltage of 5 V. Calculate voltage gain and gain in decibels.

Step 1: Calculate Voltage Gain

Av = Vo / Vi

Av = 5 / 0.05

Av = 100

Step 2: Convert to Decibels

Av(dB) = 20 log10(100)

Av(dB) = 20 × 2

Av(dB) = 40 dB
Answer: Voltage gain = 100 and gain = 40 dB.

7. Introduction to Frequency Response

Frequency response describes how the gain of an amplifier changes with the frequency of the input signal.

An amplifier does not provide the same gain at every frequency.

Its response is generally divided into three regions:

  1. Low-frequency region.
  2. Mid-frequency region.
  3. High-frequency region.

Typical BJT Amplifier Frequency Response

Note: The curve is conceptual and not drawn to a specific frequency scale. The cutoff frequencies are measured relative to the midband gain.

8. Low-Frequency Response

At low frequencies, amplifier gain decreases mainly because of the reactance of:

  • Input coupling capacitor.
  • Output coupling capacitor.
  • Emitter bypass capacitor.

Capacitive reactance increases when frequency decreases.

XC = 1 / (2πfC)

Therefore, at low frequencies, coupling and bypass capacitors may no longer behave as effective AC short circuits.

9. Mid-Frequency Response

In the mid-frequency region:

  • Coupling capacitors have sufficiently low reactance.
  • The emitter bypass capacitor provides effective AC bypassing when fitted and correctly sized.
  • Internal transistor capacitances have relatively little effect.
  • Amplifier gain is approximately constant.
Important: The midband region is generally the region in which an amplifier delivers its designed voltage gain.

10. High-Frequency Response

At high frequencies, amplifier gain decreases mainly due to internal transistor capacitances and circuit parasitic capacitances.

Important transistor capacitances include:

  • Base-emitter capacitance (Cπ).
  • Base-collector capacitance (Cμ).

These capacitances create frequency-dependent impedances and reduce the amplifier's high-frequency response.

10.1 Miller Effect

In a common-emitter amplifier, the base-collector capacitance can appear larger at the input because of the voltage gain between the base and collector. This is known as the Miller effect.

CMiller ≈ Cμ(1 − Av)

Here Av is the signed voltage gain from base to collector. For an inverting CE stage, Av is negative, so the effective input capacitance can be substantially greater than Cμ.

Practical importance: The Miller effect can reduce the high-frequency bandwidth of a common-emitter amplifier. Cascode circuits are one technique used to reduce this effect.

11. Cutoff Frequencies

The lower cutoff frequency and upper cutoff frequency define the useful operating frequency range of an amplifier.

11.1 Lower Cutoff Frequency (fL)

The lower cutoff frequency is the frequency at which the voltage gain falls to approximately 70.7% of its midband value as frequency decreases.

11.2 Upper Cutoff Frequency (fH)

The upper cutoff frequency is the frequency at which the voltage gain falls to approximately 70.7% of its midband value as frequency increases.

Gain at cutoff = 0.707 × Midband voltage gain

This corresponds to a power reduction of approximately one-half, or a gain reduction of 3 dB.

12. Bandwidth of Amplifier

Bandwidth is the difference between the upper and lower cutoff frequencies.

BW = fH − fL

For example, if:

  • fL = 100 Hz
  • fH = 100 kHz
BW = 100000 − 100

BW = 99900 Hz

BW = 99.9 kHz
Answer: The amplifier bandwidth is 99.9 kHz.

13. Numerical Example: Cutoff Gain

A CE amplifier has a midband voltage gain magnitude of 100. Calculate the voltage-gain magnitude at either cutoff frequency.

Gain at cutoff = 0.707 × 100

= 70.7

The corresponding gain in decibels is:

20 log10(70.7) ≈ 36.99 dB

The midband gain is 40 dB, so the cutoff gain is approximately 3 dB lower.

14. Factors Affecting Amplifier Frequency Response

Factor Effect
Input coupling capacitor Affects low-frequency response
Output coupling capacitor Affects low-frequency response
Emitter bypass capacitor Affects low-frequency gain and response
Base-emitter capacitance Contributes to high-frequency limitations
Base-collector capacitance Contributes to Miller effect
Wiring and PCB parasitics Can reduce high-frequency performance
Load resistance Changes gain and may affect cutoff frequencies

15. Practical Methods to Improve Frequency Response

  • Select coupling capacitors suitable for the required low-frequency range.
  • Choose an appropriate emitter bypass capacitor.
  • Use transistors with suitable high-frequency characteristics.
  • Minimize unnecessary wiring and PCB parasitic capacitance.
  • Use proper grounding and layout techniques.
  • Consider cascode configurations where Miller effect is significant.
  • Check the load impedance and source impedance.

16. Practical Applications

  • Audio amplifier design.
  • Sensor signal conditioning.
  • Communication receiver circuits.
  • RF amplifier stages.
  • Industrial measurement equipment.
  • Analog signal processing systems.

17. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is voltage gain?

It is the ratio of output AC voltage to input AC voltage.

Q2. What is power gain?

It is the ratio of output AC power to input AC power.

Q3. What is frequency response?

It describes how amplifier gain changes with input signal frequency.

Q4. What are cutoff frequencies?

They are the lower and upper frequencies at which gain falls to 70.7% of its midband magnitude.

Q5. What is bandwidth?

It is the difference between upper and lower cutoff frequencies.

Q6. Why does gain decrease at low frequencies?

Because coupling and emitter bypass capacitors have higher reactance at lower frequencies.

Q7. Why does gain decrease at high frequencies?

Because transistor internal capacitances and circuit parasitic capacitances affect the signal response.

Q8. What is the Miller effect?

It is the apparent increase in input capacitance caused by a capacitance connected between input and output terminals of an amplifying stage.

Q9. What does −3 dB mean?

It represents approximately half the power, or 70.7% of the voltage amplitude when the impedance conditions are unchanged.

19. Lesson Summary

  • Amplifier gain measures the ratio of output to input signal.
  • Voltage gain = Vo / Vi.
  • Current gain = Io / Ii.
  • Power gain = Po / Pi.
  • Voltage and current amplitude gains can be expressed using 20 log10.
  • Power gain is expressed using 10 log10.
  • Frequency response describes gain variation with frequency.
  • Low-frequency response is affected by coupling and bypass capacitors.
  • High-frequency response is affected by internal and parasitic capacitances.
  • The cutoff gain is 0.707 times the midband gain magnitude.
  • Bandwidth = fH − fL.
  • The Miller effect can reduce CE amplifier bandwidth.
Remember: The frequency response determines the range of input signal frequencies an amplifier can amplify effectively.
