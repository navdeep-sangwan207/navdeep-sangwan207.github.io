2. Common-Emitter Amplifier: Introduction

In a common-emitter (CE) amplifier, the emitter terminal is common to both the input and output circuits for AC analysis. The input signal is applied between base and emitter, while the output is measured between collector and emitter.

  • Input: base-emitter circuit.
  • Output: collector-emitter circuit.
  • Common terminal: emitter, usually at AC ground.
  • Typical output: amplified and approximately 180° phase-inverted.
The CE configuration is widely used because it provides useful voltage gain and current gain. Its performance depends on the bias network, collector load, emitter degeneration, transistor parameters and frequency.

4. Working Principle of a CE Amplifier

  1. The voltage-divider network establishes a suitable DC base voltage.
  2. The transistor operates in the forward-active region at its Q-point.
  3. A small positive input variation increases base-emitter voltage.
  4. Base current and collector current increase.
  5. The voltage drop across RC increases.
  6. Collector voltage decreases, producing an inverted output variation.
ΔvC ≈ −ΔiC × (RC || RL)

Thus, an increase in input voltage produces a decrease in output voltage. This is the reason for the approximate 180° phase inversion.

5. DC Analysis of a Voltage-Divider Biased CE Amplifier

For DC analysis, coupling capacitors and bypass capacitors are treated as open circuits after sufficient settling time. The base divider is replaced by its Thevenin equivalent.

VTH = VCC × R2/(R1 + R2)
RTH = R1 || R2

For a transistor with an emitter resistor RE:

IB = (VTH − VBE)/[RTH + (β + 1)RE]
IC ≈ βIB
IE ≈ (β + 1)IB
VE = IERE
VC = VCC − ICRC
VCE = VC − VE
The active-region equations must be checked against the actual collector voltage and saturation limit. If the calculated VCE is too low, the transistor is not operating in the assumed forward-active region.

6. Worked DC Bias Example

Given: VCC = 12 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, β = 100 and VBE = 0.7 V.

Step 1: Thevenin voltage.

VTH = 12 × 10/(47 + 10) = 2.105 V

Step 2: Thevenin resistance.

RTH = 47 kΩ || 10 kΩ = 8.246 kΩ

Step 3: Base current.

IB = (2.105 − 0.7)/[8.246k + 101 × 1k]
IB ≈ 12.87 µA

Step 4: Collector and emitter currents.

IC ≈ 100 × 12.87 µA = 1.287 mA
IE ≈ 1.300 mA

Step 5: Collector and emitter voltages.

VC = 12 − (1.287 mA × 2.2 kΩ) = 9.169 V
VE = 1.300 mA × 1 kΩ = 1.300 V
VCE = 9.169 − 1.300 = 7.869 V
The estimated Q-point is ICQ ≈ 1.287 mA and VCEQ ≈ 7.87 V. This is consistent with forward-active operation in the simplified model.

7. AC Equivalent Circuit

For midband AC analysis:

  • Ideal DC voltage sources are set to AC ground.
  • Coupling capacitors are approximated as short circuits if their reactance is sufficiently small.
  • A sufficiently large emitter bypass capacitor is approximated as an AC short.
  • The transistor is replaced by its small-signal equivalent model.

With the emitter AC-grounded and neglecting transistor output resistance:

RAC = RC || RL
Av ≈ −gm(RC || RL)

Without an external load or with a very large load:

Av ≈ −gmRC

8. Voltage Gain with Emitter Degeneration

If the external emitter resistor RE is not bypassed, it introduces negative feedback. This reduces voltage gain but improves linearity and stabilizes the operating behaviour.

A useful approximate T-model expression, assuming the base is driven by an ideal low-impedance voltage source and neglecting ro, is:

Av ≈ −(RC || RL)/(re + RE)

More generally, the base-side source resistance and transistor input resistance affect the voltage reaching the base. A complete gain calculation must include these effects.

When a bypass capacitor CE is used, the emitter resistor is partially or largely bypassed for AC signals above its effective corner frequency. The gain then approaches the bypassed-emitter result over the midband region.

9. Input Impedance

For an AC-grounded emitter, the transistor's approximate base input resistance is rπ. The complete amplifier input resistance also includes the bias divider:

Rin ≈ R1 || R2 || rπ

where:

rπ = β/gm

With an unbypassed emitter resistor, the base input resistance increases approximately to:

Rin(base) ≈ rπ + (β + 1)RE

This approximation neglects ro and other parasitic effects. The overall input resistance also includes the bias divider in parallel.

10. Output Impedance

Looking into the collector, a simplified midband estimate is:

Rout ≈ RC || ro

If transistor output resistance ro is much larger than RC:

Rout ≈ RC

When a load RL is connected through an output coupling capacitor, the loaded collector resistance becomes approximately RC || RL for midband analysis.

11. Coupling Capacitors and Emitter Bypass Capacitor

Input coupling capacitor Cin

Blocks DC from the signal source while allowing the AC input signal to reach the transistor base. Its reactance is:

XC = 1/(2πfC)

Output coupling capacitor Cout

Prevents the collector's DC voltage from appearing across the load while passing the amplified AC signal.

Emitter bypass capacitor CE

Provides a low-impedance AC path around RE. It increases midband gain by reducing AC negative feedback while leaving the DC bias current substantially unchanged in the ideal steady-state model.

Capacitors are not perfect short circuits at every frequency. Their reactance, ESR, leakage and the surrounding resistance affect the practical frequency response.

14. Frequency Response of a CE Amplifier

The voltage gain of a practical CE amplifier is not constant over all frequencies.

Frequency rangeDominant effectsGain behaviour
Low frequencyInput/output coupling capacitors and emitter bypass capacitorGain rises as frequency increases from the low-frequency region.
MidbandCapacitors have relatively low reactance; transistor parasitics are less dominantGain is approximately constant.
High frequencyBase-emitter and base-collector capacitances, wiring capacitance and Miller effectGain decreases as frequency increases.

The lower and upper cutoff frequencies are conventionally defined at the points where the gain magnitude falls to approximately 0.707 of its midband value (−3 dB).

Bandwidth = fH − fL

15. Worked AC Gain Example

Given: ICQ = 1.3 mA, RC = 2.2 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ and VT = 26 mV. Assume the emitter is AC-grounded and neglect ro.

Step 1: Transconductance.

gm = 1.3 mA/26 mV = 50 mS

Step 2: Effective collector load.

RC || RL = (2.2 × 10)/(2.2 + 10) = 1.803 kΩ

Step 3: Voltage gain.

Av ≈ −0.05 × 1803 = −90.15

For a 2 mV peak input:

Vout(peak) ≈ 90.15 × 2 mV = 180.3 mV
The idealized output is approximately 180 mV peak and is inverted. Check the actual Q-point and output headroom before assuming this amplitude is achievable without clipping.

16. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Construct a voltage-divider biased common-emitter amplifier and measure its DC operating point, voltage gain and phase relationship.

Equipment

  • NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222; verify its pinout.
  • Current-limited low-voltage DC supply.
  • Resistors R1, R2, RC and RE selected for the design.
  • Input/output coupling capacitors and optional emitter bypass capacitor.
  • Function generator, oscilloscope and digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Assemble the circuit with power switched off.
  2. Check transistor terminals and resistor connections.
  3. Apply the DC supply and measure VB, VE and VC.
  4. Calculate VCE and verify that the transistor is in the active region.
  5. Apply a small sinusoidal input at a suitable midband frequency.
  6. Measure Vin and Vout using the oscilloscope.
  7. Calculate Av = Vout/Vin using the same amplitude convention.
  8. Observe and record the phase inversion.
  9. Compare gain with and without the emitter bypass capacitor.
  10. Vary the frequency and observe the low-frequency response.

18. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a common-emitter amplifier?

A BJT amplifier in which the emitter is common to the input and output circuits for AC analysis.

2. Why is CE widely used?

It provides useful voltage and current gain in a relatively simple circuit.

3. Why does a CE amplifier invert phase?

An increase in base drive increases collector current, increasing the RC voltage drop and reducing collector voltage.

4. What is the purpose of R1 and R2?

They establish the base DC bias voltage.

5. What is the purpose of RE?

It provides negative feedback for improved DC and thermal stability.

6. Why is an emitter bypass capacitor used?

It reduces AC emitter degeneration and increases midband voltage gain.

7. What is the function of Cin?

It couples the input AC signal while blocking DC.

8. What is the function of Cout?

It couples the output AC signal while blocking the collector DC voltage.

9. How is the DC operating point calculated?

Use the DC equivalent circuit and bias-network equations to find ICQ and VCEQ.

10. What is the approximate midband gain of a bypassed CE stage?

Av ≈ −gm(RC || RL), neglecting ro and other non-ideal effects.

11. What is the approximate input resistance?

R1 || R2 || rπ for an AC-grounded emitter, in the simplified model.

12. What determines output resistance?

It is approximately RC || ro looking into the collector, neglecting other effects.

13. What happens when RL decreases?

The effective collector load decreases, reducing the magnitude of voltage gain.

14. What happens when ICQ increases?

Transconductance increases, since gm = ICQ/VT, although the Q-point and power dissipation also change.

15. Why is voltage-divider bias preferred?

It is generally less sensitive to β variation than simple fixed bias, particularly when the divider is sufficiently stiff and RE provides feedback.

16. What is the midband frequency region?

The range where coupling and bypass capacitors have relatively low reactance and transistor parasitic capacitances have not yet significantly reduced gain.

17. What is the Miller effect?

The apparent multiplication of a feedback capacitance between the input and output of an inverting amplifier.

18. How can CE amplifier gain be increased?

Possible methods include increasing the effective collector load, increasing gm through bias current, or bypassing emitter degeneration. Each method has limits involving headroom, bandwidth, power and distortion.
