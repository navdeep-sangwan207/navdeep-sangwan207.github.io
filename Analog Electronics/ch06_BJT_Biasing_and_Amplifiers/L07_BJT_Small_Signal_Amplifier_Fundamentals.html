  • Understand how a BJT amplifies small AC signals.
  • Distinguish DC bias quantities from AC signal quantities.
  • Explain the purpose of small-signal analysis.

2. What Is a Small-Signal Amplifier?

A transistor amplifier uses a DC bias condition to establish a stable operating point. A small AC input signal is then superimposed on this DC operating condition.

The transistor's nonlinear current-voltage relationship can be approximated by a linear model for sufficiently small changes around the Q-point. This allows us to calculate incremental voltage and current gain.

A transistor does not create energy. It controls energy drawn from the DC supply so that a small input signal can control a larger output signal.

3. DC and AC Components

Consider the collector current of a transistor operating around a DC bias current ICQ. When an AC input is applied:

iC(t) = ICQ + ic(t)

Similarly, the base current can be written as:

iB(t) = IBQ + ib(t)

Here:

  • ICQ and IBQ are quiescent DC currents.
  • ic(t) and ib(t) are small instantaneous AC variations.
  • The total instantaneous current is the sum of the DC and AC components.

For voltage:

vBE(t) = VBEQ + vbe(t)
vCE(t) = VCEQ + vce(t)

Capital letters with Q commonly indicate DC quiescent values; lowercase quantities commonly represent small-signal variations.

4. Why Small-Signal Analysis Is Required

The BJT is nonlinear. Its collector current approximately follows an exponential relationship with base-emitter voltage in forward-active operation:

IC ≈ IS eVBE/VT

Directly solving the nonlinear relationship for every small input variation is inconvenient. Around a selected Q-point, the curve can be approximated by its tangent. This produces a linear incremental model.

Small-signal analysis is valid when the signal is small enough that the local linear approximation remains accurate and the transistor stays in its intended operating region.

5. Thermal Voltage and Transconductance

At room temperature, the thermal voltage VT is approximately 25.8 mV (often rounded to 26 mV).

VT = kT/q

For a BJT in forward-active operation, the small-signal transconductance is:

gm = ICQ/VT

where ICQ is the DC collector current and VT is the thermal voltage.

Transconductance describes how much the collector current changes for a small change in base-emitter voltage:

ic ≈ gm × vbe

Unit of gm: Siemens (A/V).

Worked example

Given ICQ = 1 mA and VT = 26 mV:

gm = 1 mA / 26 mV = 0.03846 S = 38.46 mS

For a small vbe = 2 mV:

ic ≈ 38.46 mS × 2 mV = 76.9 µA

6. Dynamic Emitter Resistance

The intrinsic small-signal emitter resistance is approximately the reciprocal of transconductance:

re ≈ VT/IEQ

At room temperature, a convenient approximation is:

re ≈ 26 mV / IEQ

IEQ must be expressed in amperes for the result to be in ohms.

Worked example

If IEQ = 1.02 mA:

re ≈ 26 mV / 1.02 mA ≈ 25.5 Ω
Some textbooks use the approximation re = 25 mV/IE. Use the thermal voltage specified by the question or operating temperature. Do not confuse intrinsic re with an external emitter resistor RE.

7. Small-Signal Base Resistance rπ

In the hybrid-π model, the base-emitter input is represented by a small-signal resistance rπ.

rπ = β/gm

It can also be expressed approximately as:

rπ ≈ β × re × (IEQ/ICQ)

Because IEQ is close to ICQ for a high-β transistor, a common approximation is rπ ≈ βre.

Worked example

For β = 100 and ICQ = 1 mA, using VT = 26 mV:

gm = 1 mA/26 mV = 38.46 mS
rπ = 100/0.03846 ≈ 2.6 kΩ

9. AC Equivalent Circuit

To analyse the AC behaviour of a common-emitter amplifier:

  1. Set independent DC voltage sources to AC ground (ideal voltage sources become short circuits in the small-signal AC model).
  2. Coupling capacitors are treated as short circuits only when their reactance is sufficiently small at the signal frequency.
  3. A sufficiently large emitter bypass capacitor is treated as an AC short over the intended operating frequency range.
  4. Replace the transistor with its small-signal model.
  5. Calculate the input resistance, output resistance and voltage gain.
The DC analysis determines the Q-point first. Small-signal AC analysis is then performed around that operating point. The AC model does not replace the DC bias analysis.

10. Common-Emitter Small-Signal Voltage Gain

For a common-emitter amplifier with an AC-grounded emitter, neglecting ro and assuming the load is coupled to the collector, the approximate voltage gain is:

Av ≈ −gm(RC || RL)

The negative sign indicates approximately 180° phase inversion between the input and output voltage.

If no external load is connected, or the load is sufficiently large:

Av ≈ −gmRC

Using the T-model approximation, with an unbypassed external emitter resistance RE and neglecting base loading, the gain is reduced. A simplified estimate is:

Av ≈ −(RC || RL)/(re + RE)

This expression is an approximation. A complete calculation must account for source resistance, base loading, transistor output resistance, emitter bypassing and the exact circuit topology.

13. Small-Signal Model: Worked Numerical Problem

Given: ICQ = 2 mA, β = 120, VT = 26 mV, RC = 3.3 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ.

Step 1: Calculate transconductance.

gm = 2 mA/26 mV = 76.92 mS

Step 2: Calculate hybrid-π resistance.

rπ = β/gm = 120/0.07692 ≈ 1.56 kΩ

Step 3: Calculate the effective collector load.

RC || RL = (3.3 × 10)/(3.3 + 10) = 2.48 kΩ

Step 4: Estimate voltage gain for an AC-grounded emitter.

Av ≈ −gm(RC || RL)
Av ≈ −0.07692 × 2481 ≈ −190.8
The idealized midband gain estimate is approximately −191 V/V. A real circuit will generally have a lower gain due to source resistance, emitter degeneration, transistor output resistance, loading and non-ideal coupling/bypass capacitors.

14. Frequency Response and Coupling Capacitors

Small-signal gain depends on frequency. Coupling and bypass capacitors influence the low-frequency response, while transistor capacitances and wiring parasitics influence the high-frequency response.

Frequency regionImportant effects
Low frequencyInput/output coupling capacitors and emitter bypass capacitor have significant reactance.
MidbandCoupling and bypass capacitors are approximately AC shorts; gain is relatively constant.
High frequencyJunction capacitances, Miller effect and parasitic capacitances reduce gain.

The Miller effect can make the effective input capacitance of an inverting amplifier significantly larger than the physical base-collector capacitance.

15. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Measure the small-signal voltage gain of a common-emitter amplifier and compare it with the theoretical estimate.

Equipment and components

  • NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222 (verify its pinout and ratings).
  • Current-limited DC supply appropriate for the circuit.
  • Bias resistors, RC, RE, coupling capacitors and emitter bypass capacitor as per the selected design.
  • Function generator, oscilloscope and digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Build the amplifier and verify its DC bias conditions.
  2. Measure VC, VE and VCE to verify the Q-point.
  3. Apply a small sinusoidal input at a midband frequency.
  4. Measure the input and output peak-to-peak voltages.
  5. Calculate measured voltage gain Av = Vout/Vin.
  6. Observe the approximate 180° phase inversion.
  7. Increase the input amplitude gradually and observe when distortion begins.
  8. Compare the measured gain with the small-signal model prediction.

17. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is small-signal analysis?

It is a linear approximation of transistor behaviour for small variations around a DC operating point.

2. Why is a DC bias point required?

It establishes the operating region and allows the transistor to amplify an AC signal without immediately entering cutoff or saturation.

3. What is transconductance?

It is the incremental change in collector current divided by the incremental base-emitter voltage; gm = ICQ/VT.

4. What is thermal voltage?

VT = kT/q. It is approximately 26 mV at room temperature.

5. What is dynamic emitter resistance?

The intrinsic small-signal emitter resistance, approximately re = VT/IEQ.

6. What is rπ?

The small-signal base-emitter resistance in the hybrid-π model; rπ = β/gm.

7. What is the hybrid-π model?

A small-signal equivalent model representing the BJT using rπ, a controlled current source and, when needed, output resistance ro.

8. What does gm represent physically?

How strongly a small change in base-emitter voltage controls collector current.

9. Why does a common-emitter amplifier invert phase?

An increase in base drive increases collector current, increasing the voltage drop across RC and reducing collector voltage.

10. What is the approximate CE voltage gain?

For an AC-grounded emitter and neglecting ro, Av ≈ −gm(RC || RL).

11. What happens if ICQ increases?

gm increases because gm = ICQ/VT, while intrinsic re decreases approximately as current increases.

12. What is the role of an emitter bypass capacitor?

It provides a low-impedance AC path around the external emitter resistor over its intended frequency range, increasing AC gain.

13. Why are coupling capacitors used?

They pass AC signals while blocking DC from disturbing adjacent bias networks.

14. What is the Miller effect?

It is the apparent multiplication of a capacitance connected between input and output nodes of an amplifier with voltage gain.

15. What is the difference between re and RE?

re is intrinsic small-signal emitter resistance; RE is an external resistor connected in the emitter circuit.

16. When is small-signal analysis invalid?

When the signal is too large for local linearization, or the transistor leaves its intended operating region.

17. Why does a transistor amplifier have a limited bandwidth?

Low-frequency coupling/bypass capacitor effects and high-frequency device/parasitic capacitances limit its response.

18. Why does the DC supply matter if the input signal is small?

The DC supply provides the energy delivered to the load; the input controls the variation.
