Common-Base Amplifier and Configuration Comparison

Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers | Lecture 10

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the common-base transistor configuration.
  • Explain the operation of a CB amplifier using an NPN transistor.
  • Analyse its DC bias and small-signal equivalent circuit.
  • Calculate current gain, voltage gain and input/output impedance.
  • Understand its frequency response and high-frequency behaviour.
  • Compare CB, CE and CC amplifier configurations.
  • Identify practical applications and troubleshoot CB circuits.

2. Introduction to Common-Base Configuration

In a common-base (CB) amplifier, the base terminal is common to the input and output circuits for AC signals.

  • Input is applied between emitter and base.
  • Output is taken between collector and base.
  • The base is maintained at a fixed DC bias and is usually AC-grounded through a suitable bypass capacitor.
Important characteristics: Low input impedance, high output impedance, current gain slightly below unity, and potentially high voltage gain.

4. Working Principle

For an NPN common-base amplifier operating in the forward-active region:

  • The emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased.
  • Electrons injected from the emitter cross the thin base region.
  • Most injected carriers are collected by the collector.
  • A small fraction recombines in the base, producing base current.
IE = IC + IB
α = IC/IE

For normal forward-active operation, α is slightly below unity. A small change in emitter current produces a nearly equal change in collector current, which explains the high current-transfer efficiency.

5. DC Biasing of a Common-Base Amplifier

A practical CB amplifier uses a bias network to establish the base voltage and an emitter resistor to set emitter current.

For a simplified NPN circuit with base held at VB and emitter resistor RE:

VE ≈ VB − VBE
IE ≈ VE/RE
IC ≈ αIE
VC = VCC − ICRC
VCE = VC − VE

Check that the collector-base junction remains reverse biased and the transistor has sufficient VCE to remain in the forward-active region.

6. Worked DC Bias Example

Given: VCC = 12 V, VB = 2 V, VBE = 0.7 V, RE = 1 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ and α = 0.99.

Step 1: Calculate emitter voltage.

VE = 2 − 0.7 = 1.3 V

Step 2: Calculate emitter current.

IE = 1.3/1k = 1.3 mA

Step 3: Calculate collector current.

IC = 0.99 × 1.3 = 1.287 mA

Step 4: Calculate collector voltage.

VC = 12 − (1.287 mA × 2.2 kΩ)
VC ≈ 9.169 V

Step 5: Calculate collector-emitter voltage.

VCE = 9.169 − 1.3 = 7.869 V
The estimated Q-point is IC ≈ 1.287 mA and VCE ≈ 7.87 V. The collector is at a higher potential than the base, consistent with reverse bias of the collector-base junction for this NPN example.

7. Current Gain

The current gain of a common-base transistor stage is:

α = IC/IE

Since IE = IC + IB:

α = β/(β + 1)
β = α/(1 − α)

For example, if β = 100:

α = 100/101 = 0.9901

The CB current gain is less than one because the collector current is slightly less than the emitter current.

8. Voltage Gain

Although current gain is below unity, the common-base amplifier can provide high voltage gain because its input resistance is low and its output resistance is high.

For a simplified midband model with an ideal emitter drive and collector load:

Av ≈ gm(RC || RL)

where:

  • gm = IC/VT is transistor transconductance.
  • RC is the collector resistor.
  • RL is the AC load resistance.
  • VT is approximately 26 mV near room temperature.

The gain is positive under the usual voltage definitions: increasing emitter voltage reduces VBE and collector current, causing collector voltage to rise. The input and output voltages are therefore approximately in phase.

The gain expression is an idealized midband approximation. Source resistance, emitter degeneration, transistor output resistance, load and bias network modify the actual gain.

9. Input Impedance

The emitter is the input terminal. The small-signal resistance looking into the emitter is relatively low.

rin ≈ 1/gm ≈ VT/IC

For IC = 1 mA and VT = 26 mV:

rin ≈ 26 Ω

Emitter degeneration and source resistance affect the total input impedance. A low input impedance makes the CB configuration suitable for certain low-impedance signal sources and high-frequency stages.

10. Output Impedance

The output is taken from the collector. The output impedance is generally high compared with that of an emitter follower.

In a simple model, the collector-side output resistance is approximately:

Rout ≈ RC || ro

Here ro is the transistor's small-signal output resistance. If ro is much larger than RC, the output impedance is approximately RC. Actual output impedance depends on the bias network and external load.

11. Frequency Response

The CB amplifier is useful at high frequencies in part because its base is held at AC ground. The input is applied to the emitter, reducing the influence of collector-to-base capacitance feedback compared with a common-emitter stage.

Low-frequency limitations

  • Input coupling capacitor reactance.
  • Emitter bypass capacitor reactance, if used.
  • Output coupling capacitor reactance.

High-frequency limitations

  • Base-emitter and base-collector junction capacitances.
  • Transistor transit time.
  • Wiring and layout parasitics.
  • Load capacitance.
XC = 1/(2πfC)

A coupling capacitor's reactance should be sufficiently small compared with the resistance it sees over the intended operating band.

Configuration selection guide

  • Choose CE when substantial voltage gain and general-purpose amplification are required.
  • Choose CC when buffering and impedance transformation are important.
  • Choose CB when a low input impedance, high output impedance and useful high-frequency performance are needed.

16. Practical Applications of CB Amplifiers

  • High-frequency amplifier stages.
  • RF amplifier circuits.
  • Impedance transformation between low-impedance and higher-impedance stages.
  • Cascode amplifier input stages.
  • Wide-band amplifier designs where reduced Miller effect is beneficial.

Cascode connection: A common-emitter stage followed by a common-base stage can reduce the voltage variation at the first transistor's collector, helping reduce Miller-effect limitations and improve bandwidth.

17. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Calculate α and base current

A CB transistor has IE = 2 mA and IC = 1.98 mA.

α = IC/IE = 1.98/2 = 0.99
IB = IE − IC = 0.02 mA = 20 µA

Problem 2: Calculate voltage gain

Given IC = 2 mA, RC = 2.2 kΩ, RL = 10 kΩ and VT = 26 mV.

gm = IC/VT = 2 mA/26 mV = 76.92 mS
RC || RL = 2.2k || 10k = 1.803 kΩ
Av ≈ gm(RC || RL) ≈ 138.7

This is an idealized small-signal estimate. Real gain is lower when source resistance, emitter degeneration and transistor output resistance are included.

Problem 3: Calculate input resistance

Given IC = 1 mA and VT = 26 mV:

rin ≈ VT/IC = 26 mV/1 mA = 26 Ω

18. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Build and test a low-voltage common-base amplifier and measure its voltage gain and phase relationship.

Components

  • BC547 or 2N2222 NPN transistor; verify the device pinout.
  • Low-voltage DC supply with current limiting.
  • Collector resistor RC and emitter resistor RE.
  • Base-bias resistors and a suitable bypass capacitor.
  • Input and output coupling capacitors.
  • Function generator, oscilloscope and multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Construct the bias network and confirm all connections with power off.
  2. Apply the low-voltage supply and measure VB, VE and VC.
  3. Verify forward-active operation before applying an AC signal.
  4. Apply a small sine wave at the emitter through a coupling capacitor.
  5. Measure input and collector output amplitudes.
  6. Observe the phase relationship on the oscilloscope.
  7. Change the load and observe the gain change.

20. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a common-base amplifier?

A BJT amplifier in which the base is common to the input and output AC circuits.

2. Which terminal receives the input?

The emitter.

3. From which terminal is output taken?

The collector.

4. What is the current gain of a CB amplifier?

α = IC/IE, slightly below unity in normal forward-active operation.

5. What is the relationship between α and β?

α = β/(β + 1), and β = α/(1 − α).

6. Why is CB input impedance low?

The input is applied at the emitter, where the small-signal resistance is approximately 1/gm.

7. Why can CB provide high voltage gain?

A relatively large collector load is driven by a small input resistance, giving a potentially large transconductance gain.

8. Does CB invert phase?

No. The collector output is approximately in phase with the emitter input using the usual voltage definitions.

9. What is the main application of CB?

High-frequency and RF amplifier stages.

10. What is the input resistance approximation?

rin ≈ VT/IC, or approximately 1/gm.

11. What is the output resistance approximation?

Rout ≈ RC || ro in a simple collector-output model.

12. Why is the base AC-grounded?

To establish a common AC reference for input and output and provide a low-impedance base reference over the operating band.

13. What is the Miller effect?

It is the apparent multiplication of a feedback capacitance by circuit gain, which can reduce bandwidth. CB and cascode arrangements can reduce its impact.

14. Why is CE widely used?

It offers substantial voltage and current gain and is versatile for general-purpose amplification.

15. Why is CC called an emitter follower?

Because the emitter output follows the base input with a small voltage difference.

16. Which configuration has the highest input impedance?

Common collector, in the usual comparison.

17. Which configuration has the lowest input impedance?

Common base.

18. Which configuration has approximately 180° phase shift?

Common emitter.

19. What is a cascode amplifier?

A two-transistor arrangement commonly using a CE stage followed by a CB stage.

20. What determines the high-frequency response?

Transistor junction capacitances, transit time, wiring parasitics and load capacitance.
