Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers

1. Introduction to BJT Amplifiers

A BJT amplifier is an electronic circuit that uses a bipolar junction transistor to increase the amplitude of an input signal.

A small variation in the base-emitter input signal produces a corresponding variation in collector current. A load resistor converts this current variation into an amplified output voltage.

BJT amplifiers are commonly used in:

  • Audio amplification.
  • Sensor signal conditioning.
  • Communication circuits.
  • Microphone preamplifiers.
  • Analog signal processing.
  • Electronic instrumentation.
Basic principle: A small input signal controls a larger change in transistor collector current, allowing signal amplification.

2. What is a Small-Signal Amplifier?

A small-signal amplifier operates with a transistor biased at a suitable DC operating point. A relatively small AC signal is superimposed on this DC condition.

The transistor remains in its active region during the signal cycle, and its nonlinear characteristics can be approximated by a linear model around the operating point.

DC and AC Components

The instantaneous base current can be represented as:

iB(t) = IBQ + ib(t)

Similarly:

iC(t) = ICQ + ic(t)

vBE(t) = VBEQ + vbe(t)

Where:

  • IBQ and ICQ are DC quiescent currents.
  • VBEQ is the DC base-emitter bias voltage.
  • ib(t), ic(t) and vbe(t) are small AC variations.
Capital letters with Q commonly represent DC quiescent quantities, while lowercase quantities represent small AC variations.

3. Role of the Q-Point in Amplification

The Q-point (quiescent operating point) is the DC operating condition of a transistor when no AC input signal is applied.

It is commonly specified by:

Q-point = (ICQ, VCEQ)

A properly selected Q-point allows the transistor to amplify the input signal without entering cutoff or saturation during normal operation.

4. DC Analysis and AC Analysis

BJT amplifier analysis is generally divided into two stages.

4.1 DC Analysis

DC analysis determines the transistor's biasing condition and Q-point.

During DC analysis:

  • Capacitors are treated as open circuits in steady state.
  • AC input sources are set to zero.
  • DC supplies remain active.
  • Collector current and collector-emitter voltage are calculated.

4.2 AC Analysis

AC analysis determines how the amplifier responds to the input signal.

For midband small-signal analysis:

  • DC voltage supplies are treated as AC ground.
  • Coupling capacitors are approximated as short circuits.
  • Bypass capacitors are approximated as short circuits when their reactance is sufficiently small.
  • The transistor is replaced by an appropriate small-signal model.
These capacitor approximations are midband assumptions. At low frequencies, capacitor reactance must be considered. At high frequencies, transistor parasitic capacitances become important.

5. Small-Signal Equivalent Models

A small-signal model represents the transistor's incremental behavior around its DC operating point. It simplifies AC circuit analysis.

5.1 Hybrid-π Model

The hybrid-π model is commonly used to analyze BJT amplifier circuits.

Important parameters include:

Parameter Meaning Typical Expression
gm Transconductance gm = ICQ / VT
rπ Small-signal base-emitter resistance rπ = β / gm
ro Output resistance due to Early effect ro ≈ VA / ICQ

Where:

  • gm = transconductance in siemens.
  • VT = thermal voltage, approximately 25.9 mV at 300 K.
  • β = small-signal common-emitter current gain.
  • VA = Early voltage.

The output resistance ro may be neglected in introductory calculations when it is much larger than the other resistances in the circuit.

5.2 T-Model

The T-model is another small-signal representation of a BJT. It is especially useful in circuits involving emitter resistance.

re ≈ VT / IEQ

At room temperature:

re ≈ 25.9 mV / IEQ

IEQ must be expressed in amperes to obtain re in ohms.

6. Important Small-Signal Parameters

6.1 Transconductance (gm)

Transconductance represents the change in collector current produced by a small change in base-emitter voltage around the operating point.

gm = ICQ / VT

6.2 Input Resistance (rπ)

The small-signal base-emitter input resistance in the hybrid-π model is:

rπ = β / gm

rπ = βVT / ICQ

6.3 Intrinsic Emitter Resistance (re)

The approximate intrinsic emitter resistance is:

re ≈ VT / IEQ
The approximation rπ ≈ βre is commonly used when IEQ and ICQ are close, as is normally the case for a transistor with a reasonably large beta.

7. Voltage Gain, Current Gain and Power Gain

7.1 Voltage Gain

Voltage gain is the ratio of output AC voltage to input AC voltage.

Av = vo / vi

The gain magnitude is often expressed in decibels:

Voltage Gain (dB) = 20 log10 |Av|

7.2 Current Gain

Current gain is the ratio of output AC current to input AC current.

Ai = io / ii

7.3 Power Gain

Power gain is the ratio of output signal power to input signal power.

Ap = Po / Pi

For compatible voltage and current reference directions, power gain is related to voltage and current gain by:

Ap = Av × Ai

Power gain in decibels is:

Power Gain (dB) = 10 log10 Ap

8. Phase Relationship in a BJT Amplifier

The phase relationship between input and output depends on the transistor configuration.

Configuration Voltage Phase Relationship
Common Emitter (CE) 180° phase inversion
Common Collector (CC) Approximately 0° phase shift
Common Base (CB) Approximately 0° phase shift

9. Coupling and Bypass Capacitors

9.1 Input Coupling Capacitor

The input coupling capacitor passes the AC signal to the transistor while blocking unwanted DC from the signal source.

9.2 Output Coupling Capacitor

The output coupling capacitor transfers the amplified AC signal to the load while preventing the transistor's DC collector voltage from reaching the load.

9.3 Emitter Bypass Capacitor

An emitter bypass capacitor is connected in parallel with the emitter resistor for AC operation.

It can reduce AC negative feedback and increase voltage gain in the frequency range where its impedance is sufficiently low.

XC = 1 / (2πfC)

Where:

  • XC = capacitive reactance in ohms.
  • f = frequency in hertz.
  • C = capacitance in farads.

10. Numerical Example: Small-Signal Parameters

A BJT operates at a collector quiescent current of 1 mA. Assume:

  • ICQ = 1 mA
  • β = 100
  • VT = 25.9 mV

Step 1: Calculate Transconductance

gm = ICQ / VT

gm = 0.001 / 0.0259

gm ≈ 0.0386 S

gm ≈ 38.6 mS

Step 2: Calculate Input Resistance

rπ = β / gm

rπ = 100 / 0.0386

rπ ≈ 2591 Ω

rπ ≈ 2.59 kΩ

Step 3: Calculate Intrinsic Emitter Resistance

Assuming IEQ ≈ ICQ:

re ≈ VT / IEQ

re ≈ 0.0259 / 0.001

re ≈ 25.9 Ω
Calculated results:
  • gm ≈ 38.6 mS
  • rπ ≈ 2.59 kΩ
  • re ≈ 25.9 Ω

11. Characteristics of a Good Amplifier

  • Suitable voltage, current or power gain.
  • Low signal distortion.
  • Stable operating point.
  • Appropriate input impedance.
  • Appropriate output impedance.
  • Required operating frequency range.
  • Acceptable noise performance.
  • Reliable thermal performance.

13. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a BJT amplifier?

It is a circuit that uses a bipolar junction transistor to increase the amplitude of an electrical signal.

Q2. What is a small-signal amplifier?

It is an amplifier that operates around a DC operating point and uses a linearized transistor model for small AC variations.

Q3. What is a Q-point?

It is the transistor's DC operating point without an AC input signal.

Q4. Why is DC analysis performed before AC analysis?

DC analysis determines the bias current and voltage required to establish the operating point for small-signal analysis.

Q5. Define transconductance.

Transconductance is the ratio of a small change in collector current to a small change in base-emitter voltage around the operating point.

Q6. What is the approximate value of thermal voltage at room temperature?

Approximately 25.9 mV at 300 K.

Q7. Why does a common-emitter amplifier invert the signal?

An increase in base-emitter signal increases collector current, producing a larger voltage drop across the collector resistor and decreasing collector output voltage.

Q8. What is the purpose of an emitter bypass capacitor?

It reduces AC emitter degeneration over its effective frequency range, increasing voltage gain.

15. Lesson Summary

  • A BJT amplifier increases the amplitude of an input signal.
  • Small-signal amplification takes place around a DC Q-point.
  • DC analysis determines the operating point.
  • AC analysis determines the signal response.
  • The hybrid-π model is used for small-signal analysis.
  • gm = ICQ / VT.
  • rπ = β / gm.
  • Voltage gain is Av = vo / vi.
  • A common-emitter amplifier produces approximately 180° phase inversion.
  • Coupling and bypass capacitors control AC signal paths.
Remember: Correct DC biasing establishes the Q-point, while the small-signal model helps determine the amplifier's AC performance.
