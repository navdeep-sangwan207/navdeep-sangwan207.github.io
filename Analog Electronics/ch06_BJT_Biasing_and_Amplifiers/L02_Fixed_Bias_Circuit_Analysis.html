Lesson 2: Fixed Bias and Collector-to-Base Bias

Understand two basic BJT biasing methods, their circuit operation, DC equations and stability.

Chapter 6: BJT Biasing and Amplifiers

1. Introduction to BJT Biasing Methods

BJT biasing methods are used to establish a suitable DC operating point by controlling the base current and collector current of a transistor.

Two basic biasing techniques are:

  1. Fixed Bias (Base Bias)
  2. Collector-to-Base Bias (Collector Feedback Bias)

Both circuits can be used with an NPN transistor. The following analysis assumes a silicon transistor operating in the forward-active region unless stated otherwise.

2. Fixed Bias Circuit

Fixed bias is one of the simplest methods of biasing a BJT. A resistor RB is connected between the DC supply VCC and the transistor base.

The collector is connected to VCC through a collector resistor RC, while the emitter is connected to ground.

Fixed-Bias NPN Transistor Circuit

Conceptual circuit layout: VCC supplies both the base-bias and collector circuits.

Working Principle

  1. VCC supplies current through RB to the base.
  2. The base-emitter junction becomes forward biased.
  3. A small base current IB flows.
  4. The transistor controls a larger collector current IC.
  5. RC develops a voltage drop according to the collector current.

3. Fixed Bias Equations

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law to the base-emitter loop:

VCC = IB × RB + VBE

IB = (VCC − VBE) / RB

For forward-active operation:

IC ≈ β × IB

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law to the collector loop:

VCC = IC × RC + VCE

VCE = VCC − IC × RC

These equations assume that the transistor remains in the active region. If the calculated collector current demands more current than the collector circuit can supply, the transistor will enter saturation and IC ≈ βIB will no longer be valid.

4. Fixed Bias Numerical Example

Consider the following circuit parameters:

  • VCC = 12 V
  • RB = 220 kΩ
  • RC = 2 kΩ
  • VBE = 0.7 V
  • β = 100

Step 1: Calculate Base Current

IB = (VCC − VBE) / RB

IB = (12 − 0.7) / 220 kΩ

IB = 11.3 / 220000

IB = 51.36 µA

Step 2: Calculate Collector Current

IC = β × IB

IC = 100 × 51.36 µA

IC = 5.136 mA

Step 3: Calculate VCE

VCE = VCC − IC × RC

VCE = 12 − (5.136 mA × 2 kΩ)

VCE = 12 − 10.272

VCE = 1.728 V
Calculated Q-Point:
IB = 51.36 µA
ICQ = 5.136 mA
VCEQ = 1.728 V

The calculated result is consistent with the active-region approximation for this example.

5. Advantages and Disadvantages of Fixed Bias

Advantages

  • Simple circuit construction.
  • Requires very few components.
  • Easy to calculate and understand.
  • Useful for introductory transistor analysis.

Disadvantages

  • Q-point strongly depends on transistor beta.
  • Collector current changes with temperature.
  • Does not provide effective thermal stabilization.
  • Less suitable for stable linear amplifier designs.
Important: Fixed bias is generally not preferred when a stable amplifier operating point is required because the collector current is sensitive to beta and temperature.

6. Collector-to-Base Bias

Collector-to-base bias is also called collector-feedback bias. In this method, the base resistor RB is connected between the collector and the base instead of directly between VCC and the base.

This arrangement introduces negative feedback that helps stabilize collector current.

Collector-to-Base Feedback Bias Circuit

Conceptual circuit: RB connects the collector node back to the base.

Working Principle

  1. Base current flows from the collector node through RB into the base.
  2. Collector current increases if the transistor conducts more strongly.
  3. The voltage drop across RC increases.
  4. The collector voltage decreases.
  5. The voltage driving base current through RB decreases.
  6. Base current and collector current are therefore opposed from increasing further.
Negative feedback: An increase in collector current reduces collector voltage, which reduces base current. This opposes the original increase in collector current.

7. Collector-to-Base Bias Equations

Applying KVL around the collector-base-emitter loop:

VCC = IB × RB + VBE + (IB + IC) × RC

Since IC ≈ βIB in the forward-active region:

VCC = IB × RB + VBE + (β + 1)IB × RC

IB = (VCC − VBE) / [RB + (β + 1)RC]

Collector current and collector-emitter voltage are:

IC ≈ β × IB

VCE = VCC − (IC + IB) × RC

The second equation includes the emitter current through RC because the collector resistor supplies both collector and base current in this circuit.

8. Collector-to-Base Bias Numerical Example

Consider:

  • VCC = 12 V
  • RB = 470 kΩ
  • RC = 2 kΩ
  • VBE = 0.7 V
  • β = 100

Step 1: Calculate Base Current

IB = (VCC − VBE) / [RB + (β + 1)RC]

IB = (12 − 0.7) / [470 kΩ + 101 × 2 kΩ]

IB = 11.3 / 672 kΩ

IB = 16.82 µA

Step 2: Calculate Collector Current

IC = β × IB

IC = 100 × 16.82 µA

IC = 1.682 mA

Step 3: Calculate VCE

VCE = VCC − (IC + IB) × RC

VCE = 12 − (1.682 mA + 0.01682 mA) × 2 kΩ

VCE ≈ 8.60 V
Calculated Q-Point:
IB ≈ 16.82 µA
ICQ ≈ 1.682 mA
VCEQ ≈ 8.60 V

9. Advantages and Disadvantages of Collector Feedback Bias

Advantages

  • Provides negative feedback for DC stabilization.
  • More stable than simple fixed bias.
  • Reduces sensitivity of collector current to beta variations.
  • Uses a relatively simple circuit.

Disadvantages

  • Bias point is still affected by transistor parameters and temperature.
  • Feedback reduces the available signal gain unless designed appropriately.
  • Collector voltage changes with collector current.
  • Less predictable than a well-designed voltage-divider bias circuit with emitter stabilization.

10. Comparison: Fixed Bias vs Collector-to-Base Bias

Parameter Fixed Bias Collector-to-Base Bias
Base resistor connection VCC to base Collector to base
Feedback No collector feedback Negative feedback
Q-point stability Poor Improved
Beta sensitivity High Lower than fixed bias
Circuit complexity Very simple Simple
Typical use Basic transistor circuits Basic stabilized bias circuits

11. Practical Applications

  • Basic transistor switching and demonstration circuits.
  • Introductory common-emitter amplifier circuits.
  • Transistor biasing experiments in electronics laboratories.
  • Studying negative feedback in transistor circuits.
For practical amplifier design, do not select a bias circuit solely because its calculated Q-point is correct. Check beta variation, temperature, transistor power dissipation and component tolerances.

12. Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is fixed bias?

Fixed bias is a BJT biasing method in which a resistor connects the supply voltage to the transistor base to establish base current.

Q2. Why is fixed bias called base bias?

Because the base current is established using a resistor connected to the base.

Q3. What is collector-to-base bias?

It is a biasing arrangement in which the base resistor is connected between collector and base, providing negative feedback.

Q4. Why is collector feedback used?

It opposes changes in collector current and improves the stability of the operating point.

Q5. Which circuit has better stability?

Collector-to-base bias generally has better stability than fixed bias because of negative feedback.

Q6. What is the main limitation of fixed bias?

Its collector current is highly sensitive to transistor beta and temperature variations.

Q7. Does collector-to-base bias completely eliminate thermal runaway?

No. It improves DC stabilization but does not completely eliminate temperature-related problems.

Numerical Practice 1: Fixed Bias

An NPN transistor has VCC = 10 V, RB = 200 kΩ, RC = 1 kΩ, β = 80 and VBE = 0.7 V.

Calculate IB, IC and VCE.

Show Answer

IB = (10 − 0.7) / 200 kΩ = 46.5 µA

IC = 80 × 46.5 µA = 3.72 mA

VCE = 10 − (3.72 mA × 1 kΩ) = 6.28 V

Numerical Practice 2: Collector-to-Base Bias

An NPN transistor has VCC = 15 V, RB = 560 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, β = 100 and VBE = 0.7 V.

Calculate IB, IC and VCE.

Show Answer

IB = (15 − 0.7) / [560 kΩ + 101 × 2.2 kΩ]

IB ≈ 18.76 µA

IC ≈ 1.876 mA

VCE = 15 − (1.876 mA + 0.01876 mA) × 2.2 kΩ

VCE ≈ 10.83 V

14. Lesson Summary

  • Fixed bias uses a base resistor connected to VCC.
  • Collector-to-base bias connects the base resistor to the collector.
  • Fixed bias has poor Q-point stability.
  • Collector feedback provides negative feedback.
  • Collector feedback improves stability compared with fixed bias.
  • Neither method completely eliminates temperature effects.
Remember: Fixed bias is simple, while collector-to-base bias improves stability through negative feedback.
