2. What Is a Fixed-Bias Circuit?

Fixed bias is a simple BJT biasing arrangement in which a resistor RB connects the base to a DC supply VBB. The collector is connected to another DC supply VCC through resistor RC, while the emitter is grounded for an NPN transistor.

The base resistor establishes the base current. Under forward-active conditions, the collector current is approximately determined by the transistor's DC current gain.

IB = (VBB − VBE) / RB
IC ≈ βDC × IB
VCE = VCC − IC RC

Main components

VBB

Base-bias supply.

RB

Base resistor controlling base current.

VCC

Collector supply.

RC

Collector resistor setting the collector load line.

Q1

NPN transistor used as the active device.

Ground

Common reference for the emitter and supplies.

4. Working Principle

  1. The base supply VBB applies a forward-bias voltage across the base-emitter junction through RB.
  2. Base current IB flows into the base terminal.
  3. When the transistor is in forward-active operation, collector current is approximately βDC times base current.
  4. Collector current produces a voltage drop across RC.
  5. The remaining collector supply voltage appears across the collector-emitter terminals.
VRC = IC × RC
VCE = VCC − VRC

Animated current-flow demonstration

Base drive: OFF

IB = 0

IC ≈ 0

VCE ≈ VCC

Transistor condition: Cutoff

5. DC Equivalent Circuit

For DC analysis, capacitors in an amplifier circuit are treated as open circuits after reaching steady state. Any AC input source is set to zero. The remaining circuit is the DC bias network.

  • Coupling capacitors block DC and do not affect the steady-state bias calculation.
  • Bypass capacitors are open circuits under DC analysis.
  • Only the DC supplies, resistors and transistor remain in the DC equivalent circuit.
Base loop:
VBB − IB RB − VBE = 0

Collector loop:
VCC − IC RC − VCE = 0

6. Base Current Calculation

Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law to the base-emitter loop:

VBB = IB RB + VBE
IB = (VBB − VBE) / RB

Example

Given:

  • VBB = 5 V
  • RB = 220 kΩ
  • VBE = 0.7 V
IB = (5 − 0.7) / 220000
IB = 19.55 µA

Base current is approximately 19.55 µA.

7. Collector Current Calculation

In forward-active operation, the collector current is approximated using the DC current gain beta.

IC = βDC × IB

Example

For IB = 19.55 µA and βDC = 100:

IC = 100 × 19.55 µA
IC = 1.955 mA

This is the active-region estimate. The external collector circuit limits the actual current. If βIB exceeds the current allowed by the collector load line, the transistor enters saturation and IC is no longer equal to βIB.

8. Collector-Emitter Voltage Calculation

Applying Kirchhoff's voltage law to the collector loop:

VCC = IC RC + VCE
VCE = VCC − IC RC

Example

Given VCC = 12 V, RC = 2.2 kΩ and IC = 1.955 mA:

VCE = 12 − (1.955 mA × 2.2 kΩ)
VCE = 12 − 4.301
VCE ≈ 7.70 V

The approximate Q-point is (1.955 mA, 7.70 V).

Consider the following circuit values:

IC = 100 × 19.55 µA
IC = 1.955 mA

Step 3: Collector-emitter voltage

VCE = 12 − (1.955 mA × 2.2 kΩ)
VCE = 7.70 V

Step 4: Check saturation limit

Using an idealized VCE = 0 load-line endpoint:

IC(max) = VCC / RC
IC(max) = 12 / 2200
IC(max) = 5.45 mA

The estimated collector current of 1.955 mA is below the ideal load-line limit. The calculated VCE is positive and comfortably above the usual saturation voltage, so the forward-active approximation is consistent with this example.

Q-point: ICQ ≈ 1.955 mA and VCEQ ≈ 7.70 V.

12. Effect of Beta Variation

A fixed-bias circuit depends strongly on transistor beta. If beta increases while VBB, RB and VBE remain approximately unchanged, the base current remains nearly fixed but the active-region collector-current estimate increases.

IC ≈ βDC × IB

However, the collector load limits the maximum possible current. Therefore, increasing beta beyond the value that drives the transistor into saturation does not cause collector current to increase without limit.

13. Advantages of Fixed Bias

  • Simple circuit construction.
  • Few components are required.
  • Easy to understand and analyse.
  • Useful for introductory transistor bias calculations.
  • Can be used in switching circuits where appropriate base drive is provided.

14. Disadvantages and Limitations

  • Q-point is strongly dependent on transistor beta.
  • Collector current is sensitive to temperature-related parameter changes.
  • There is no emitter resistor providing local DC negative feedback in the basic circuit.
  • It can suffer from poor thermal stability.
  • It is generally unsuitable as the preferred bias arrangement for a stable discrete linear amplifier.

A fixed-bias circuit may be adequate for simple switching or teaching examples, but a more stable bias arrangement is normally preferred for practical linear amplifier design.

15. Practical Laboratory Experiment

Objective

To construct a fixed-bias NPN transistor circuit and determine its DC operating point.

Components

  • NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222.
  • Two suitable DC supplies or a dual-output supply.
  • RB = 220 kΩ (example starting value).
  • RC = 2.2 kΩ (example starting value).
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Check the transistor pin configuration using its datasheet.
  2. Connect the emitter to common ground.
  3. Connect RC between collector and VCC.
  4. Connect RB between VBB and base.
  5. Ensure the base and collector supplies share a common ground reference.
  6. Apply current-limited low-voltage DC supplies.
  7. Measure VB, VC and VE with respect to ground.
  8. Calculate VBE = VB − VE and VCE = VC − VE.
  9. Estimate IC from the collector resistor voltage drop.
  10. Compare the measured Q-point with the calculated value.

17. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a fixed-bias circuit?
A BJT bias arrangement in which a base resistor is connected to a DC base supply to establish base current.
2. Why is it called fixed bias?
The base current is primarily set by the base supply and base resistor, assuming the transistor junction voltage is approximately constant.
3. What is the base current equation?
IB = (VBB − VBE) / RB.
4. What is the collector current equation in forward-active operation?
IC ≈ βDC × IB.
5. How do you calculate VCE?
For an emitter-grounded circuit, VCE = VCC − IC RC.
6. What is the main disadvantage of fixed bias?
Its operating point is strongly dependent on beta and temperature-related transistor variations.
7. Does IC always equal beta times IB?
No. That approximation applies in forward-active operation. In saturation, the external load limits collector current.
8. Why is an emitter resistor absent in basic fixed bias?
The simplest fixed-bias arrangement grounds the emitter directly, so it does not provide emitter-resistor negative feedback.
9. What happens when beta increases?
The active-region collector-current estimate increases for the same base current, until the collector circuit forces saturation.
10. Why are two supplies shown in some fixed-bias diagrams?
A separate VBB simplifies analysis of the base loop; practical circuits may derive both bias voltages from a common supply.
11. What is the collector load-line equation?
VCE = VCC − IC RC.
12. Is fixed bias suitable for a stable linear amplifier?
It is generally not preferred because the Q-point is sensitive to beta and temperature variations.
13. How can bias stability be improved?
Use suitable feedback biasing, emitter resistance and a properly designed voltage-divider network.
14. What is the function of RC?
It develops a collector voltage drop and establishes the collector load line.
15. What is the Q-point of a transistor?
The DC operating point represented by ICQ and VCEQ with no external AC signal.
