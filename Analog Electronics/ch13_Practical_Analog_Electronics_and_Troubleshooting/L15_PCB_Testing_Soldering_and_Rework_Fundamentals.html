Lesson 15: PCB Testing, Soldering and Rework Fundamentals

Practical PCB Inspection, Assembly, Repair and Troubleshooting

1. Introduction to PCB

A Printed Circuit Board (PCB) is a board used to mechanically support and electrically connect electronic components through conductive copper tracks, pads and vias.

PCBs are widely used in power supplies, embedded systems, communication equipment, industrial controllers, consumer electronics and automotive electronics.

Functions of a PCB

  • Provides mechanical support to components.
  • Provides electrical connections between components.
  • Organizes circuit components in a compact layout.
  • Improves repeatability in electronic manufacturing.
  • Supports reliable circuit assembly and testing.
Learning Objective: Understand PCB construction, inspect circuit boards, perform continuity tests, identify soldering defects and learn basic through-hole and SMD rework.

2. Types of Printed Circuit Boards

1. Single-Sided PCB

Copper tracks are present on one side of the insulating substrate.

  • Simple construction.
  • Low manufacturing cost.
  • Used in simple electronic circuits.

2. Double-Sided PCB

Copper tracks are present on both sides of the board. Vias or plated-through holes can connect the two layers.

  • Supports more complex circuits.
  • Allows routing on two copper layers.
  • Used in many industrial and embedded boards.

3. Multilayer PCB

A multilayer PCB contains three or more conductive copper layers separated by insulating material.

  • Supports high component density.
  • Allows dedicated power and ground planes.
  • Used in computers, communication systems and advanced electronics.

4. Flexible PCB

Flexible PCBs use flexible insulating materials and can bend to fit mechanical requirements.

5. Rigid-Flex PCB

Rigid-flex PCBs combine rigid board sections with flexible circuit sections.

3. Basic PCB Construction

A typical PCB contains several materials and conductive structures.

PCB Part Function
Substrate Provides mechanical support and insulation.
Copper Layer Forms conductive tracks and planes.
Solder Mask Protects copper and helps prevent solder bridging.
Silkscreen Displays component labels and identification markings.
Pad Provides a soldering point for component terminals.
Via Provides an electrical connection between PCB layers.

Common PCB Materials

  • FR-4: Common glass-reinforced epoxy laminate.
  • Aluminium substrate: Used in many LED and power applications.
  • Polyimide: Used in flexible PCBs.
  • High-frequency laminates: Used in RF and microwave designs.

4. Through-Hole and Surface-Mount Technology

Through-Hole Technology (THT)

In through-hole technology, component leads pass through holes in the PCB and are soldered to pads.

  • Strong mechanical connections.
  • Convenient for manual assembly.
  • Common in connectors, transformers and larger components.

Surface-Mount Technology (SMT)

In surface-mount technology, components are mounted directly on PCB surface pads.

  • Supports compact circuit design.
  • Allows high component density.
  • Suitable for automated assembly.
  • Common in modern electronic devices.
Feature THT SMT
Mounting Leads through holes Mounted on surface pads
Component Size Generally larger Often smaller
Assembly Manual or automated Usually automated in production
Rework Relatively straightforward Requires suitable tools and technique

5. PCB Inspection

PCB inspection is the first step in identifying manufacturing defects, component damage and electrical faults.

Visual Inspection Checklist

  • Check for burnt or discolored components.
  • Look for cracked or damaged components.
  • Inspect solder joints.
  • Check for solder bridges.
  • Inspect PCB tracks for cuts or scratches.
  • Check connectors and component polarity.
  • Look for corrosion and contamination.
  • Inspect vias and plated-through holes.

Inspection Tools

  • Magnifying glass.
  • Inspection microscope.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • ESD-safe tweezers.
  • Appropriate lighting.

6. PCB Track and Continuity Testing

Continuity testing checks whether an electrical connection exists between two PCB points.

Testing Procedure

  1. Switch OFF the circuit.
  2. Disconnect external power sources.
  3. Discharge capacitors safely.
  4. Set the multimeter to continuity mode.
  5. Place one probe at the first track point.
  6. Place the other probe at the second point.
  7. Observe the resistance or continuity indication.
A continuity beep does not always prove that a PCB connection is healthy under operating current. Check resistance, current capacity and the circuit's operating behavior when necessary.

7. PCB Short-Circuit Testing

A short circuit can occur between power and ground, adjacent tracks or component terminals.

Testing Procedure

  1. Disconnect the PCB from the power source.
  2. Discharge capacitors.
  3. Set the multimeter to resistance mode.
  4. Measure between the supply rail and ground.
  5. Observe whether the resistance is unexpectedly low.
  6. Compare the reading with the circuit design.
  7. Isolate sections or components to locate the fault.

Low resistance between power and ground does not automatically mean a short circuit. Some circuits have low-resistance loads, coils or semiconductor paths. Capacitors may also cause changing resistance readings while charging from the meter.

8. Introduction to Soldering

Soldering is a process used to join electronic components to PCB pads using molten solder.

The solder cools and forms an electrical and mechanical connection.

Common Soldering Tools

  • Soldering iron.
  • Solder wire.
  • Flux.
  • Soldering iron stand.
  • Tip cleaning sponge or brass wool.
  • Side cutters.
  • Desoldering pump.
  • ESD-safe tweezers.

Soldering Iron Temperature

Temperature depends on solder alloy, component sensitivity, PCB thermal mass and the manufacturer's process guidance.

For many hand-soldering tasks, a controlled temperature in the approximate range of 300°C to 370°C is commonly used. This is a starting range, not a universal setting.

9. Types of Solder

1. Lead-Based Solder

Traditional electronics solder commonly uses a tin-lead alloy, such as Sn63/Pb37.

  • Good wetting characteristics.
  • Relatively low melting temperature.
  • Contains lead, which is hazardous.

2. Lead-Free Solder

Lead-free solder is used in many modern electronics manufacturing processes. Common alloys include SAC305 (Sn96.5/Ag3.0/Cu0.5).

  • Does not use lead as an alloy constituent.
  • Typically requires a higher process temperature than common tin-lead solder.
  • Widely used for RoHS-compliant assembly.
Lead-free does not mean completely harmless. Soldering fumes, flux residues and hot materials still require suitable ventilation and safe handling.

10. Soldering Procedure

Step-by-Step Method

  1. Inspect the PCB pad and component lead.
  2. Clean the soldering iron tip.
  3. Apply a suitable amount of flux if required.
  4. Position the component correctly.
  5. Heat the pad and component lead together.
  6. Feed solder into the heated joint.
  7. Remove the solder wire.
  8. Remove the soldering iron.
  9. Allow the joint to cool without movement.
  10. Inspect the finished solder joint.
The solder should wet both the pad and component lead. A good joint depends on proper surface preparation, heating, flux and solder quantity.

11. Types of Soldering Defects

Defect Description Possible Cause
Cold Solder Joint Poorly formed solder connection. Insufficient heating or joint movement during cooling.
Solder Bridge Unwanted connection between adjacent pads. Excess solder or poor technique.
Insufficient Solder Incomplete solder coverage. Insufficient solder supply or poor wetting.
Excessive Solder Too much solder around the joint. Excessive solder feed.
Lifted Pad PCB pad separates from the substrate. Excessive heat or mechanical force.
Cracked Joint Physical crack in the solder connection. Mechanical stress or thermal cycling.

12. Desoldering Techniques

Desoldering is the process of removing solder from an existing joint to remove or replace a component.

1. Desoldering Pump

A spring-loaded desoldering pump creates suction to remove molten solder.

2. Desoldering Wick

Desoldering wick is a braided copper material that absorbs molten solder.

3. Desoldering Station

A powered desoldering station heats the solder joint and removes molten solder using vacuum suction.

Basic Procedure

  1. Switch OFF and disconnect the equipment.
  2. Identify the component and its polarity.
  3. Apply flux where appropriate.
  4. Heat the solder joint.
  5. Remove molten solder using a pump or wick.
  6. Repeat carefully if necessary.
  7. Remove the component without pulling against solid solder.
  8. Inspect the PCB pads and holes.
Do not pull a component while its leads remain soldered. Excessive force can damage PCB pads, tracks and plated-through holes.

13. SMD Soldering and Rework

Surface-Mount Device (SMD) components are mounted directly on the PCB surface. Their small size requires suitable tools and controlled rework techniques.

Common SMD Packages

  • 0603 and 0805 passive components.
  • SOT-23 transistors and regulators.
  • SOIC integrated circuits.
  • TSSOP packages.
  • QFN packages.
  • BGA packages.

SMD Rework Tools

  • Hot-air rework station.
  • Temperature-controlled soldering station.
  • Fine-tip soldering iron.
  • ESD-safe tweezers.
  • Flux and solder wick.
  • Microscope or magnifying system.

Basic SMD Rework Procedure

  1. Identify the component and its orientation.
  2. Check the package and datasheet.
  3. Apply suitable flux.
  4. Use controlled heating appropriate to the component and PCB.
  5. Remove the component after solder melts.
  6. Clean the pads without damaging them.
  7. Position the replacement component.
  8. Solder and inspect all connections.
  9. Perform electrical tests after cooling.

14. PCB Track Repair

PCB tracks may become damaged because of overheating, corrosion, mechanical damage or excessive rework.

Common Repair Methods

  • Repairing a damaged track using suitable insulated wire.
  • Replacing a damaged connector.
  • Repairing a pad using an appropriate PCB repair kit.
  • Restoring a connection using a suitable jumper wire.
  • Replacing damaged components and checking surrounding tracks.

Track Repair Procedure

  1. Disconnect the PCB from power.
  2. Identify the damaged track.
  3. Verify the original connection using the PCB layout or schematic.
  4. Remove loose or damaged material carefully.
  5. Prepare the repair points.
  6. Install a suitable jumper or repair conductor.
  7. Secure the repair mechanically where required.
  8. Check continuity and isolation from adjacent tracks.
  9. Verify circuit operation.
The repair conductor must have suitable current capacity and insulation. A jumper wire must not create unwanted connections or compromise high-voltage spacing.

15. ESD Protection During PCB Handling

Electrostatic Discharge (ESD) is the sudden transfer of electrical charge between objects at different electrical potentials.

ESD can damage sensitive components such as MOSFETs, microcontrollers, memory devices and integrated circuits.

ESD Protection Methods

  • Use a properly grounded ESD wrist strap.
  • Work on an ESD-safe mat.
  • Use ESD-safe tools and containers.
  • Store sensitive components in ESD-safe packaging.
  • Avoid touching component pins unnecessarily.
  • Follow the workstation's ESD control procedure.
An ESD wrist strap must be used with an approved ESD grounding system. Do not connect yourself directly to mains earth using improvised wiring.

16. PCB Testing After Repair

A repaired PCB must be inspected and tested before returning it to service.

Testing Checklist

  1. Inspect repaired solder joints.
  2. Check component polarity and orientation.
  3. Check continuity of repaired tracks.
  4. Check for shorts between supply and ground.
  5. Verify connectors and wiring.
  6. Apply power using a current-limited supply when appropriate.
  7. Measure supply and important signal voltages.
  8. Check output waveform if necessary.
  9. Test under the specified load.
  10. Observe temperature and operating stability.

17. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Perform basic PCB inspection, continuity testing, soldering and desoldering on a low-voltage training board.

Required Equipment

  • Practice PCB or low-voltage training board.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Temperature-controlled soldering station.
  • Solder wire and flux.
  • Desoldering pump or solder wick.
  • ESD-safe tweezers and mat.
  • Magnifying glass.

Procedure

  1. Inspect the PCB visually.
  2. Identify pads, tracks, vias and components.
  3. Perform continuity testing on selected tracks.
  4. Practice soldering a through-hole resistor.
  5. Inspect the solder joint.
  6. Remove the resistor using a suitable desoldering method.
  7. Inspect the pad and plated-through hole.
  8. Install the resistor again.
  9. Check continuity and adjacent-track isolation.
  10. Power the low-voltage circuit and verify operation.

Observation Table

Test Expected Result Observation
Visual inspection No visible damage Record
Track continuity Expected electrical connection Record
Solder joint Good wetting and connection Record
Adjacent track isolation No unintended short Record
Final operation Normal circuit operation Record

Expected Result

The learner should be able to inspect a PCB, identify basic soldering defects, perform continuity checks and complete simple through-hole soldering and desoldering operations.

18. Common PCB Troubleshooting Problems

Problem Possible Cause Corrective Action
PCB not powering ON Open track, faulty connector or fuse. Inspect power path and measure supply voltage.
Intermittent operation Cracked solder joint or loose connector. Inspect and repair the connection.
Excessive current Short circuit or damaged component. Disconnect power and isolate the faulty section.
Unstable signal Ground problem or poor solder connection. Check ground paths and signal connections.
Component overheating Overload or incorrect component rating. Check circuit current and power dissipation.
Noisy output Poor grounding or filtering. Inspect decoupling and grounding arrangements.

20. Lesson Summary

  • PCBs provide mechanical support and electrical connections.
  • Common PCB types include single-sided, double-sided and multilayer.
  • THT and SMT are two major component assembly technologies.
  • Visual inspection helps identify physical PCB defects.
  • Continuity and resistance measurements help locate electrical faults.
  • Soldering creates electrical and mechanical connections.
  • Desoldering allows component removal and replacement.
  • SMD rework requires controlled heating and suitable tools.
  • ESD precautions protect sensitive electronic components.
  • Every PCB repair should be verified through electrical testing.
Placement Tip: Be prepared to demonstrate soldering, desoldering, PCB continuity testing, component replacement and identification of common soldering defects during practical electronics interviews.

21. Workshop Safety Precautions

  • Use a temperature-controlled soldering station.
  • Keep the soldering iron in its stand when not in use.
  • Use suitable fume extraction or ventilation.
  • Avoid touching the hot iron tip and heated joints.
  • Wear appropriate eye protection.
  • Use ESD protection when handling sensitive components.
  • Disconnect power before PCB repair.
  • Never apply excessive mechanical force during desoldering.
  • Wash hands after handling lead-containing solder.
