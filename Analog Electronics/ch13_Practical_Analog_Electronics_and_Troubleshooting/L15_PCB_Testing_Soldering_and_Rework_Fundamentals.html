PCB Testing, Soldering and Rework Fundamentals

Practical printed circuit board inspection, soldering, component replacement and testing.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand PCB construction and copper-layer arrangements.
  • Identify common PCB components and mounting technologies.
  • Inspect solder joints and recognize typical soldering defects.
  • Understand through-hole and SMD soldering techniques.
  • Perform basic desoldering and component rework.
  • Apply ESD protection during electronic assembly and repair.
  • Test PCB tracks, pads, vias and connectors.
  • Perform functional testing after PCB repair.

2. What Is a Printed Circuit Board?

A printed circuit board (PCB) mechanically supports electronic components and electrically connects them using conductive copper tracks, pads and planes.

Substrate

Insulating base material, commonly FR-4 glass-epoxy laminate.

Copper layer

Conductive material forming tracks, pads and planes.

Solder mask

Protective coating that helps prevent solder bridging and oxidation.

Silkscreen

Printed reference designators, component outlines and markings.

Vias

Conductive connections between copper layers.

Pads

Conductive areas where component terminals are soldered.

3. PCB Layer Structures

Single-sided PCB

Copper tracks on one side of the insulating substrate.

Double-sided PCB

Conductive tracks on both sides, connected using vias or plated-through holes.

Multilayer PCB

Several copper layers separated by insulating dielectric materials.

5. PCB Inspection

Visual inspection checklist

  • Check board identification and revision.
  • Inspect component placement and orientation.
  • Verify polarity of diodes, LEDs and electrolytic capacitors.
  • Look for solder bridges between adjacent pads.
  • Check for missing, tilted or tombstoned SMD components.
  • Inspect connectors, vias and PCB edges.
  • Look for burnt tracks, damaged pads and discolouration.

6. Soldering Fundamentals

Soldering joins conductive terminals using a molten solder alloy. A good joint provides reliable electrical continuity and mechanical attachment.

Basic soldering process

  1. Clean the PCB pad and component lead.
  2. Apply suitable flux where needed.
  3. Heat the pad and terminal together using the soldering iron tip.
  4. Feed solder into the heated joint, not merely onto the iron tip.
  5. Allow solder to flow around the joint.
  6. Remove solder wire, then remove the iron.
  7. Allow the joint to cool without movement.
  8. Inspect the joint for proper wetting, shape and bridging.
Choose tip geometry and temperature based on the solder alloy, component thermal mass, PCB construction and manufacturer's guidance. Avoid prolonged heating that can lift pads or damage components.

9. Through-Hole Component Soldering

Desoldering procedure

  1. Switch off and disconnect the PCB from all power sources.
  2. Apply suitable flux to the joint.
  3. Heat the solder joint until the solder melts fully.
  4. Use a desoldering pump or braid to remove solder.
  5. Confirm that the lead is free before removing the component.
  6. Avoid pulling against a joint that is still attached.
Excessive force or prolonged heating can damage plated-through holes, pads and internal copper connections.

10. SMD Soldering and Rework

Common SMD packages

  • Chip resistors and capacitors.
  • SOT-23 and other small transistor packages.
  • SOIC and TSSOP integrated circuits.
  • QFP and QFN packages.
  • BGA packages, generally requiring specialized equipment and inspection.

Basic SMD rework procedure

  1. Identify the component and confirm its replacement specification.
  2. Protect nearby heat-sensitive components.
  3. Apply appropriate flux.
  4. Use controlled hot air and suitable nozzle geometry.
  5. Remove the component only after the solder has reflowed.
  6. Clean and inspect the pads.
  7. Position the replacement with correct orientation.
  8. Reflow using a suitable temperature profile.
  9. Inspect for bridges, alignment and damaged pads.
Use component and PCB manufacturer thermal-profile guidance. Avoid treating one hot-air temperature as suitable for every package, board or solder alloy.

12. ESD Protection

Electrostatic discharge (ESD) is a sudden transfer of electric charge between objects at different electrical potentials. It can damage sensitive semiconductor devices.

ESD wrist strap

Provides a controlled path for static charge equalization when correctly connected to an approved ESD grounding system.

ESD mat

Provides a controlled dissipative work surface.

ESD-safe packaging

Protects sensitive components during storage and transport.

ESD-safe handling

Reduces direct contact with sensitive terminals and maintains a suitable work environment.

Follow the facility's ESD-control procedure. A wrist strap must never be used as protection when working on energized mains circuits. Disconnect power before handling exposed circuitry.

13. PCB Continuity and Short-Circuit Testing

PCB testing verifies electrical connectivity and detects unintended connections.

Continuity testing

  1. Disconnect all power.
  2. Identify the two points that should be connected using the schematic or PCB layout.
  3. Verify that the circuit is discharged.
  4. Measure resistance or continuity between the points.
  5. Compare with expected connectivity.

Short-circuit testing

Check supply rails against ground and adjacent nets. Interpret readings carefully because capacitors may initially charge from the meter and semiconductor paths can create low or directional resistance readings.

A continuity beep does not automatically mean a short circuit. Some valid circuit paths, coils and semiconductor junctions can produce continuity indications.

14. PCB Track and Pad Repair

PCB repair may involve restoring a broken electrical connection or replacing a damaged pad. Repair methods must maintain electrical performance and mechanical reliability.

  • Identify the damaged net from the schematic or PCB layout.
  • Determine whether the track is a signal, power or high-current path.
  • Remove contamination and inspect the extent of damage.
  • Use a suitable jumper wire or approved repair method when permitted.
  • Provide mechanical support and insulation as required.
  • Verify continuity and check for adjacent-net shorts.
  • Perform functional testing under the required operating conditions.
Do not use a thin jumper wire to replace a high-current, safety-critical or controlled-impedance PCB trace without an engineering-approved repair specification.

16. Practical Lab: PCB Inspection and Rework

Objective: Inspect a low-voltage training PCB, identify solder defects, perform an approved repair and verify its electrical operation.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage PCB training board.
  • Temperature-controlled soldering station.
  • Solder wire and suitable flux.
  • Desoldering pump or solder wick.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Magnifier or inspection microscope.
  • ESD-safe workstation.
  • Fume extraction.

Procedure

  1. Disconnect the board from power.
  2. Inspect the board under good lighting.
  3. Identify solder bridges, poor joints or damaged tracks.
  4. Record the suspected defect and expected electrical connection.
  5. Apply the appropriate rework method.
  6. Inspect the repaired joint under magnification.
  7. Perform unpowered continuity and adjacent-net checks.
  8. Reconnect a current-limited low-voltage supply.
  9. Measure supply voltage and functional outputs.
  10. Record the final inspection and test result.

18. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is a PCB?
A printed circuit board mechanically supports electronic components and electrically connects them through copper tracks, pads and planes.
2. What is the difference between THT and SMT?
THT component leads pass through PCB holes, while SMT components are mounted directly onto surface pads.
3. What is solder bridging?
An unintended solder connection between adjacent pads or terminals that can create a short circuit.
4. What is flux used for?
Flux helps remove surface oxides and promotes proper solder wetting.
5. What is a cold solder joint?
A joint with inadequate wetting or bonding, potentially caused by poor heating, contamination or movement during solidification.
6. What is ESD?
Electrostatic discharge is a sudden transfer of charge that can damage sensitive electronic components.
7. What is a via?
A conductive connection between different copper layers of a PCB.
8. Why is excessive soldering heat dangerous?
It can damage components, lift copper pads, weaken PCB laminate and damage plated-through holes.
9. What is the purpose of ICT?
In-circuit testing checks component and connection characteristics using dedicated test points and fixtures.
10. What should be checked after PCB rework?
Inspect the joint, verify continuity, check for shorts, confirm component orientation and perform functional testing.

19. Practical and Interview Exercises

  1. Explain how you would identify a solder bridge on a PCB.
  2. Describe the procedure for removing a through-hole resistor without damaging the plated hole.
  3. Why should a PCB be checked for shorts before applying power?
  4. Explain how an ESD wrist strap and mat protect sensitive components.
  5. Differentiate between a solder bridge, open joint and lifted pad.
  6. Explain how you would diagnose an LED circuit that does not operate after PCB assembly.
  7. Describe the difference between continuity testing, ICT and functional testing.
  8. Prepare a simple PCB inspection report for a board that failed functional testing.

20. Key Takeaways

  • Understand PCB layers, pads, tracks and vias.
  • Differentiate through-hole and surface-mount assembly.
  • Inspect solder joints for bridging, poor wetting and insufficient solder.
  • Use controlled soldering and desoldering methods.
  • Follow ESD and fume-control procedures.
  • Check continuity and adjacent-net isolation before powering a repaired board.
  • Complete functional verification and document the repair.
