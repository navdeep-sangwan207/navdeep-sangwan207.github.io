BJT Testing Using a Multimeter

NPN and PNP identification, junction testing, terminal verification and practical transistor fault diagnosis.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the three terminals of a bipolar junction transistor.
  • Distinguish NPN and PNP transistors using their circuit symbols and junction behaviour.
  • Test base-emitter and base-collector junctions using DMM diode mode.
  • Identify possible open, shorted and leaky transistor faults.
  • Understand why collector and emitter identification requires additional information.
  • Use datasheets and practical measurements to verify a transistor.

2. BJT Construction and Terminals

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a three-terminal semiconductor device used for signal amplification and electronic switching.

Base (B)

A thin, lightly doped control region between emitter and collector.

Collector (C)

Collects carriers and is generally designed to dissipate significant power in many transistor types.

Emitter (E)

Heavily doped region that injects carriers into the base region.

NPN and PNP symbols

Remember: NPN arrow points out; PNP arrow points in. This mnemonic identifies the transistor type, not the physical pin arrangement.

3. Equivalent Junction Model

A BJT can be viewed as two PN junctions sharing the base region. This model is useful for basic multimeter testing.

NPN transistor

The base is P-type. The emitter and collector are N-type. With the red probe on the base, both base junctions may show forward conduction when the black probe touches emitter or collector.

PNP transistor

The base is N-type. The emitter and collector are P-type. With the black probe on the base, both base junctions may show forward conduction when the red probe touches emitter or collector.

A BJT is not literally two independent diodes. The equivalent-junction model is useful for basic junction checks, but it cannot fully verify transistor gain, leakage, switching speed or high-current performance.

6. Identifying the Base, Collector and Emitter

The base can often be identified by finding the terminal that forms a forward-biased PN junction with both of the other terminals under the appropriate probe polarity.

General identification process

  1. Remove power from the circuit and discharge capacitors.
  2. Identify the package marking and locate its datasheet if available.
  3. Test terminal pairs in diode mode while recording probe polarity and readings.
  4. Find the common terminal that forms two forward junctions: this is the base candidate.
  5. Determine NPN or PNP from the required forward-test probe polarity.
  6. Use the datasheet or a transistor tester to distinguish collector and emitter reliably.
Important limitation: The two base junctions may have slightly different forward voltages, but this is not a dependable universal method of distinguishing collector from emitter. Use the datasheet pinout. Some transistor packages have different pin arrangements even when they look identical.

Package examples

TO-92

Small plastic package. Pin order varies significantly between part numbers and manufacturers.

TO-220

Power transistor package. Tab connection may be collector, drain or another terminal depending on the device.

TO-3

Metal-can package used by some power transistors. Consult the exact datasheet for terminal identification.

8. Checking Collector–Emitter Leakage and Short Circuit

With a transistor removed from the circuit, check the collector-emitter pair in both probe directions. A simple healthy transistor usually shows no strong conduction between collector and emitter when the base is left unconnected.

Possible short

A very low reading in both directions can indicate collector-emitter short circuit.

Possible leakage

Unexpected conduction may indicate leakage, contamination, damage or a device-specific internal path.

Open junction

Missing expected base junction conduction can indicate an open junction or poor probe contact.

A DMM diode test cannot reliably measure collector-emitter leakage at rated voltage or determine breakdown voltage. Use a suitable transistor tester or controlled test circuit for those parameters.

9. hFE / DC Current Gain Measurement

Some multimeters include an hFE socket for small-signal BJT gain measurement. The transistor is inserted into the correct NPN or PNP socket with the correct terminal arrangement.

\[ h_{FE}=\frac{I_C}{I_B} \]

Where \(I_C\) is collector current and \(I_B\) is base current under the measurement conditions.

The displayed hFE value is measured at a particular test current and collector-emitter voltage. It can differ from the datasheet value at another operating point. Do not use an hFE reading alone to approve a power transistor.

Example

If collector current is 12 mA and base current is 0.1 mA:

\[ h_{FE}=\frac{12}{0.1}=120 \]

The approximate DC current gain is 120 at those test conditions.

10. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Identify and test one NPN and one PNP transistor using a digital multimeter.

Required equipment

  • Digital multimeter with diode-test mode
  • One NPN and one PNP transistor with known part numbers
  • Datasheets for both transistors
  • Breadboard and test leads
  • Optional transistor tester with hFE socket

Procedure

  1. Record each transistor's part number and datasheet pinout.
  2. Identify the package terminals using the datasheet.
  3. Set the DMM to diode-test mode.
  4. Measure both base junctions in forward and reverse directions.
  5. Measure collector-emitter in both directions.
  6. Record all readings and compare them with expected behaviour.
  7. If the DMM supports hFE measurement, record the gain and meter test conditions.
  8. Classify each device as apparently healthy or requiring further testing.
Do not test an energized transistor circuit in diode mode. Remove the transistor or isolate its terminals when other components can affect readings.

11. Common BJT Faults and Troubleshooting

FaultPossible symptomChecks
Base-emitter openMissing expected base-emitter forward reading; transistor may not turn onCheck base-emitter junction and solder connections
Base-collector openMissing expected base-collector junction readingVerify probe polarity and isolate the transistor
Collector-emitter shortLow resistance or voltage drop between C and ETest both directions with base disconnected
Collector-emitter leakageUnexpected conduction or heatingUse a suitable leakage test at controlled voltage
Wrong pin connectionUnexpected operation, low gain or heatingConfirm the exact manufacturer pinout
Thermal damageGain reduction, leakage or short circuitCheck current, power dissipation and heatsinking

Field troubleshooting workflow

  1. Inspect transistor package and PCB for physical damage.
  2. Verify the transistor part number and pin configuration.
  3. Check supply voltage and surrounding bias resistors.
  4. Power down, discharge and isolate the transistor.
  5. Perform base junction and collector-emitter tests.
  6. Use an appropriate gain or leakage test if the junction checks pass but the circuit still fails.
  7. Replace only after confirming the fault and checking the cause of failure.

12. Worked Numerical Problems

Example 1: Current gain

A transistor has a base current of 40 µA and collector current of 4 mA. Calculate hFE.

\[ h_{FE}=\frac{4\,mA}{40\,\mu A}=100 \]

Its approximate DC current gain is 100 at the stated operating point.

Example 2: Base current

A transistor must conduct 60 mA with a forced beta of 10. Estimate the base current.

\[ I_B=\frac{I_C}{10}=\frac{60\,mA}{10}=6\,mA \]

This is a simple switching-drive estimate. Verify the transistor's saturation characteristics and base-emitter limits.

Example 3: Base resistor

A 5 V logic output drives an NPN transistor with an assumed base-emitter voltage of 0.7 V. If the required base current is 5 mA, calculate the base resistor.

\[ R_B=\frac{5-0.7}{0.005}=860\ \Omega \]

An appropriate standard resistor may be selected after checking the logic output's current capability and the transistor's requirements.

13. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What are the three terminals of a BJT?
Base, collector and emitter.
2. How do you identify an NPN transistor using diode mode?
The base commonly behaves as the common anode of two junctions: red probe on base and black probe on emitter or collector gives forward readings.
3. How do you identify a PNP transistor using diode mode?
The base commonly behaves as the common cathode: black probe on base and red probe on emitter or collector gives forward readings.
4. What is the typical silicon BJT junction forward voltage?
Often around 0.5–0.8 V under a multimeter's diode-test conditions, depending on device and current.
5. Can a multimeter reliably distinguish collector and emitter?
Not reliably for every transistor. The datasheet pinout or a suitable transistor tester should be used.
6. What does hFE represent?
The DC common-emitter current gain, approximately collector current divided by base current.
7. What indicates a shorted transistor?
Unexpected very low resistance or diode drop between terminals, especially collector and emitter. Confirm with the device isolated.
8. Why should a transistor be removed or isolated before testing?
Other circuit components and parallel paths can alter the measured values.
9. Does a correct diode test prove that a BJT is fully healthy?
No. It checks basic junction behaviour, not gain at the required operating point, leakage at rated voltage, switching performance or thermal reliability.
10. What is the difference between NPN and PNP emitter arrows?
NPN arrow points outward; PNP arrow points inward. The arrow indicates conventional emitter current direction.

16. Key Takeaways

  • A BJT has base, collector and emitter terminals.
  • For typical NPN junction testing, red goes to base; for PNP, black goes to base.
  • Both base junctions should show forward conduction in the appropriate probe direction.
  • Collector-emitter should not show a strong short-like conduction path in a basic isolated test.
  • Use the exact datasheet to confirm pin configuration and device ratings.
  • A DMM junction test is a first-level diagnostic, not a complete performance test.
