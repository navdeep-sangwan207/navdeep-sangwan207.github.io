Operational Amplifier Circuit Testing

Practical verification of op-amp circuits using a digital multimeter, function generator and oscilloscope.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Identify op-amp IC packages, pin numbers and supply terminals.
  • Verify positive and negative supply voltages.
  • Test inverting and non-inverting amplifier circuits.
  • Measure closed-loop voltage gain.
  • Test comparator and zero-crossing detector operation.
  • Identify output saturation and input common-mode limitations.
  • Observe slew-rate limitation and clipping on an oscilloscope.
  • Diagnose oscillation, offset, incorrect gain and output faults.

2. Operational Amplifier Fundamentals

An operational amplifier is a high-gain differential amplifier. Its output depends on the voltage difference between its non-inverting and inverting inputs, subject to supply and operating limitations.

\[ V_{out}=A_{OL}(V_+-V_-) \]

In a closed-loop amplifier, negative feedback controls the gain and operating behavior.

Non-inverting input (+)

The input signal is applied here in a non-inverting configuration.

Inverting input (−)

The input signal or feedback network is connected here in an inverting configuration.

Output

Provides the amplified or conditioned signal within output limits.

Power pins

Provide the positive and negative supply rails, or a single supply as supported by the IC.

3. Identifying the IC and Pin Configuration

The classic LM741 is an eight-pin op-amp. Its common DIP-8 pin arrangement is:

PinLM741 function
1Offset null
2Inverting input
3Non-inverting input
4Negative supply (V−)
5Offset null
6Output
7Positive supply (V+)
8No connection (NC)
This pinout is for the LM741 only. Other op-amps, including LM358, LM324 and modern rail-to-rail devices, have different pin arrangements and supply requirements. Verify the exact part number and package datasheet before connecting power.

4. Power Supply Verification

Before testing signal behavior, verify that the op-amp receives the correct supply voltage.

Dual-supply operation

A classic LM741 commonly operates from positive and negative supply rails. For example, a suitable dual supply may be +12 V and −12 V, subject to the datasheet limits.

Single-supply operation

Some op-amps support single-supply operation. The input common-mode range and output swing must still be checked. A signal may need a reference or virtual ground to operate correctly.

Practical test

  1. Switch OFF the circuit and inspect the IC orientation.
  2. Verify the supply polarity and voltage ratings from the datasheet.
  3. Apply a current-limited supply.
  4. Measure each supply pin relative to circuit ground.
  5. Check for unexpected current consumption or heating.
  6. Only then apply the test signal.
A classic LM741 is not a rail-to-rail op-amp and is generally unsuitable for operation directly from a 5 V single supply. Select an op-amp whose supply range and input/output ranges suit the application.

5. Inverting Amplifier Testing

The inverting amplifier produces an output with opposite polarity to the input signal.

\[ A_v=-\frac{R_f}{R_{in}} \] \[ V_{out}=-\frac{R_f}{R_{in}}V_{in} \]

Testing procedure

  1. Verify the resistor values using a DMM with power OFF.
  2. Apply a small sinusoidal input signal from a function generator.
  3. Measure input and output amplitudes using an oscilloscope.
  4. Check that the output is inverted relative to the input.
  5. Calculate measured gain and compare with theoretical gain.

6. Non-Inverting Amplifier Testing

The output has the same polarity as the input.

\[ A_v=1+\frac{R_f}{R_g} \] \[ V_{out}=A_vV_{in} \]

Testing procedure

  1. Verify feedback resistor connections.
  2. Apply a low-amplitude input signal.
  3. Observe input and output waveforms simultaneously.
  4. Measure the output-to-input amplitude ratio.
  5. Check for clipping and distortion as input amplitude increases.
A correctly operating non-inverting amplifier produces an output in phase with the input, as long as the circuit remains within its linear operating limits.

8. Comparator Circuit Testing

An op-amp used open-loop as a comparator switches its output toward one of its output limits depending on the relative input voltages.

\[ V_+>V_- \Rightarrow V_{out}\text{ moves toward positive output limit} \] \[ V_+The actual output levels depend on the op-amp supply, output-stage architecture, load and datasheet specifications.

Practical test

  1. Verify the op-amp supply connections.
  2. Apply a reference voltage to one input.
  3. Apply a slowly varying test voltage to the other input.
  4. Observe the output transition as the input crosses the reference.
  5. Measure the switching threshold and output voltage limits.
An op-amp is not always an ideal substitute for a dedicated comparator. Check input common-mode range, recovery from saturation, output behavior and switching-speed requirements.

10. Output Saturation and Clipping

An op-amp output cannot exceed its specified output swing. When the demanded output exceeds the available range, the waveform clips.

  • Output clipping may occur due to excessive input amplitude or gain.
  • The output swing may not reach either supply rail.
  • Output current capability and load impedance affect the achievable swing.
  • Input common-mode range can also be exceeded before output clipping is visible.

Example

A non-inverting amplifier has a gain of 11 and receives a 1 V peak input. The ideal output demand is 11 V peak. If the available output swing is only ±10 V, the output cannot reproduce the requested waveform without clipping.

11. Slew Rate Testing

Slew rate is the maximum rate of change of the output voltage.

\[ SR=\max\left|\frac{dV_{out}}{dt}\right| \]

It is commonly specified in V/µs.

For a sinusoidal output, the approximate maximum frequency for undistorted full-amplitude operation due to slew rate is:

\[ SR\geq2\pi fV_{p} \]

Where \(V_p\) is the output peak amplitude.

Practical observation

  1. Configure a suitable closed-loop amplifier.
  2. Apply a sine wave and gradually increase frequency or amplitude.
  3. Observe the output for increasing distortion.
  4. At high enough slew demand, the output may become approximately triangular.
  5. Estimate slew rate from the steepest output slope.

13. Oscilloscope Testing

An oscilloscope is essential for checking signal amplitude, phase, clipping, oscillation, distortion and transient response.

Recommended measurement sequence

  1. Use a function generator to provide a known input signal.
  2. Set the oscilloscope probe attenuation correctly.
  3. Connect channel 1 to the input and channel 2 to the output.
  4. Use a safe common ground reference.
  5. Set the time base to display several signal cycles.
  6. Adjust vertical scales to measure both signals accurately.
  7. Compare amplitude, phase and waveform shape.
  8. Increase input frequency or amplitude gradually to examine limitations.
The oscilloscope ground clip is normally earth-referenced. Connect it only to an appropriate circuit reference. Never attach it to an unknown live node or mains-referenced circuit. Use an appropriately rated differential probe or isolation arrangement when required.

16. Practical Lab Record

Objective: Verify the gain and output behavior of an op-amp amplifier using a function generator and oscilloscope.

Equipment

  • Suitable op-amp IC and datasheet.
  • Current-limited, correctly rated power supply.
  • Precision resistors for feedback and input networks.
  • Function generator.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Two-channel oscilloscope and correctly rated probes.
  • Breadboard or suitable PCB test setup.

Procedure

  1. Identify the op-amp and verify its pin configuration.
  2. Assemble an inverting amplifier with a known feedback ratio.
  3. Verify supply voltage and polarity.
  4. Apply a low-amplitude sine wave.
  5. Measure input and output peak-to-peak voltage.
  6. Calculate the experimental gain and compare it with theory.
  7. Observe phase inversion.
  8. Repeat with a non-inverting configuration.
  9. Increase signal frequency and amplitude gradually to observe bandwidth and slew-rate limitations.

17. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is an operational amplifier?
It is a high-gain differential amplifier whose behavior is commonly controlled by external feedback components.
2. What is the difference between inverting and non-inverting amplifiers?
The inverting amplifier produces an output with opposite polarity; the non-inverting amplifier produces an output in phase with the input.
3. What is the virtual ground concept?
In a suitable negative-feedback circuit, the inverting input may be held near the non-inverting input voltage without being physically connected to ground.
4. Why does an op-amp output saturate?
The demanded output exceeds the available output swing or the circuit is driven outside its linear operating conditions.
5. What is slew rate?
It is the maximum rate of change of output voltage, usually expressed in V/µs.
6. What is input offset voltage?
It is the small differential input voltage required to make the output zero under specified conditions.
7. Why can an op-amp oscillate?
Unwanted phase shift and gain in the feedback loop can reduce stability and create oscillation.
8. What is the purpose of negative feedback?
It controls closed-loop gain and can improve linearity, bandwidth and stability when the circuit is properly designed.
9. Why must input common-mode range be checked?
An input voltage outside the permitted common-mode range can cause incorrect operation even when the supply voltage is correct.
10. Why is a dedicated comparator sometimes preferred?
A comparator is designed for switching and may offer faster response, better saturation recovery and a suitable output stage.

18. Numerical and Practical Exercises

  1. An inverting amplifier has Rf = 100 kΩ and Rin = 10 kΩ. Calculate voltage gain.
  2. A non-inverting amplifier has Rf = 90 kΩ and Rg = 10 kΩ. Calculate gain.
  3. A non-inverting amplifier has a gain of 6 and receives a 0.5 V peak input. Calculate ideal output peak voltage.
  4. An op-amp has a slew rate of 0.5 V/µs. Calculate the maximum sine-wave frequency for 5 V peak output based on slew rate alone.
  5. Explain why an op-amp supplied from ±12 V may not produce a ±12 V output.
  6. Describe how you would distinguish output saturation from a missing supply rail.
  7. Explain how to measure phase inversion using a two-channel oscilloscope.

19. Key Takeaways

  • Verify the exact op-amp pinout and supply limits before testing.
  • Measure supply rails before applying signals.
  • Compare measured closed-loop gain with resistor-based calculations.
  • Check input common-mode range and output swing.
  • Use an oscilloscope to identify clipping, oscillation, phase and slew-rate limitation.
  • Check feedback, bypassing, load and grounding when troubleshooting.
  • Use a dedicated comparator where the application requires reliable high-speed switching.
