Diode and Zener Diode Testing Using a Multimeter

Component identification, forward/reverse bias measurement, Zener breakdown and practical fault diagnosis.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Identify diode anode and cathode from package markings and circuit symbols.
  • Understand forward bias, reverse bias and reverse breakdown.
  • Test a conventional diode using the DMM diode-test mode.
  • Distinguish open, shorted and leaky diode faults.
  • Test a Zener diode and understand why an ordinary DMM may not show its Zener voltage.
  • Apply practical troubleshooting procedures to rectifier and protection circuits.

2. Diode Identification and Polarity

A semiconductor diode is a two-terminal device that permits substantial current in its forward direction and blocks current in its reverse direction, within its specified ratings.

Anode (A)

The P-type side of the diode. In the conventional forward-biased condition, current enters the anode.

Cathode (K)

The N-type side. A stripe or band on many axial diodes marks the cathode.

Forward bias

Anode is positive relative to cathode. The diode conducts when sufficient forward voltage is applied.

Reverse bias

Cathode is positive relative to anode. Only a small leakage current flows below breakdown.

Common diode packages

Identification rule: On many cylindrical rectifier and Zener diodes, the band indicates the cathode. Always confirm the part number and datasheet; package markings are not universal.

Diode circuit symbol

3. Forward and Reverse Bias Operation

Forward bias

When the anode is sufficiently positive relative to the cathode, the depletion region narrows and the diode conducts. A silicon diode commonly has a forward voltage around 0.6–0.8 V at a moderate test current, but the actual value depends on current, temperature and device construction.

Reverse bias

When the cathode is positive relative to the anode, the depletion region widens. The diode blocks current until reverse breakdown, provided its rating is not exceeded.

Forward bias: conventional current flows from anode to cathode after the forward voltage is sufficient.

4. DMM Diode-Test Mode

In diode-test mode, a digital multimeter applies a small, controlled test current and displays the voltage drop across the device. This is not the same as measuring resistance.

Typical silicon diode reading

Forward reading

Often approximately 0.5–0.8 V for a silicon PN diode under the meter's test conditions.

Reverse reading

Usually OL or an over-range indication for a healthy diode when tested in circuit isolation.

OL both directions

Possible open diode, poor probe contact or an unsuitable meter range.

Near zero both directions

Possible shorted diode. Confirm after isolating it from parallel circuit paths.

Important: A diode reading is not a fixed universal pass/fail value. Check the datasheet and the multimeter's test current and open-circuit voltage.

5. Practical Procedure: Testing a Standard Diode

  1. Switch off the circuit and disconnect it from the power source.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely using an appropriate resistor and verify that voltage is absent.
  3. Identify the diode's anode and cathode.
  4. For an accurate isolated test, desolder or lift one diode lead from the circuit.
  5. Select diode-test mode on the DMM.
  6. Connect the red probe to the anode and black probe to the cathode.
  7. Record the forward voltage reading.
  8. Reverse the probes and record the reverse reading.
  9. Compare both readings with the device type and datasheet expectations.

7. Zener Diode Fundamentals

A Zener diode is designed to operate in reverse breakdown within a specified current range. It is commonly used for voltage references, shunt regulation and overvoltage protection.

Forward direction

Behaves broadly like a conventional silicon diode, with a forward drop often around 0.6–0.8 V.

Reverse direction below breakdown

Blocks current except for small leakage current.

Reverse breakdown

Maintains an approximately specified voltage over its intended operating-current range.

Zener regulator circuit

Conceptual shunt-regulator diagram: the Zener is reverse biased across the load, with a series resistor limiting current. Verify the exact symbol and connections before building a circuit.

Why a normal DMM diode test may not measure Zener voltage

The DMM's diode-test mode usually does not provide the voltage and controlled operating current required to drive a Zener diode into reverse breakdown. A reverse test may simply display OL. This does not prove that the Zener is defective.

Never connect a Zener directly across a power supply without current limiting. Excess current can destroy the component.

8. Testing a Zener Diode Correctly

To verify the Zener breakdown voltage, use a current-limited test circuit or a suitable semiconductor tester.

Basic test circuit

Test procedure

  1. Read the Zener part number and obtain its datasheet.
  2. Determine the nominal Zener voltage and recommended test current.
  3. Calculate a suitable series resistor for the test supply.
  4. Connect the Zener in reverse bias with a current-limiting resistor.
  5. Use a voltmeter across the Zener and an ammeter or calculated resistor current to verify operating current.
  6. Measure the voltage only after confirming that current and power remain within ratings.

Series resistor calculation

\[ R_S=\frac{V_S-V_Z}{I_Z} \]

Where \(V_S\) is the test supply, \(V_Z\) is the expected Zener voltage and \(I_Z\) is the chosen test current.

Resistor power:

\[ P_R=I_Z^2R_S \]

Zener power:

\[ P_Z=V_ZI_Z \]

10. Common Diode Faults and Troubleshooting

Observed symptomPossible causeRecommended action
No forward reading; OL both waysOpen diode, damaged bond or poor contactIsolate one lead and repeat the test
Near-zero reading in both directionsShorted diode or parallel circuit pathRemove one lead and retest
Unexpected low reverse resistanceLeakage, contamination or circuit loadingIsolate the diode; compare with datasheet
Rectifier output is lowOpen/shorted rectifier diode, transformer fault or capacitor issueTest each diode and measure AC/DC stages
Zener output is not regulatedIncorrect Zener, insufficient current, open resistor or overloadCheck Zener voltage, current and load conditions
Diode overheatsExcess forward current, reverse stress or inadequate thermal designCheck current, power and thermal ratings

Practical troubleshooting sequence

  1. Confirm the symptom and inspect the PCB for cracks, burns and solder defects.
  2. Switch off power and safely discharge capacitors.
  3. Identify the suspect diode and confirm its marking.
  4. Perform forward and reverse diode tests.
  5. Isolate one lead if other circuit components affect the reading.
  6. For a Zener, test reverse breakdown using a current-limited supply.
  7. Check the series resistor, load and power-supply rails before replacing parts.
  8. After repair, power up using an appropriate current-limited source and verify operation.

11. Worked Numerical Problems

Example 1: Zener series resistor

A 5.1 V Zener is tested from a 12 V supply at 10 mA. Find the ideal series resistor.

\[ R_S=\frac{12-5.1}{0.01}=690\ \Omega \]

Resistor power:

\[ P_R=(0.01)^2(690)=0.069\ W \]

Zener power:

\[ P_Z=5.1(0.01)=0.051\ W \]
Ideal calculation: 690 Ω. Select a suitable standard resistor with adequate power rating and tolerance after checking the datasheet and worst-case conditions.

Example 2: Forward diode power

A silicon diode has a forward voltage of 0.72 V at 100 mA. Estimate its forward power dissipation.

\[ P_D=V_FI_F=0.72\times0.1=0.072\ W \]

The estimated dissipation is 72 mW. Confirm the component's thermal conditions and maximum ratings.

Example 3: Zener current

A 9 V supply feeds a 5.1 V Zener through a 390 Ω resistor. Ignore load current. Estimate the current.

\[ I_Z=\frac{9-5.1}{390}=0.01\ A=10\ mA \]

Estimated Zener power is \(5.1\times0.01=51\ mW\).

13. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is the function of a PN junction diode?
It conducts substantial current in forward bias and blocks current in reverse bias until breakdown, within its ratings.
2. How do you identify the cathode of a common axial diode?
A band or stripe commonly marks the cathode. Confirm with the part marking and datasheet.
3. What does a DMM diode-test mode measure?
It applies a small test current and displays the voltage drop across the device.
4. What readings suggest a shorted diode?
A very low voltage drop in both probe directions may indicate a short. Isolate one lead to rule out parallel paths.
5. Why can a healthy Zener show OL in reverse diode-test mode?
The DMM may not generate sufficient reverse voltage to reach the Zener breakdown region.
6. How is Zener breakdown voltage measured?
Apply reverse bias through a calculated current-limiting resistor and measure the voltage across the Zener at a controlled current.
7. Why is a series resistor required with a Zener regulator?
It limits current and protects the Zener from excessive power dissipation.
8. What is the difference between a normal diode and a Zener diode?
A normal rectifier diode is generally intended to block reverse voltage; a Zener is designed to operate in controlled reverse breakdown at its specified voltage and current.
9. Why should a diode be isolated before testing?
Parallel components and other circuit paths can affect the measured value and produce misleading results.
10. What is the power dissipation of a Zener diode?
Approximately \(P_Z=V_ZI_Z\). The result must remain within the derated power rating.

16. Key Takeaways

  • The cathode band identifies the cathode on many common axial diodes, but the datasheet is authoritative.
  • Use diode-test mode to measure forward voltage and check reverse blocking.
  • Isolate a diode when in-circuit paths could affect the result.
  • Do not assume a reverse OL reading means a Zener diode is faulty.
  • Test Zener breakdown using a current-limited supply and a correctly calculated resistor.
  • Always check current, voltage, power and thermal ratings before applying power.
