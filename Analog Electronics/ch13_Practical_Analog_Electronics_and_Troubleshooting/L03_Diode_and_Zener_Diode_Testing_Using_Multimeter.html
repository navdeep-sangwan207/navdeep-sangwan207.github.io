Lesson 3: Diode and Zener Diode Testing Using a Multimeter

Diode Identification, Forward and Reverse Bias Testing and Fault Diagnosis

1. Introduction

Diodes are semiconductor devices that allow current to flow primarily in one direction. They are widely used in rectifiers, voltage regulators, protection circuits, switching circuits and signal-processing applications.

A Zener diode is a specially designed diode that operates in reverse breakdown within its specified current and power limits. It is commonly used for voltage regulation and overvoltage protection.

Learning Objective: Identify ordinary and Zener diodes, understand their terminals, perform forward and reverse tests using a digital multimeter, and identify common diode faults.

2. Basic Construction and Working of a Diode

A semiconductor diode is formed by joining P-type and N-type semiconductor materials. This creates a PN junction.

  • Anode: P-type terminal.
  • Cathode: N-type terminal.
  • Depletion region: Region around the junction where mobile charge carriers are depleted.

3. Diode Symbol and Identification

The diode symbol indicates the direction of conventional forward current. The vertical bar represents the cathode.

Most axial rectifier diodes have a band near the cathode terminal. Always verify the marking and package information against the manufacturer's datasheet.

Common Diodes

Diode Type Application
1N4001–1N4007 Rectifier diode family Power rectification
1N4148 Small-signal switching diode Signal switching
1N5819 Schottky diode Low-voltage rectification
LED Light-emitting diode Indication and lighting
Zener diode Voltage-reference diode Voltage regulation and clamping

4. Forward Bias Operation

A diode is forward biased when the anode is connected to a more positive potential than the cathode.

When the forward voltage is sufficient, the diode conducts current. A silicon diode commonly has a forward voltage drop around 0.6 V to 0.8 V under typical operating conditions.

The actual forward voltage depends on diode type, current and temperature.

5. Reverse Bias Operation

A diode is reverse biased when the cathode is connected to a more positive potential than the anode.

Under normal reverse-bias conditions, only a small leakage current flows. If the reverse voltage exceeds the diode's breakdown rating, the current may rise sharply and damage an ordinary diode unless it is externally limited.

Important: A standard multimeter diode test does not normally apply enough reverse voltage to test the rated reverse breakdown voltage of an ordinary diode.

6. Understanding Zener Diodes

A Zener diode is designed to operate in reverse breakdown and maintain an approximately constant voltage across it when operated within its specified current range.

Zener Diode Applications

  • Voltage reference circuits.
  • Simple shunt voltage regulators.
  • Overvoltage protection.
  • Signal clipping circuits.
  • Voltage clamping circuits.

Common Zener Diode Ratings

Parameter Meaning
VZ Nominal Zener voltage.
IZ Zener operating current.
IZK Current near the Zener knee region.
IZT Test current at which Zener voltage is specified.
PZ Maximum power dissipation.
ZZ Dynamic Zener impedance.

7. Introduction to Diode Testing Using a DMM

A digital multimeter's diode mode applies a small test current and measures the voltage across the device.

It is useful for checking whether a diode conducts in the forward direction and blocks in the reverse direction.

Preparation

  1. Switch OFF the circuit power.
  2. Disconnect the diode from the circuit or isolate one terminal if necessary.
  3. Discharge capacitors safely.
  4. Inspect the diode for physical damage.
  5. Identify the anode and cathode.
  6. Check the multimeter manual for its diode-test limits.

8. Testing an Ordinary Diode with a Multimeter

Step 1: Forward Bias Test

  1. Set the DMM to diode mode.
  2. Connect the red probe to the anode.
  3. Connect the black probe to the cathode.
  4. Observe the forward voltage reading.

A typical silicon PN junction may display approximately 0.5 V to 0.8 V, depending on the test current and device.

Step 2: Reverse Bias Test

  1. Connect the black probe to the anode.
  2. Connect the red probe to the cathode.
  3. Observe the display.

A healthy ordinary diode should normally display OL or an out-of-range indication in reverse bias, provided the multimeter's test voltage is below the diode's breakdown voltage.

9. Testing a Zener Diode with a Multimeter

A Zener diode can be tested in forward bias using the multimeter's diode mode, just like an ordinary silicon diode.

Forward Bias Test

  1. Select diode mode.
  2. Connect red probe to anode.
  3. Connect black probe to cathode.
  4. Observe the forward voltage.

Reverse Bias Test

  1. Reverse the probes.
  2. Observe the display.
  3. Note whether the meter displays OL.
Important: A normal DMM diode-test mode generally cannot verify the rated Zener breakdown voltage. An OL reading in reverse bias does not prove that the Zener voltage is correct.

How to Verify Zener Voltage

To measure the actual Zener voltage, use a suitable DC supply and a series current-limiting resistor.

10. Practical Zener Voltage Test Circuit

Consider a 5.1 V Zener diode connected in reverse bias with a series resistor.

Resistor Calculation

Assume:

  • Supply voltage = 12 V
  • Zener voltage = 5.1 V
  • Desired Zener current = 10 mA
  • Neglect load current for this example.

Using Ohm's law:

R = (VS - VZ) / IZ

R = (12 - 5.1) / 0.01

R = 6.9 / 0.01

R = 690 Ω

Select a suitable standard resistor value, such as 680 Ω or 750 Ω, after checking the actual current and power dissipation.

Resistor Power Calculation

PR = (VS - VZ) × IZ

PR = 6.9 × 0.01

PR = 0.069 W

A resistor with a suitably higher power rating should be selected, allowing for tolerance and operating conditions.

Measurement: Connect the multimeter in DC voltage mode across the Zener diode. A correctly designed circuit should produce a voltage close to its specified Zener voltage when the diode operates within its regulation region.

11. Understanding Diode Test Results

Forward Reading Reverse Reading Possible Condition
Normal forward voltage OL Normal diode behaviour.
Near 0 V Near 0 V Possible shorted diode.
OL OL Possible open diode or poor probe contact.
Unusually high or unstable Unusual reading Possible damage, wrong test conditions or in-circuit interference.

These are diagnostic indications, not absolute proof. Isolate the diode and repeat the test before concluding that the component is faulty.

12. Common Diode Faults

12.1 Open Diode

An open diode does not conduct in the expected forward direction. It may cause a rectifier or signal circuit to stop functioning.

12.2 Shorted Diode

A shorted diode may conduct in both directions with very low resistance. It can cause excessive current, blown fuses or power supply failure.

12.3 Leaky Diode

A leaky diode may allow excessive reverse current. A basic diode test may not reveal all leakage problems.

12.4 Incorrectly Connected Diode

Reversed diode polarity may prevent the intended circuit operation or cause component damage.

13. Applications of Diode Testing

  • Testing rectifier bridges in power supplies.
  • Checking reverse-polarity protection diodes.
  • Testing flyback diodes in relay driver circuits.
  • Checking Zener voltage regulator circuits.
  • Testing LEDs and indicator circuits.
  • Troubleshooting PCB-level faults.
  • Checking semiconductor components during maintenance.

14. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Test ordinary silicon diodes and Zener diodes using a digital multimeter and verify Zener breakdown voltage using a current-limited test circuit.

Required Components and Equipment

  • Digital multimeter with diode mode.
  • 1N4007 rectifier diode.
  • 1N4148 switching diode.
  • 5.1 V Zener diode.
  • 680 Ω resistor.
  • Suitable low-voltage DC supply.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

Experiment A: Ordinary Diode Test

  1. Identify the anode and cathode of the 1N4007 diode.
  2. Set the multimeter to diode mode.
  3. Measure forward voltage.
  4. Reverse the probes.
  5. Record both readings.

Experiment B: Zener Diode Test

  1. Identify the Zener diode terminals.
  2. Perform a forward-bias diode test.
  3. Perform a reverse-bias diode test.
  4. Observe that the multimeter may display OL in reverse bias.
  5. Build a suitable current-limited Zener test circuit.
  6. Measure the voltage across the Zener diode.

Observation Table

1N4148 ________ ________

Expected Result:

The ordinary diode and Zener diode are tested successfully. Their forward conduction behaviour is observed, and the Zener breakdown voltage is verified using a properly current-limited circuit.

16. Lesson Summary

  • A diode is a PN junction semiconductor device.
  • Forward bias allows current to flow through the diode.
  • Reverse bias normally blocks current except for leakage.
  • A Zener diode is designed to operate in reverse breakdown.
  • DMM diode mode checks forward junction behaviour.
  • A reverse OL reading does not verify the Zener breakdown voltage.
  • A current-limiting resistor is essential in a Zener test circuit.
  • Diode faults include open, shorted and leaky conditions.
