Function Generator Basics and Signal Testing

Generating, adjusting and verifying electrical test signals in analog circuits.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the working principle and applications of a function generator.
  • Identify sine, square, triangle and pulse waveforms.
  • Adjust frequency, amplitude, DC offset and duty cycle.
  • Understand frequency sweep and modulation.
  • Understand output impedance and load matching.
  • Verify generator settings using an oscilloscope.
  • Use generated signals to test amplifiers, filters, comparators and other analog circuits.
  • Diagnose common signal-generation and measurement errors.

2. What Is a Function Generator?

A function generator is an electronic test instrument that produces electrical waveforms with adjustable parameters. It is used to stimulate a circuit and observe its response.

Signal generation

Produces standard periodic waveforms for testing.

Frequency control

Generates signals over a specified frequency range.

Amplitude control

Adjusts the signal voltage level.

Offset control

Adds a DC voltage to the AC waveform.

Modulation

Varies a carrier signal according to another signal or control input.

Sweep

Changes frequency automatically over a specified range.

3. Types of Generated Waveforms

Sine wave

Used for amplifier gain, frequency response, filter and distortion tests.

Square wave

Used for switching response, rise time, transient and digital-interface tests.

Triangle wave

Used for integrator, comparator and waveform-shaping circuit tests.

Pulse wave

Used for pulse response, timing, triggering and switching tests.

6. Amplitude, Peak and Peak-to-Peak Voltage

For a zero-offset symmetrical sine wave:

\[ V_{pp}=2V_p \] \[ V_{RMS}=\frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{V_{pp}}{2\sqrt{2}} \]

For example, a sine wave of 4 Vpp has:

\[ V_p=2V,\quad V_{RMS}\approx1.414V \]

With a DC offset, the signal's instantaneous voltage range is:

\[ V_{max}=V_{offset}+V_p \] \[ V_{min}=V_{offset}-V_p \]
Function generator amplitude conventions vary. Check whether the display indicates a voltage for a 50 Ω termination or a high-impedance load. The voltage at the actual load may differ significantly.

8. DC Offset and Signal Clipping

DC offset shifts the complete waveform above or below the zero-voltage reference.

For a 4 Vpp sine wave with +1 V offset:

\[ V_p=2V \] \[ V_{max}=1+2=3V \] \[ V_{min}=1-2=-1V \]

If a circuit's input range is 0 V to 3.3 V, this signal exceeds the permitted negative input range. A suitable attenuator, bias network or protection interface may be required.

Before connecting a function generator to an IC or amplifier, check the permitted input voltage, common-mode range, absolute maximum ratings and power-supply requirements. Never assume that a signal is safe merely because its Vpp value is small.

9. Duty Cycle and Pulse Measurements

Duty cycle is the percentage of one period during which a periodic pulse is high.

\[ D=\frac{T_{ON}}{T}\times100\% \] \[ T=T_{ON}+T_{OFF} \]

For a 1 kHz signal with 25% duty cycle:

\[ T=\frac{1}{1000}=1ms \] \[ T_{ON}=0.25ms,\quad T_{OFF}=0.75ms \]
Some generators provide independent pulse-width and duty-cycle controls. Their limits may depend on frequency and minimum pulse width.

11. Frequency Sweep

A frequency sweep automatically changes signal frequency over a selected range. It is useful for evaluating amplifier and filter frequency response.

For a filter test, record input and output amplitudes at multiple frequencies. Calculate gain:

\[ A_v=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} \]

For voltage gain in decibels:

\[ A_v(dB)=20\log_{10}\left|\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\right| \]

12. Modulation Fundamentals

Amplitude modulation (AM)

The carrier amplitude changes according to the modulating signal.

Frequency modulation (FM)

The instantaneous carrier frequency changes according to the modulating signal.

Frequency-shift keying (FSK)

Digital data is represented by switching between discrete frequencies.

Pulse-width modulation (PWM)

Information is represented by changing the pulse width or duty cycle.

Modulation capabilities depend on the generator model. Basic function generators may not support all these modes.

13. Output Impedance and Load Matching

Many bench function generators have a nominal 50 Ω output impedance. Their voltage display may assume that the load is also 50 Ω.

Using a simplified Thevenin model:

\[ V_L=V_S\frac{R_L}{R_S+R_L} \]

where \(R_S\) is the generator's source resistance and \(R_L\) is the load resistance.

If the generator is represented by a 2 V open-circuit source with 50 Ω source resistance:

  • For a 50 Ω load, the load voltage is 1 V.
  • For a high-impedance load, the voltage approaches 2 V.
Actual instruments may calibrate their displayed amplitude for a selected 50 Ω or high-impedance termination. Check the output-load menu and instrument manual before calculating expected voltage.

15. Practical Lab: Testing an Amplifier

Objective: Apply a known input signal and measure the voltage gain of an amplifier.

Equipment

  • Function generator.
  • Oscilloscope with two probes.
  • Low-voltage amplifier trainer or correctly assembled amplifier.
  • Regulated DC power supply.
  • Digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Check the amplifier circuit and supply polarity before powering it.
  2. Set the function generator to sine wave, 1 kHz and a small amplitude such as 100 mVpp.
  3. Set the DC offset to zero unless the circuit requires a defined input bias.
  4. Connect the generator ground to the circuit's appropriate signal reference.
  5. Connect oscilloscope channel 1 to the amplifier input and channel 2 to the output.
  6. Verify the input amplitude and frequency before evaluating the output.
  7. Measure output peak-to-peak voltage without clipping.
  8. Calculate voltage gain and compare with the expected value.
  9. Increase frequency gradually to investigate bandwidth.
  10. Repeat at different input amplitudes to identify clipping or nonlinear distortion.

17. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is a function generator?
An electronic test instrument that generates signals such as sine, square, triangle and pulse waveforms for circuit testing.
2. What is the difference between an oscilloscope and a function generator?
A function generator produces an electrical test signal; an oscilloscope displays and measures electrical signals.
3. What is DC offset?
DC offset is a constant voltage added to an AC waveform, shifting its average level.
4. Define duty cycle.
The percentage of one signal period during which a pulse remains at its high level.
5. Why is a 50 Ω output impedance common?
It is a widely used transmission-line impedance that supports predictable signal transfer and termination in test systems.
6. What is frequency sweep?
Automatic variation of signal frequency over a selected range, commonly used for frequency-response testing.
7. What is the purpose of a triangle wave?
It is useful for testing integrators, comparators, waveform shapers and linearity.
8. What happens if amplitude is too high?
The circuit may clip, saturate, exceed input limits or suffer damage.
9. How do you measure amplifier gain?
Apply a known input signal and measure the output. Voltage gain is output voltage divided by input voltage, using consistent amplitude definitions.
10. Why verify a function generator using an oscilloscope?
To confirm actual amplitude, frequency, offset, waveform shape, duty cycle and possible distortion at the circuit input.

18. Numerical and Practical Exercises

  1. A sine wave has 6 Vpp. Calculate peak and RMS voltage for zero offset.
  2. A 2 kHz square wave has a duty cycle of 40%. Calculate period, ON time and OFF time.
  3. A 3 Vpp sine wave has a +1 V offset. Calculate its maximum and minimum instantaneous voltages.
  4. A 50 Ω source is represented by a 4 V open-circuit source. Calculate the voltage across a 50 Ω load.
  5. An amplifier receives 200 mVpp and produces 2 Vpp. Calculate voltage gain and gain in decibels.
  6. Explain how a function generator can be used to determine the cutoff frequency of an RC low-pass filter.
  7. Explain why a 50 Ω generator setting may produce a different voltage when connected to a high-impedance oscilloscope input.

19. Key Takeaways

  • A function generator provides controlled electrical signals for testing circuits.
  • Understand the difference between amplitude, peak, peak-to-peak and RMS voltage.
  • DC offset changes the waveform's average voltage.
  • Duty cycle controls the ON-time proportion of a pulse waveform.
  • Output impedance and load termination affect the measured amplitude.
  • Frequency sweep is valuable for amplifier and filter analysis.
  • Always verify actual input voltage using an oscilloscope before testing sensitive circuits.
