Oscilloscope Basics and Waveform Measurement

Practical waveform analysis using analog and digital storage oscilloscopes.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the working principle and controls of an oscilloscope.
  • Identify vertical, horizontal and trigger controls.
  • Use 1X and 10X probes correctly.
  • Perform probe compensation.
  • Measure amplitude, peak-to-peak voltage, time period and frequency.
  • Understand AC/DC coupling and trigger modes.
  • Measure phase difference between two signals.
  • Identify clipping, noise, distortion, ripple and transient faults.

2. What Is an Oscilloscope?

An oscilloscope is an electronic test instrument that displays electrical voltage as a function of time.

\[ V=f(t) \]

The horizontal axis represents time, while the vertical axis represents voltage.

Vertical system

Controls channel selection, volts per division, position and coupling.

Horizontal system

Controls time per division, horizontal position and acquisition time window.

Trigger system

Stabilizes repetitive waveforms by selecting a condition that starts acquisition or display.

Acquisition system

Samples and stores waveform data in a digital storage oscilloscope.

3. Basic Oscilloscope Display

Each major grid square is called a division. If the vertical scale is 2 V/div and a waveform occupies 3 vertical divisions peak-to-peak:

\[ V_{pp}=3\times2=6V \]

If one cycle occupies 4 horizontal divisions and the time base is 0.5 ms/div:

\[ T=4\times0.5=2ms \] \[ f=\frac{1}{T}=500Hz \]

5. Oscilloscope Probe Basics

A probe connects the test point to the oscilloscope input. Common passive probes offer 1X and 10X attenuation settings.

1X probe

Provides little attenuation but generally has higher capacitive loading and lower bandwidth.

10X probe

Attenuates the signal by approximately ten times, normally reducing circuit loading and increasing usable bandwidth.

When a 10X probe is selected, the oscilloscope channel probe factor must also be set to 10X. Otherwise the displayed voltage will be incorrect.

Probe compensation

Probe compensation matches the probe capacitance to the oscilloscope input. It is commonly checked using the oscilloscope's calibration square-wave output.

The sketches illustrate typical compensation shapes. Follow the specific probe and oscilloscope manufacturer's compensation procedure.

6. Voltage Measurement

Peak-to-peak voltage is the difference between maximum and minimum waveform voltage.

\[ V_{pp}=V_{max}-V_{min} \]

For a symmetrical sine wave centred at zero:

\[ V_p=\frac{V_{pp}}{2} \] \[ V_{RMS}=\frac{V_p}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{V_{pp}}{2\sqrt{2}} \]

These RMS relationships apply to a pure sinusoidal AC component, not arbitrary waveforms.

Example

A sine wave measures 4 vertical divisions peak-to-peak with a 2 V/div setting.

\[ V_{pp}=4\times2=8V \] \[ V_p=4V,\qquad V_{RMS}\approx2.828V \]

7. Time Period and Frequency Measurement

\[ T=(\text{horizontal divisions})\times(\text{time/div}) \] \[ f=\frac{1}{T} \]

Example

A waveform occupies 5 divisions at 200 µs/div.

\[ T=5\times200\mu s=1ms \] \[ f=1kHz \]

For example, when measuring a 12 V DC supply with 100 mV ripple, AC coupling can make the ripple easier to examine. Measure the actual DC level separately using DC coupling or a DMM.

10. Triggering and Stable Waveforms

Triggering determines when the oscilloscope begins displaying or acquiring a waveform.

  • Edge trigger: Triggers when the signal crosses a selected voltage level.
  • Rising edge: Triggers as the signal moves from lower to higher voltage.
  • Falling edge: Triggers as the signal moves from higher to lower voltage.
  • Trigger level: Selects the voltage threshold.
  • Auto mode: Typically maintains a display even without a valid trigger event.
  • Normal mode: Acquires when the specified trigger condition occurs.
  • Single mode: Captures one qualifying event and then stops.
For a repetitive sine wave, select an edge and trigger level near the middle of the signal's voltage range to obtain a stable display.

11. Two-Channel Phase Measurement

Two channels can display input and output signals simultaneously. The time difference between corresponding points can be used to calculate phase difference.

\[ \phi=\frac{\Delta t}{T}\times360^\circ \]

For a 1 kHz signal, the period is 1 ms. If the output lags by 0.25 ms:

\[ \phi=\frac{0.25}{1}\times360^\circ=90^\circ \]

The sign of phase difference depends on which waveform leads or lags.

13. Lissajous Patterns

Lissajous patterns are produced when two sinusoidal signals are applied to the horizontal and vertical inputs of an oscilloscope operating in X-Y mode.

  • Similar frequencies and zero phase difference can produce a straight diagonal line.
  • A 90° phase difference between equal-frequency sine waves can produce a circle when their amplitudes are equal and the axes are properly scaled.
  • Other phase relationships produce elliptical patterns.
  • Different frequency ratios can produce more complex stationary patterns.

14. Ripple Measurement in Power Supplies

Power-supply ripple is an unwanted periodic AC component superimposed on a DC output.

Measurement procedure

  1. Verify that the power supply is isolated and safe to test.
  2. Connect the oscilloscope ground to the appropriate circuit reference.
  3. Use DC coupling to observe the total DC level and ripple.
  4. Use AC coupling when needed to magnify the ripple waveform.
  5. Choose an appropriate vertical scale and time base.
  6. Measure ripple peak-to-peak voltage.
  7. Compare the result with the design specification.
Never connect an ordinary earth-grounded oscilloscope probe directly across a mains-live circuit. Use an appropriately rated differential probe and suitable safety procedures. For training, work with isolated, low-voltage circuits.

16. Practical Lab Experiment

Objective: Measure the voltage, frequency and phase relationship of sinusoidal signals using a two-channel oscilloscope.

Equipment

  • Digital storage oscilloscope.
  • Two suitable oscilloscope probes.
  • Function generator.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Low-voltage amplifier circuit or signal source.

Procedure

  1. Switch on the oscilloscope and inspect its probe settings.
  2. Connect the probe to the calibration output and compensate it.
  3. Generate a 1 kHz sine wave from the function generator.
  4. Display the waveform on channel 1.
  5. Measure peak-to-peak voltage and time period.
  6. Calculate frequency and compare with the generator setting.
  7. Connect a second signal to channel 2.
  8. Measure time difference between corresponding waveform points.
  9. Calculate phase difference.
  10. Repeat using a square wave and observe rise time and overshoot.

17. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is an oscilloscope?
An instrument that displays voltage as a function of time, allowing analysis of waveform shape and timing.
2. What is volts per division?
It is the vertical scale factor that specifies the voltage represented by one major vertical grid division.
3. What is time per division?
It is the horizontal scale factor that specifies the time represented by one major horizontal division.
4. Why is a 10X probe commonly used?
It generally reduces capacitive loading on the circuit and extends usable bandwidth, at the cost of attenuating the signal.
5. What is probe compensation?
It adjusts the probe's compensation network to match the oscilloscope input, ensuring accurate waveform reproduction.
6. What is the difference between AC and DC coupling?
DC coupling displays the DC component and AC variation. AC coupling blocks the DC component and displays the varying component within its frequency response.
7. What is triggering?
Triggering defines the condition used to start or stabilize waveform acquisition and display.
8. How do you calculate phase difference?
Measure the time shift between corresponding waveform points and use phase = (time shift / period) × 360°.
9. Why does a square wave look rounded?
Possible causes include limited bandwidth, probe capacitance, incorrect compensation and slow signal rise time.
10. Why must the oscilloscope ground connection be checked?
Many bench oscilloscopes have earth-referenced probe grounds. Incorrect grounding can short circuit nodes and create serious electrical hazards.

18. Numerical and Practical Exercises

  1. A waveform occupies 5 vertical divisions at 1 V/div. Calculate peak-to-peak voltage.
  2. A waveform occupies 4 horizontal divisions at 250 µs/div. Calculate frequency.
  3. A 10X probe is connected, but the oscilloscope channel is set to 1X. Explain the measurement error.
  4. A 2 kHz signal has a time shift of 125 µs between two channels. Calculate phase difference.
  5. Explain how to measure 50 mV ripple on a 15 V DC supply.
  6. Describe how probe compensation affects square-wave measurements.
  7. Explain why an earth-grounded oscilloscope should not be connected carelessly to a mains-live circuit.

19. Key Takeaways

  • Understand volts/div, time/div and trigger settings before measuring.
  • Match the oscilloscope probe factor with the channel setting.
  • Compensate passive probes before precision measurements.
  • Use DC coupling to measure total signal level and AC coupling to inspect small variations.
  • Calculate voltage, frequency and phase from measured grid divisions.
  • Use two channels to compare input and output signals.
  • Always consider probe loading, instrument bandwidth and grounding safety.
