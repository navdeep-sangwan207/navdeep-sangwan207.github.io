Lesson 7: Relay Driver Circuit

Transistor-Based Relay Interfacing and Flyback Protection

1. Introduction

A relay is an electrically operated switch used to control one electrical circuit using another electrical circuit. It allows a low-voltage control signal to operate a separate load circuit.

Relays are widely used in industrial control panels, automation systems, motor control, protection circuits, home appliances and embedded systems.

A microcontroller GPIO pin generally cannot supply the current required by a relay coil. A transistor driver is therefore used between the controller and relay.

Learning Objective: Understand relay operation, identify relay terminals, design a transistor relay driver, calculate its base resistor and understand flyback diode protection.

2. What is a Relay?

A relay is an electromagnetic or electronically operated switching device. In a conventional electromagnetic relay, current flowing through a coil produces a magnetic field that moves an armature and changes the contact position.

Main Parts of an Electromagnetic Relay

  • Coil: Produces the magnetic field.
  • Armature: Movable mechanical part.
  • Common contact (COM): Moving contact terminal.
  • Normally Open (NO): Contact open when the coil is de-energized.
  • Normally Closed (NC): Contact closed when the coil is de-energized.
  • Spring: Returns the armature when the coil is de-energized.

3. Working Principle of a Relay

When the Relay Coil is OFF

  • No current flows through the relay coil.
  • The electromagnet does not produce an operating magnetic field.
  • The spring keeps the armature in its normal position.
  • COM is connected to NC.
  • COM is disconnected from NO.

When the Relay Coil is ON

  • Current flows through the relay coil.
  • The coil produces a magnetic field.
  • The armature moves towards the electromagnet.
  • COM disconnects from NC.
  • COM connects to NO.
Relay Condition COM-NC COM-NO
Coil OFF Connected Disconnected
Coil ON Disconnected Connected

4. Types of Relays

Relay Type Description Application
Electromagnetic Relay Uses an energized coil and mechanical contacts. Industrial switching.
Solid-State Relay (SSR) Uses semiconductor switching devices. Silent and frequent switching.
Reed Relay Uses magnetically operated reed contacts. Signal switching.
Time Delay Relay Changes contact state after a time interval. Automation and sequencing.
Latching Relay Maintains its contact state after the coil is de-energized. Low-power control systems.

5. Relay Terminal Identification

A typical single-pole double-throw (SPDT) relay has five terminals:

  • Two coil terminals.
  • One COM terminal.
  • One NO terminal.
  • One NC terminal.
Relay terminal layouts differ between manufacturers and models. Always use the relay datasheet or printed terminal diagram before making connections.

6. Why is a Transistor Required?

A relay coil requires a particular operating voltage and current. A microcontroller GPIO pin is designed for low-current signal control, not for directly powering most relay coils.

A transistor acts as an electronic switch between the power supply and the relay coil.

Functions of the Transistor Driver

  • Provides the required coil current from an external supply.
  • Allows a low-current control signal to operate a relay.
  • Separates the controller output from the relay coil current path.
  • Enables automatic switching using digital logic.
A transistor driver does not automatically provide galvanic isolation. The controller and coil supply commonly share a ground in a basic NPN driver circuit.

7. NPN Transistor-Based Relay Driver Circuit

A common relay driver uses an NPN transistor such as BC547, BC337 or 2N2222, selected according to the relay coil current and transistor ratings.

Circuit Connections

  1. Connect one relay coil terminal to the positive DC supply.
  2. Connect the other coil terminal to the transistor collector.
  3. Connect the transistor emitter to ground.
  4. Connect the controller output to the transistor base through a resistor.
  5. Connect the flyback diode across the relay coil in reverse bias during normal operation.
  6. Connect the controller ground and driver supply ground together.
The diagram is a simplified concept illustration. For an actual circuit, connect the flyback diode directly across the relay coil: cathode to the positive coil supply and anode to the transistor collector side.

8. Working of the Relay Driver Circuit

When Input is LOW

  • Base current is approximately zero.
  • The NPN transistor remains in cutoff.
  • Relay coil current stops.
  • The relay returns to its normal contact position.

When Input is HIGH

  • Base current flows through the base resistor.
  • The transistor enters saturation when adequately driven.
  • Current flows through the relay coil.
  • The relay produces a magnetic field.
  • The armature moves and changes the contact position.

9. Base Resistor Calculation for Relay Driver

The base resistor limits the current supplied to the transistor base. 

RB = (VIN - VBE) / IB

Design Example

Assume:

  • Control voltage = 5 V.
  • Relay coil current = 70 mA.
  • Approximate base-emitter voltage = 0.7 V.
  • Forced beta = 10.

Step 1: Calculate the base current.

IB = IC / 10

IB = 70 mA / 10

IB = 7 mA

Step 2: Calculate the base resistor.

RB = (5 - 0.7) / 0.007

RB = 614.3 ohms

A standard 620 Ω resistor may be selected after checking the transistor's saturation performance and the controller's output current capability.

Select the transistor based on relay coil current, collector-emitter voltage, power dissipation and safe operating area. A small-signal transistor may not be suitable for every relay.

10. Flyback Diode Protection

A relay coil is an inductive load. When the transistor switches OFF, the collapsing magnetic field produces a voltage that can rise sharply across the coil.

This voltage is called back EMF or inductive kickback. It can damage the transistor and other connected electronic components.

Purpose of the Flyback Diode

  • Provides a path for the coil's stored energy.
  • Limits the voltage spike across the transistor.
  • Protects the switching device from inductive kickback.
  • Improves reliability of the driver circuit.

Correct Diode Connection

For a conventional low-side NPN relay driver:

  • Connect the diode cathode to the positive relay supply.
  • Connect the diode anode to the transistor collector side of the coil.
A standard flyback diode slows relay coil current decay and can increase relay release time. For faster release, a suitably designed TVS or Zener-based clamp may be used, subject to the transistor's voltage rating and circuit requirements.

11. Selecting a Relay

Relay selection depends on both coil specifications and contact ratings.

Parameter Meaning
Coil Voltage Rated voltage required to energize the coil.
Coil Current Current required by the relay coil.
Coil Resistance DC resistance of the coil.
Contact Rating Maximum specified contact voltage and current under stated conditions.
Operate Time Time required for the relay to operate.
Release Time Time required for the relay to return to its normal state.
Contact Configuration SPST, SPDT, DPDT and other arrangements.
The contact rating must be suitable for the actual load type. Motor and other inductive loads can have high starting current and arcing, so their switching ratings may be lower than resistive-load ratings.

12. Testing a Relay Using a Multimeter

Step 1: Identify the Coil Terminals

  1. Disconnect the relay from the circuit.
  2. Set the multimeter to resistance mode.
  3. Measure resistance between the suspected coil terminals.
  4. Compare the result with the relay datasheet.

Step 2: Check the Contacts

  1. Set the multimeter to continuity mode.
  2. Measure between COM and NC.
  3. Measure between COM and NO.
  4. Compare the results with the relay's normal contact state.

Step 3: Energize the Relay Safely

  1. Use a suitable DC supply matching the relay coil rating.
  2. Observe the relay operation.
  3. Check whether COM changes from NC to NO.
  4. Disconnect the supply and verify that the contacts return to their normal state.
Never apply an arbitrary voltage to a relay coil. Use the rated coil voltage and verify polarity if the relay includes an internal diode or LED.

13. Common Relay Driver Faults

Problem Possible Cause Suggested Check
Relay does not energize Insufficient coil voltage, faulty coil or missing base drive. Measure coil voltage and transistor base drive.
Transistor overheats Excessive coil current or insufficient base current. Check relay coil current and transistor ratings.
Transistor fails repeatedly Missing or incorrectly connected flyback protection. Check diode orientation and voltage rating.
Relay chatters Unstable supply or inadequate coil voltage. Measure the coil supply during operation.
Contacts do not switch Damaged contacts or mechanical failure. Check continuity and relay operation.
Controller resets Supply dips or electrical interference. Check grounding, supply regulation and decoupling.

14. Relay vs Solid-State Relay

Parameter Electromagnetic Relay Solid-State Relay
Switching mechanism Mechanical contacts Semiconductor devices
Moving parts Yes No
Switching noise Can produce clicking sound Silent operation
Contact wear Possible No mechanical contact wear
ON-state loss Very low contact resistance when healthy Semiconductor voltage drop and heating
Isolation Provided by suitable relay construction Often provided by an internal optocoupler or isolation barrier
Applications General power switching Frequent and silent switching

15. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Design and test an NPN transistor-based relay driver circuit with flyback diode protection.

Required Components

  • 5 V DC relay with known coil current.
  • BC337 or suitable NPN transistor.
  • 1N4007 diode, if appropriate for the coil and switching requirements.
  • Base resistor calculated for the selected transistor and control source.
  • DC power supply matching the relay coil rating.
  • Push button or digital control signal.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

Procedure

  1. Read the relay datasheet and identify its coil voltage and current.
  2. Identify the transistor terminals.
  3. Calculate the required base resistor.
  4. Connect the relay coil between the positive supply and transistor collector.
  5. Connect the emitter to ground.
  6. Connect the flyback diode across the relay coil with correct polarity.
  7. Connect the control input through the base resistor.
  8. Apply a LOW input and observe the relay.
  9. Apply a HIGH input and observe the relay.
  10. Measure coil voltage and verify contact operation.

Observation Table

Input Transistor Relay Coil COM Contact
LOW OFF De-energized Connected to NC
HIGH ON Energized Connected to NO

Expected Result

The relay switches ON when the control input is HIGH and switches OFF when the control input is LOW. The flyback diode protects the transistor from inductive voltage spikes.

17. Lesson Summary

  • A relay is an electrically operated switching device.
  • An electromagnetic relay uses a coil and movable contacts.
  • COM, NO and NC are common relay contact terminals.
  • A transistor is used to drive the relay coil.
  • An NPN transistor is commonly used as a low-side relay driver.
  • A base resistor limits transistor base current.
  • A flyback diode protects the transistor from inductive kickback.
  • Relay coil voltage and current must match the power supply and driver.
  • Relay contact ratings must suit the connected load.
  • Relays can be controlled using microcontrollers and digital logic.
