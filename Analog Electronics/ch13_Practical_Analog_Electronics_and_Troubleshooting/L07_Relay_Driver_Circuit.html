Relay Driver Circuit

Electromagnetic relay operation, transistor driver design, coil calculations and inductive-load protection.

2. What Is an Electromagnetic Relay?

A relay is an electrically operated switch. A current flowing through its coil generates a magnetic field that moves an armature and changes the state of its contacts.

Coil

Produces a magnetic field when energized.

Armature

Movable mechanical part attracted by the magnetic field.

Contacts

Open or close the external electrical circuit.

Spring

Returns the armature to its normal position when the coil is de-energized.

“Normally” always refers to the relay coil being de-energized. Confirm the terminal diagram printed on the actual relay body or datasheet.

4. Why Is a Relay Driver Required?

A microcontroller GPIO is designed for relatively small currents. Many relay coils require more current than a GPIO can safely supply. A transistor driver acts as an interface between the low-power control signal and the relay coil.

Control side

Microcontroller, logic IC or sensor output.

Driver stage

NPN transistor or suitable MOSFET.

Load side

Relay coil supplied from a suitable DC source.

5. NPN Transistor Relay Driver Circuit

The NPN transistor is connected as a low-side switch. The relay coil is connected between the positive coil supply and the transistor collector.

Working principle

  1. GPIO LOW: base current is absent, transistor is OFF, relay coil is de-energized.
  2. GPIO HIGH: base current flows through RB, turning the transistor ON.
  3. Coil current produces a magnetic field and moves the relay armature.
  4. COM transfers from NC to NO.
  5. When GPIO goes LOW, the transistor turns OFF and the flyback diode carries the coil's decaying current.
The flyback diode is connected in reverse bias during normal coil energization: cathode to the positive coil supply, anode to the transistor collector. Reversing it can short the supply when the relay is energized.

6. Relay Coil Current Calculation

If the coil voltage and DC resistance are known, approximate steady-state coil current using Ohm's law:

\[ I_{coil}=\frac{V_{coil}}{R_{coil}} \]

Example: A 12 V relay has a coil resistance of 400 Ω.

\[ I_{coil}=\frac{12}{400}=0.03A=30mA \]

Approximate coil power:

\[ P_{coil}=V_{coil}I_{coil}=12\times0.03=0.36W \]
Use the coil voltage and resistance specifications from the relay datasheet. Coil resistance changes with temperature, and actual pull-in and release behaviour must be checked against the manufacturer's specifications.

7. Base Resistor Design

Assume the 30 mA relay coil is switched by an NPN transistor. For an initial forced-beta design of 10:

\[ I_B=\frac{I_C}{10}=\frac{30mA}{10}=3mA \]

For a 5 V control signal and assumed VBE(sat) = 0.8 V:

\[ R_B=\frac{5-0.8}{0.003}=1400\Omega \]

A standard 1.2 kΩ resistor provides slightly more than the calculated base current, provided the GPIO output can safely supply it.

Check the transistor's VCE(sat) at the selected base current and collector current. Also check GPIO output-current limits, relay coil current, transistor power dissipation and supply capability.

9. Flyback Diode Selection

Important selection parameters include:

  • Reverse voltage rating: must exceed the maximum reverse voltage expected in the application, with suitable margin.
  • Forward current rating: must tolerate the initial coil-current circulation and repetitive operating conditions.
  • Surge capability: check the diode's non-repetitive surge-current specification where relevant.
  • Recovery behaviour: consider switching frequency and the circuit's transient requirements.

Common rectifier diodes such as the 1N400x family are often used for low-frequency DC relay coils, but suitability must be checked against the coil current, voltage and release-time requirements.

A flyback diode reduces the voltage transient but may increase relay release time. A TVS diode or Zener clamp can provide a higher controlled clamp voltage when faster release is required, provided transistor voltage ratings are respected.

10. Relay Contact Ratings and Isolation

The relay contact rating is separate from its coil rating. A relay with a 12 V DC coil may have contacts rated for a different voltage and current.

SpecificationMeaning
Coil rated voltageVoltage intended to energize the relay coil.
Coil resistance/currentDetermines coil power and driver current.
Contact voltageMaximum permitted switching voltage for specified conditions.
Contact currentPermitted switched current for the stated load type.
Electrical lifeExpected number of switching operations under specified electrical conditions.
Mechanical lifeExpected mechanical operating cycles under specified conditions.
Insulation ratingIsolation-related specifications between coil, contacts and other parts.
AC mains switching presents shock, fire and arc hazards. Do not build or test mains circuits on a breadboard. Use correctly rated, enclosed components, fusing, clearances and qualified supervision. A relay's printed rating is not a universal rating for every resistive, inductive or motor load.

12. Practical Relay Testing with a Digital Multimeter

Coil resistance test

  1. Disconnect the relay completely from the circuit.
  2. Identify coil pins using the relay datasheet.
  3. Set the DMM to resistance mode.
  4. Measure resistance across the coil terminals.
  5. Compare the result with the specified nominal coil resistance.

Contact continuity test

  1. Identify COM, NO and NC terminals.
  2. With the coil unenergized, check COM–NC continuity.
  3. Check COM–NO; it should normally be open.
  4. Use the specified coil voltage with a suitable current-limited supply to energize the relay.
  5. Disconnect power and verify that contacts return to their normal state.
Never measure resistance or continuity on an energized circuit. Use only the rated DC coil supply and observe correct polarity if the relay contains an internal diode or LED.

Procedure

  1. Read the relay datasheet and identify coil and contact terminals.
  2. Measure coil resistance with the relay disconnected.

  4. Calculate transistor base current and base resistor.
  5. Assemble the low-voltage driver circuit with the flyback diode correctly oriented.
  6. Apply a LOW control signal and verify the normal contact state.
  7. Apply a HIGH signal and observe relay operation.
  8. Measure coil voltage and transistor VCE in the ON state.
  9. Disconnect the control signal and observe release operation.

15. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is an electromagnetic relay?
An electrically operated switch that uses a magnetic field generated by a coil to move mechanical contacts.
2. What is the difference between NO and NC contacts?
NO is open and NC is closed when the relay coil is de-energized.
3. Why is a transistor used in a relay driver?
It provides the coil current that a low-current logic output may not be able to supply directly.
4. Why is a flyback diode necessary?
It provides a current path for the inductive energy stored in the coil when switching off.
5. How is relay coil current calculated?
For a DC coil, use I = V/R using rated coil voltage and specified coil resistance.
6. What does COM mean?
Common terminal; it transfers between NC and NO as the relay operates.
7. Why should relay coil supply and contact ratings be distinguished?
Coil specifications describe the control-side electromagnetic actuator. Contact ratings describe the load-switching capability.
8. What happens if the flyback diode is reversed?
It may become forward biased across the coil supply during normal operation and create a short-circuit condition.
9. Why might a relay chatter?
Coil voltage may be dropping below the required operating level due to supply limitations, wiring resistance or unstable control.
10. Can a relay switch AC mains?
Only if the relay has suitable ratings and the complete design includes proper isolation, protection, enclosure and electrical safety measures.

18. Key Takeaways

  • A relay converts electrical coil energy into mechanical contact movement.
  • COM transfers between NC and NO when the coil is energized.
  • A transistor driver interfaces a low-power control signal to a relay coil.
  • Coil current is calculated from rated voltage and coil resistance.
  • A correctly oriented flyback diode protects the transistor from inductive transients.
  • Coil ratings and contact ratings are separate specifications.
  • Always verify the relay datasheet, driver ratings and electrical safety requirements.
