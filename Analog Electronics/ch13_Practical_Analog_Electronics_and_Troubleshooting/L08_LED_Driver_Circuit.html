Lesson 8: LED Driver Circuit

Current Limiting, Transistor Drivers and PWM Dimming

1. Introduction

An LED (Light Emitting Diode) is a semiconductor device that emits light when forward biased. LEDs are widely used in indicator circuits, display systems, lighting, automotive electronics and embedded systems.

An LED driver is an electronic circuit designed to provide the required current and voltage conditions for an LED or LED array.

Learning Objective: Understand LED characteristics, calculate current limiting resistors, design transistor-based LED drivers, understand constant-current drivers and implement PWM brightness control.

2. Working Principle of an LED

An LED is a PN junction semiconductor device. When it is forward biased, electrons and holes recombine near the junction and release energy in the form of photons.

This phenomenon is called electroluminescence.

LED Terminals

  • Anode: Positive terminal.
  • Cathode: Negative terminal.

LED Forward Voltage

The forward voltage depends on the LED material, colour, current and temperature.

LED Type Approximate Forward Voltage
Red 1.8 V – 2.2 V
Yellow 1.8 V – 2.2 V
Green 2.0 V – 3.2 V
Blue 2.8 V – 3.5 V
White 2.8 V – 3.5 V
These are typical approximate ranges, not guaranteed values. Always check the LED datasheet for its forward voltage and maximum rated current.

3. Why is Current Limiting Required?

An LED has a relatively steep current-voltage characteristic in its forward conduction region. A small increase in forward voltage can cause a large increase in current.

Excessive current can overheat the LED and permanently damage it.

Therefore, a current limiting resistor or a suitable constant-current driver is required.

Methods of Current Limiting

  • Series resistor.
  • Transistor-based current regulator.
  • Constant-current LED driver IC.
  • Switching regulator-based LED driver.

4. LED Current Limiting Resistor Calculation

For a simple LED circuit, the series resistor can be calculated using Ohm's law. 

R = (VS - VF) / IF

Where:

  • VS = Supply voltage.
  • VF = LED forward voltage.
  • IF = Desired LED current.
  • R = Series resistance.

Design Example

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 5 V.
  • Red LED forward voltage = 2 V.
  • Desired LED current = 10 mA.
R = (5 - 2) / 0.01

R = 300 ohms

Select a standard 330 Ω resistor.

Actual LED Current

IF = (VS - VF) / R

IF = (5 - 2) / 330

IF = 9.09 mA

Resistor Power Calculation

PR = IF² x R

PR = (0.00909)² x 330

PR = 0.0273 W approximately

A 1/4 W resistor is suitable for this example, subject to normal operating and temperature margins.

Always calculate the resistor using the maximum possible supply voltage and an appropriate LED forward-voltage value.

5. Simple LED Driver Circuit

A basic LED driver can be constructed using a DC supply, a current limiting resistor and an LED.

Working

  1. Apply the DC supply.
  2. Current flows through the resistor and LED.
  3. The LED becomes forward biased.
  4. The LED emits light.
  5. The resistor limits the LED current.

6. LED Series and Parallel Connections

Series Connection

In a series connection, LEDs are connected one after another. The same current flows through every LED.

The total forward voltage is approximately equal to the sum of individual LED forward voltages. 

Vtotal = VF1 + VF2 + VF3 + ...

R = (VS - Vtotal) / IF

Parallel Connection

In a parallel connection, multiple LEDs are connected across the same supply.

Because individual LEDs can have different forward voltages, directly connecting them in parallel with only one shared resistor may cause unequal current sharing.

Use a separate current limiting resistor for each LED branch, or use a properly designed constant-current driver.

7. Transistor-Based LED Driver Circuit

A transistor can be used as a switch to control an LED using a low-current signal from a microcontroller or digital circuit.

NPN Low-Side LED Driver

Connections

  1. Connect the LED anode through a series resistor to the positive supply.
  2. Connect the LED cathode to the transistor collector.
  3. Connect the transistor emitter to ground.
  4. Connect the control signal to the base through a suitable resistor.
  5. Connect the controller ground to the driver ground.

Working

  • LOW input: transistor is OFF and LED is OFF.
  • HIGH input: transistor turns ON and LED lights.
The diagram is conceptual. The LED series resistor and transistor base resistor are separate components. Do not omit either resistor in a practical circuit.

8. Base Resistor Calculation for an LED Driver

Consider a transistor switching an LED current of 20 mA.

Assume:

  • Control voltage = 5 V.
  • Collector current = 20 mA.
  • Forced beta = 10.
  • Base-emitter voltage = 0.7 V.
IB = IC / 10

IB = 20 mA / 10

IB = 2 mA
            
RB = (VIN - VBE) / IB

RB = (5 - 0.7) / 0.002

RB = 2150 ohms

A standard 2.2 kΩ base resistor can be selected after verifying the transistor's saturation performance and the controller's output current capability.

9. Constant-Current LED Driver

A constant-current LED driver regulates the current flowing through the LED rather than simply applying a fixed voltage.

This is important because LED brightness, operating temperature and reliability are strongly influenced by forward current.

Types of Constant-Current Drivers

  • Linear constant-current driver.
  • Switching constant-current driver.
  • Buck LED driver.
  • Boost LED driver.
  • Buck-boost LED driver.

Linear LED Driver

A linear driver controls current using a pass transistor or regulator circuit. It is relatively simple but can dissipate significant power as heat when the voltage drop is large.

Switching LED Driver

A switching driver uses an inductor, semiconductor switch, diode or synchronous switch, and control circuitry to regulate LED current efficiently.

Driver Typical Feature
Linear Simple circuit, higher heat dissipation in some conditions.
Buck Steps down input voltage.
Boost Steps up input voltage.
Buck-Boost Can regulate with input voltage above or below the required output range.

10. PWM-Based LED Brightness Control

PWM stands for Pulse Width Modulation. It controls average LED power by switching the LED ON and OFF rapidly.

The duty cycle determines the proportion of each PWM period for which the signal remains HIGH. 

Duty Cycle = (TON / T) x 100%

Where:

TON = ON time
T   = Total period
Duty Cycle Typical Brightness Effect
0% OFF
25% Low brightness
50% Medium brightness
75% Higher brightness
100% Continuously ON

PWM Waveform

PWM is widely used in microcontroller-based LED dimming. The PWM frequency should be selected to avoid visible flicker and to meet the LED driver and application requirements.

11. LED Driver Applications

  • LED indicator circuits.
  • Automotive lighting.
  • Industrial status indicators.
  • Display backlighting.
  • Street lighting.

  • Embedded system status LEDs.
  • Battery-powered lighting systems.
  • Smart lighting and dimming systems.

12. LED Driver Troubleshooting

Problem Possible Cause Suggested Check
LED does not glow Wrong polarity, open LED or no supply. Check LED polarity and supply voltage.
LED brightness is low Insufficient current or excessive series resistance. Measure LED current and supply voltage.
LED burns out Excessive forward current. Check current limiting resistor or driver.
LED flickers Unstable supply, PWM issue or poor connection. Check supply ripple and PWM signal.
Transistor overheats Excessive current or power dissipation. Check transistor ratings and operating point.
Unequal LED brightness Unequal current sharing in parallel LEDs. Use individual branch resistors or a suitable driver.

13. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Construct a transistor-based LED driver circuit and control the LED using a digital input.

Required Components

  • 5 V DC supply.
  • Red LED.
  • 330 Ω series resistor.
  • BC547 or suitable NPN transistor.
  • 2.2 kΩ base resistor for the example design.
  • Push button or digital control signal.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Identify the anode and cathode of the LED.
  2. Connect the LED in series with the 330 Ω resistor.
  3. Connect the LED-resistor branch to the transistor collector circuit.
  4. Connect the transistor emitter to ground.
  5. Connect the control input to the base through a 2.2 kΩ resistor.
  6. Apply a LOW input and observe the LED.
  7. Apply a HIGH input and observe the LED.
  8. Measure the LED current and compare it with the calculated value.

Observation Table

Input Transistor State LED State
LOW OFF OFF
HIGH ON ON

Expected Result

The LED turns ON when the control input is HIGH and turns OFF when the control input is LOW. The series resistor limits the LED current to a safe operating value.

15. Lesson Summary

  • An LED is a PN junction semiconductor device that emits light.
  • LEDs must operate within their rated forward-current limits.
  • A series resistor can limit current in a simple LED circuit.
  • Transistors can be used to switch LEDs using digital signals.
  • Constant-current drivers regulate LED current.
  • Switching drivers can provide efficient LED power conversion.
  • PWM controls average LED power and brightness.
  • Parallel LED branches should have appropriate current balancing.
  • LED driver troubleshooting includes checking polarity, current, voltage and connections.
