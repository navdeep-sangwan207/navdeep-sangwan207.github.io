LED Driver Circuit

LED electrical characteristics, current limiting, transistor drivers, constant-current operation and PWM dimming.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand LED forward voltage and current characteristics.
  • Calculate a suitable series current-limiting resistor.
  • Design series and parallel LED arrangements.
  • Use BJT and MOSFET circuits to drive LEDs.
  • Understand constant-current LED driving.
  • Calculate resistor power dissipation and driver losses.
  • Understand PWM-based LED brightness control.
  • Test and troubleshoot practical LED driver circuits.

2. LED Fundamentals

A light-emitting diode (LED) is a semiconductor device that emits light when forward biased. Its forward voltage depends on semiconductor material, LED colour, current and temperature.

Forward voltage (VF)

Voltage drop across an LED when forward current flows.

Forward current (IF)

Current flowing through the LED in its forward direction.

Reverse voltage

Maximum permitted reverse bias must not be exceeded.

Power dissipation

Approximately VF × IF for the LED under a given operating condition.

LED type / colourTypical approximate forward voltage
Red1.8–2.2 V
Yellow / amber1.9–2.4 V
Green2.0–3.2 V, depending on LED technology
Blue2.7–3.5 V
White2.8–3.6 V
These are approximate ranges, not guaranteed design values. Use the LED datasheet's forward-voltage range at the intended current and temperature.

3. Why Does an LED Need Current Limiting?

An LED has a steep current-voltage characteristic. A small increase in forward voltage can produce a large increase in current. Connecting an ordinary LED directly to a voltage source can therefore cause excessive current and permanent damage.

Never assume that an LED will safely draw its rated current just because the supply voltage is close to its forward voltage. Use a current-limiting resistor or a suitable constant-current driver.

4. Basic LED Series Resistor Circuit

The resistor absorbs the voltage remaining after the LED forward drop.

\[ R=\frac{V_{CC}-V_F}{I_F} \]

Resistor power:

\[ P_R=I_F^2R=(V_{CC}-V_F)I_F \]

Worked example

A red LED has a forward voltage of 2 V and is operated from a 5 V supply at 10 mA.

\[ R=\frac{5-2}{0.01}=300\Omega \]

A 330 Ω standard resistor gives approximately:

\[ I_F=\frac{5-2}{330}=9.09mA \]

Resistor power is approximately:

\[ P_R=(0.00909)^2\times330=0.0273W \]

A 0.25 W resistor is a suitable rating for this illustrative operating point, subject to temperature and derating requirements.

6. Multiple LEDs in Series

When LEDs are connected in series, the same current flows through every LED. Their forward voltages add together.

\[ R=\frac{V_{CC}-(V_{F1}+V_{F2}+\cdots+V_{Fn})}{I_F} \]

Example: Three LEDs each have VF = 2 V, supplied from 12 V at 15 mA.

\[ R=\frac{12-(3\times2)}{0.015}=400\Omega \]

Resistor power:

\[ P_R=(12-6)\times0.015=0.09W \]

A suitable standard resistor and power rating should be selected with margin. Check the worst-case total LED forward voltage before finalizing the design.

7. Multiple LEDs in Parallel

Connecting several LEDs directly in parallel behind one resistor is generally not recommended because small forward-voltage differences can cause unequal current sharing.

For simple resistor-driven parallel branches, use a separate resistor for each LED or matched LED string. For large LED arrays, a properly designed constant-current driver is usually preferable.

8. Transistor-Based LED Driver

A BJT can be used as a low-side switch when a control output cannot directly drive the LED current. The LED still requires a current-limiting resistor.

Design steps:

  1. Calculate LED resistor using the LED supply voltage and forward voltage.
  2. Calculate the LED current.
  3. Choose a transistor with suitable collector current and voltage ratings.
  4. Calculate base current using a conservative forced beta.
  5. Select a base resistor that supplies adequate drive without exceeding the GPIO limit.
  6. Verify the transistor's VCE(sat) and power dissipation.

9. MOSFET-Based LED Driver

A logic-level N-channel MOSFET can be used as a low-side LED switch. The LED and current-limiting resistor connect between the positive supply and MOSFET drain. The source connects to ground.

Gate

Receives the control signal. A series gate resistor and gate-to-source pull-down may be used as appropriate.

Drain

Connects to the low side of the LED load.

Source

Connects to ground in a common low-side N-channel arrangement.

For microcontroller control, check RDS(on) at the actual available gate voltage. A threshold voltage specification does not mean the MOSFET is fully enhanced at that voltage.

For PWM switching, verify MOSFET gate charge, switching losses, thermal dissipation and the controller's ability to drive the gate at the required frequency.

10. Constant-Current LED Driver

A constant-current driver regulates LED current rather than merely applying a fixed voltage. This is particularly important for high-power LEDs and LED strings.

A simple linear current regulator can use a sense resistor:

\[ I_{LED}\approx\frac{V_{REF}}{R_S} \]

Here VREF is the regulated voltage across the current-sense resistor. The actual circuit depends on the controller or regulator topology.

Linear driver

Simple, low noise, but dissipates excess voltage as heat.

Switching driver

Uses an inductor and switching device to achieve improved efficiency over a suitable input/output range.

A high-power LED should not be treated like an ordinary indicator LED. Use a driver designed for its rated current, thermal requirements and operating conditions.

11. PWM LED Brightness Control

Pulse-width modulation controls average LED output by changing the ON-time relative to the PWM period. The LED is switched rapidly between ON and OFF states.

\[ D=\frac{T_{ON}}{T}\times100\% \]

For an idealized fixed-current drive, average current is approximately:

\[ I_{AVG}\approx D\times I_{ON} \]

Example: An LED is driven at 20 mA with a PWM duty cycle of 40%.

\[ I_{AVG}\approx0.4\times20=8mA \]

This average-current approximation is useful for basic analysis, but perceived brightness is not necessarily linear with duty cycle. The PWM frequency and pulse current must remain within the LED datasheet limits.

13. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Build and test a resistor-driven LED circuit and an NPN transistor LED driver.

Equipment

  • Red LED and optional blue or white LED.
  • Resistors of suitable values and power ratings.
  • NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222, subject to ratings.
  • Low-voltage DC supply, breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Read the LED datasheet and identify its anode and cathode.
  2. Calculate the current-limiting resistor for a selected current.
  3. Connect the LED and resistor in series to a current-limited low-voltage supply.
  4. Measure voltage across the LED and resistor.
  5. Calculate actual current using the measured resistor voltage.
  6. Build an NPN low-side LED driver with a calculated base resistor.
  7. Apply LOW and HIGH control signals and record LED behaviour.
  8. If PWM is available, vary duty cycle and observe brightness changes.

15. Placement Interview Questions

1. Why does an LED need a current-limiting resistor?
Because LED current can rise sharply with a small increase in forward voltage. The resistor limits current to a safe value.
2. How is the series resistor calculated?
R = (VCC − VF) / IF for a simple series-resistor circuit.
3. Why are separate resistors recommended for parallel LEDs?
They improve current sharing because individual LED forward voltages can differ.
4. What is a constant-current LED driver?
A driver that regulates LED current to a controlled value over its specified operating range.
5. What is PWM dimming?
Brightness control by changing the proportion of time the LED is switched ON.
6. Why is a MOSFET often used in high-current LED switching?
A suitably selected MOSFET can have low ON-state resistance and requires very little steady-state gate current.
7. What is the significance of LED forward voltage?
It determines the voltage remaining for the current-limiting element or driver.
8. Why is thermal management important for high-power LEDs?
Junction temperature affects reliability, output and lifetime. Excessive temperature can damage the LED.
9. Does a MOSFET threshold voltage mean it is fully ON?
No. Threshold voltage is specified at a small drain current. Check RDS(on) at the actual gate-source voltage.
10. How can LED current be measured?
Measure current in series using a correctly configured DMM, or measure the voltage across a known series resistor and calculate I = V/R.

18. Key Takeaways

  • LEDs require controlled forward current.
  • A series resistor is a simple current-limiting solution for indicator LEDs.
  • Use separate current-limiting resistors for parallel LED branches.
  • Use BJT or MOSFET drivers when the control output cannot supply the required load current.
  • High-power LEDs generally require properly designed constant-current drivers and thermal management.
  • PWM controls average LED current and brightness.
  • Verify LED, resistor and driver ratings before construction.
