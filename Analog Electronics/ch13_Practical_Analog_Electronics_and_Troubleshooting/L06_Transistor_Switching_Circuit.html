Transistor Switching Circuit

BJT cutoff, saturation, low-side and high-side switching, base-drive design and practical load control.

2. BJT as an Electronic Switch

A bipolar junction transistor can operate as an electronic switch by moving between two operating regions:

Cutoff region — OFF

Base-emitter drive is insufficient. Collector current is approximately zero, apart from leakage current.

Saturation region — ON

The transistor is driven sufficiently hard that both junctions are forward biased. Collector-emitter voltage becomes relatively low.

In switching applications, the transistor is not normally intended to operate continuously in the active region. The base drive should be designed to achieve reliable switching under the expected load conditions.

3. NPN Low-Side Switching Circuit

An NPN transistor is commonly used as a low-side switch. The load connects between the positive supply and collector. The emitter connects to ground. A base resistor limits the base current.

Operation

  1. When the input is LOW, base current is negligible and the transistor is OFF.
  2. The collector is pulled toward the positive supply through the load.
  3. When the input is HIGH, base current flows through the base resistor.
  4. The transistor enters saturation if the base drive is sufficient.
  5. Load current flows from VCC through the load, collector and emitter to ground.
The load is connected to the positive supply, while the transistor switches its connection to ground. This is why the arrangement is called a low-side switch.

4. Base Resistor Calculation

The base resistor limits current supplied by the control signal. A common design approach uses a forced beta considerably lower than the transistor's small-signal current gain.

\[ I_B \geq \frac{I_C}{\beta_{forced}} \] \[ R_B \leq \frac{V_{drive}-V_{BE(sat)}}{I_B} \]

For a conservative switching design, a forced beta of 10 is often used as an initial design assumption, but the transistor datasheet and actual drive capability must be checked.

Worked example

A 5 V logic output must switch a 100 mA load. Assume \(V_{BE(sat)}=0.8V\) and choose forced beta = 10.

\[ I_B=\frac{100mA}{10}=10mA \] \[ R_B=\frac{5-0.8}{0.01}=420\Omega \]

A standard 390 Ω resistor would provide approximately 10.8 mA of base current, assuming the logic output can safely source that current.

Do not select a resistor from this calculation alone. Check the microcontroller or logic output's maximum source current, transistor base-current rating, VCE(sat) test conditions and total power dissipation. If the GPIO cannot supply the required current, use a driver stage or MOSFET.

6. PNP High-Side Switching

A PNP transistor can be used as a high-side switch. Its emitter connects to the positive supply and its collector supplies the load. The transistor turns ON when its base is driven sufficiently below its emitter voltage.

In a practical circuit, the control signal must be compatible with the emitter supply. A logic-level signal may require an additional NPN transistor or a dedicated driver when switching a higher supply voltage.

Never pull a PNP base directly to a low-voltage GPIO if the emitter is connected to a higher voltage unless the interface is designed to protect the GPIO and limit base current.

7. LED Switching Circuit

An LED can be switched using an NPN transistor. The LED requires a series current-limiting resistor.

\[ R_{LED}=\frac{V_{CC}-V_F-V_{CE(sat)}}{I_{LED}} \]

Example: \(VCC=12V\), \(V_F=2V\), \(VCE(sat)=0.2V\), and desired LED current = 10 mA.

\[ R_{LED}=\frac{12-2-0.2}{0.01}=980\Omega \]

A nearby standard value such as 1 kΩ is a reasonable starting point. Verify the actual LED forward voltage, current rating and resistor power dissipation.

8. Inductive Loads and Flyback Diode

Relay coils, solenoids and motors store energy in their magnetic fields. When transistor current is interrupted, the collapsing magnetic field can generate a high voltage across the coil.

A flyback diode connected across a DC relay coil provides a path for the coil current to decay and helps limit the voltage spike.

For a typical NPN low-side relay driver, the flyback diode is connected with its cathode to the positive coil supply and its anode to the transistor collector / low side of the coil.

The diode must be selected for the coil current, reverse voltage and required release time. A simple diode can slow relay release. Higher-speed or controlled clamp arrangements may be required in some applications.

10. Switching Time and Power Dissipation

Practical switching transistors do not change state instantaneously. Important switching parameters include:

  • Delay time: time between input application and the beginning of collector current rise.
  • Rise time: time for collector current to rise between specified limits.
  • Storage time: time required to remove stored charge after switching off.
  • Fall time: time for collector current to fall between specified limits.

During the transition, the transistor may simultaneously carry significant current and support significant voltage, creating switching loss.

\[ P_{instantaneous}=V_{CE}\times I_C \]

For a simple steady ON state, approximate transistor conduction loss as:

\[ P_{ON}\approx V_{CE(sat)}\times I_C \]

12. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Construct and test an NPN transistor low-side switch using a low-voltage supply and a suitable LED load.

Equipment

  • NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222, after checking its ratings and pinout.
  • LED and current-limiting resistor.
  • Base resistor and low-voltage DC supply.
  • Digital multimeter and breadboard.
  • Signal source or microcontroller GPIO, if available.

Procedure

  1. Identify transistor terminals using its datasheet.
  2. Connect emitter to circuit ground.
  3. Connect the LED and its series resistor between supply and collector.
  4. Connect the control input to base through the calculated resistor.
  5. Apply LOW input and observe the LED.
  6. Apply HIGH input and observe switching.
  7. Measure base-emitter and collector-emitter voltages in both states.
  8. Compare measured values with expected cutoff and saturation behaviour.

14. Placement Interview Questions

1. Which BJT regions are used for switching?
Cutoff for OFF and saturation for ON.
2. Why is a base resistor required?
It limits base current and protects the transistor and control output.
3. What is forced beta?
The design ratio of collector current to base current used to ensure adequate saturation drive.
4. What is a low-side switch?
A switching arrangement in which the transistor connects the load to ground.
5. What is a high-side switch?
A switching arrangement in which the transistor controls the connection between the positive supply and the load.
6. Why is a flyback diode connected across a relay coil?
It provides a path for inductive current when the transistor switches off and limits the voltage transient.
7. Why is transistor saturation desirable in a switching circuit?
It reduces collector-emitter voltage and therefore reduces ON-state conduction loss.
8. Can a microcontroller directly drive every BJT switch?
No. The required base current must be within the GPIO's permitted source or sink current.
9. What happens if a transistor is under-driven?
It may remain in the active region, causing higher VCE, greater power dissipation and overheating.
10. Why should the transistor pinout be checked?
Different part numbers and packages may have different E-B-C terminal arrangements.

17. Key Takeaways

  • BJT switching uses cutoff and saturation as the OFF and ON states.
  • Base current must be designed using a suitable forced beta.
  • NPN low-side switching is widely used for load control.
  • PNP devices can provide high-side switching but require suitable drive circuitry.
  • LEDs require current limiting; inductive loads require transient protection.
  • Check GPIO current capability, transistor ratings, thermal limits and pinout.
