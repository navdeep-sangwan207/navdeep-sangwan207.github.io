Lesson 6: Transistor Switching Circuit

BJT as an Electronic Switch – Cutoff, Saturation and Practical Design

1. Introduction

A transistor can operate as an electronic switch by controlling the current flowing through its collector-emitter path using a small base current.

In switching applications, a BJT is mainly operated in two regions:

  • Cutoff region: Transistor behaves approximately like an open switch.
  • Saturation region: Transistor behaves approximately like a closed switch.

Transistor switching is commonly used in LED drivers, relay drivers, alarm circuits, motor control, embedded systems and digital interfacing.

Learning Objective: Understand BJT switching operation, calculate a suitable base resistor, design an LED switching circuit and troubleshoot transistor switching faults.

2. Transistor as a Switch

A transistor acts as an electronic switch when it is driven between cutoff and saturation.

OFF State

When the base-emitter voltage is insufficient to turn the transistor ON, base current is approximately zero. Collector current is also approximately zero, apart from leakage.

ON State

When sufficient base current is supplied, the transistor enters saturation and allows collector current to flow through the load.

3. Operating Regions of a BJT

Parameter Cutoff Active Saturation
Base current Approximately zero Controlled Enough to drive ON
Collector current Approximately zero Proportional to base current Limited mainly by load
Base-emitter junction Not forward biased Forward biased Forward biased
Base-collector junction Reverse biased Reverse biased Forward biased
Switching state OFF Not fully ON or OFF ON
In practical switching circuits, saturation is preferred for the ON state because it produces a relatively low collector-emitter voltage and reduces transistor power loss.

4. NPN Transistor as a Low-Side Switch

An NPN transistor is commonly used as a low-side switch. The load is connected between the positive supply and the collector. The emitter is connected to ground.

When the input signal is HIGH, base current flows through the base resistor. The transistor turns ON and current flows through the LED load.

When the input signal is LOW, base current stops and the transistor turns OFF.

The diagram is a conceptual illustration. Use the circuit connections and component calculations below when building the actual circuit.

5. Working of an NPN Switching Circuit

When Input is LOW

  • Base voltage is approximately 0 V.
  • Base-emitter junction is not sufficiently forward biased.
  • Transistor remains in cutoff.
  • Collector current is approximately zero.
  • LED remains OFF.

When Input is HIGH

  • Positive voltage is applied through the base resistor.
  • Base current flows.
  • Transistor enters saturation when sufficient base drive is provided.
  • Collector current flows through the LED.
  • LED turns ON.

6. Base Resistor Calculation

A base resistor limits the current flowing into the transistor base. Without a suitable resistor, excessive base current may damage the transistor or the control output.

The base resistor is calculated using:

RB = (VIN - VBE) / IB

Where:

  • RB = Base resistor.
  • VIN = Input voltage.
  • VBE = Base-emitter voltage.
  • IB = Required base current.

Design Example

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 5 V.
  • Input voltage = 5 V.
  • Base-emitter voltage = 0.7 V (approximate).
  • Desired collector current = 100 mA.
  • Forced beta = 10 for a conservative switching design.

Step 1: Calculate base current.

IB = IC / Forced Beta

IB = 100 mA / 10

IB = 10 mA

Step 2: Calculate base resistance.

RB = (VIN - VBE) / IB

RB = (5 - 0.7) / 0.01

RB = 430 ohms

A standard resistor value of 430 ohms can be selected, provided the transistor and control output can safely handle the resulting current.

Always verify the transistor's maximum base current, collector current and power dissipation. Also check the GPIO output current rating when using a microcontroller as the input source.

7. LED Switching Circuit Design

A simple LED switching circuit can be designed using an NPN transistor, a base resistor and an LED current-limiting resistor.

Required Components

  • BC547 NPN transistor.
  • LED.
  • Base resistor: 1 kΩ (example for a suitable low-current drive).
  • LED resistor: 330 Ω (example for a 5 V supply).
  • 5 V DC supply.
  • Input switch or logic signal.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

LED Resistor Calculation

Assume:

  • Supply voltage = 5 V.
  • LED forward voltage = 2 V.
  • Transistor saturation voltage = 0.2 V.
  • Desired LED current = 10 mA.
RLED = (VCC - VF - VCE(sat)) / ILED

RLED = (5 - 2 - 0.2) / 0.01

RLED = 280 ohms

A standard resistor value of 330 Ω can be used. The resulting LED current will be slightly lower than the 10 mA design target.

Actual Circuit Connections

  1. Connect the emitter of the BC547 to ground.
  2. Connect the collector to the LED cathode.
  3. Connect the LED anode through a 330 Ω resistor to +5 V.
  4. Connect the input signal to the base through a 1 kΩ resistor.
  5. Ensure the input source and circuit share a common ground.

8. PNP Transistor as a High-Side Switch

A PNP transistor can be used as a high-side switch. Its emitter is connected to the positive supply and the load is connected between the collector and ground.

The transistor turns ON when its base voltage is sufficiently lower than its emitter voltage.

Working

  • When base voltage is close to emitter voltage, the transistor is OFF.
  • When the base is pulled sufficiently below the emitter, the transistor turns ON.
  • Current flows from the positive supply through emitter, collector and load to ground.
PNP high-side switching is useful when the load must be connected to ground and its positive supply is controlled by the transistor.
A PNP transistor connected to a supply higher than the control logic voltage may require a level-shifting or driver stage. Do not connect a high-voltage base circuit directly to a low-voltage microcontroller pin.

9. Transistor Switching Time

A transistor does not switch instantaneously. Its switching performance is described using different timing parameters.

Parameter Description
Delay Time (td) Time between input application and the start of collector current response.
Rise Time (tr) Time taken by collector current to rise between specified levels.
Storage Time (ts) Time required to remove stored charge after switching OFF.
Fall Time (tf) Time taken by collector current to decrease between specified levels.

Deep saturation can increase stored charge and cause slower turn-off. Switching speed is important in high-frequency circuits.

10. Power Dissipation in a Switching Transistor

Transistor power dissipation depends on collector current and collector-emitter voltage. 

P = VCE × IC

During saturation, VCE is relatively low, reducing conduction loss.

During switching transitions, the transistor may temporarily have significant voltage and current simultaneously, increasing switching loss.

Always check the transistor's maximum power dissipation, safe operating area, junction temperature and thermal requirements in its datasheet.

11. Applications of Transistor Switching

  • LED control circuits.
  • Relay driver circuits.
  • DC motor control.
  • Alarm and buzzer circuits.
  • Digital logic interfacing.
  • Microcontroller output interfacing.
  • Power supply control circuits.
  • Electronic load switching.

12. Common Switching Circuit Faults

Problem Possible Cause Suggested Check
LED always OFF Incorrect pinout, missing base drive, reversed LED or open connection. Check wiring, base voltage and transistor terminals.
LED always ON Base held HIGH, shorted transistor or incorrect input wiring. Check base voltage and transistor junctions.
Transistor overheating Excessive collector current or insufficient base drive. Check load current and transistor power dissipation.
Unreliable switching Insufficient base current or unstable input signal. Check base resistor and input signal.
Microcontroller resets Supply disturbance or excessive load current. Check power supply, grounding and decoupling.

13. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Design and test an NPN transistor switching circuit to control an LED using a digital input signal.

Required Components

  • BC547 NPN transistor.
  • LED.
  • 330 Ω resistor.
  • 1 kΩ resistor.
  • 5 V regulated DC supply.
  • Push button or logic input.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Identify the BC547 terminals using its datasheet.
  2. Connect the emitter to ground.
  3. Connect the collector to the LED cathode.
  4. Connect the LED anode through a 330 Ω resistor to +5 V.
  5. Connect the input through a 1 kΩ base resistor.
  6. Apply a LOW input and observe the LED.
  7. Apply a HIGH input and observe the LED.
  8. Measure the collector-emitter voltage in both states.

Observation Table

Input Transistor State LED State VCE Observation
LOW Cutoff OFF Approximately supply voltage (subject to circuit)
HIGH Saturation ON Low voltage, typically around 0.1–0.3 V

Expected Result

The LED turns OFF when the input is LOW and turns ON when the input is HIGH. The transistor operates approximately in cutoff and saturation regions.

15. Lesson Summary

  • A BJT can operate as an electronic switch.
  • Cutoff represents the OFF state.
  • Saturation represents the ON state.
  • A base resistor limits base current.
  • NPN transistors are commonly used for low-side switching.
  • PNP transistors are commonly used for high-side switching.
  • Base current must be sufficient for the required load current.
  • Transistor power dissipation must remain within safe limits.
  • Switching time affects high-frequency performance.
  • Transistor switching is widely used in embedded and electronic systems.
