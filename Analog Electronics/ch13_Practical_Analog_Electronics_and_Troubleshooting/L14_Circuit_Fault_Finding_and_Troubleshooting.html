Circuit Fault Finding and Troubleshooting

Systematic diagnosis of analog circuits using DMMs, oscilloscopes and signal tracing.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand systematic troubleshooting methods.
  • Identify common electrical and electronic circuit faults.
  • Perform visual inspection and continuity testing.
  • Use voltage tracing and signal tracing techniques.
  • Diagnose resistor, capacitor, diode, BJT and MOSFET faults.
  • Troubleshoot rectifiers, power supplies, amplifiers and op-amp circuits.
  • Use a logical fault-isolation procedure.
  • Document test results and verify corrective action.

2. What Is Circuit Troubleshooting?

Circuit troubleshooting is the systematic process of identifying, isolating and correcting faults in an electronic circuit.

Fault detection

Recognize abnormal operation or a failed output.

Fault isolation

Narrow the problem to a section or component.

Fault identification

Determine the actual failure mechanism.

Fault correction

Repair or replace the defective part.

Verification

Repeat tests to confirm normal operation.

A good technician does not replace components randomly. Each measurement should test a specific hypothesis about the circuit.

4. Systematic Troubleshooting Procedure

  1. Understand the complaint and expected circuit operation.
  2. Inspect the PCB, wiring, connectors and component polarity.
  3. Check supply rails, ground reference and protection devices.
  4. Measure key nodes and trace the signal path.
  5. Isolate the faulty stage and test likely components.
  6. Repair the cause, then repeat the original functional test.

5. Visual Inspection

Visual inspection is usually the first troubleshooting step, before applying power.

  • Look for burnt, cracked or discoloured components.
  • Inspect electrolytic capacitors for leakage, bulging or damage.
  • Check solder joints for cracks, bridges and poor wetting.
  • Check connector orientation and loose wires.
  • Verify IC orientation, polarity markings and component values.
  • Inspect PCB tracks for scratches, lifted pads or corrosion.
A component can fail without visible damage. A clean-looking PCB is not proof that the circuit is healthy.

6. Continuity and Resistance Testing

Continuity mode checks whether two points have a sufficiently low-resistance connection. The meter usually produces an audible indication below a model-dependent threshold.

Correct procedure

  1. Switch off the circuit.
  2. Disconnect the power source.
  3. Discharge capacitors safely using an appropriate method.
  4. Verify that no hazardous voltage remains.
  5. Place probes at the two test points.
  6. Interpret the reading according to circuit paths and component behaviour.
Never use resistance or continuity mode on an energized circuit. In-circuit readings may be affected by parallel paths, semiconductor junctions and capacitors.

7. Voltage Tracing

Voltage tracing means measuring voltage at selected nodes and comparing the results with expected values.

For a simple resistor divider:

For an unloaded divider:

\[ V_{out}=V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2} \]

Example: \(V_{in}=12V\), \(R_1=10k\Omega\), \(R_2=10k\Omega\).

\[ V_{out}=12\times\frac{10}{20}=6V \]

If the measured output is near 0 V, investigate a shorted lower resistor, open upper resistor, incorrect wiring or excessive loading.

8. Signal Tracing

Signal tracing follows an input waveform through successive circuit stages using an oscilloscope.

Input stage

Verify that the expected input signal reaches the circuit.

Amplifier stage

Compare amplitude, phase and waveform shape.

Filter stage

Check frequency-dependent attenuation.

Output stage

Check output amplitude, clipping and load response.

If the input signal is correct at TP1 but absent at TP2, investigate the amplifier stage and its supply, bias and connections.

11. Troubleshooting an Amplifier

  • No output: Check supply rails, input signal, bias, feedback and output connections.
  • Low gain: Verify resistor values, transistor bias, feedback components and loading.
  • Clipping: Check input amplitude, supply voltage and output swing limitations.
  • Distortion: Check bias point, nonlinear operation, loading and frequency response.
  • Oscillation: Inspect feedback paths, decoupling, grounding and layout.
  • Excessive noise: Investigate grounding, shielding, component noise and supply ripple.
For an op-amp, always verify that the input common-mode voltage and output swing are within the actual device's datasheet limits. An ideal op-amp assumption is not a troubleshooting specification.

14. Safety During Troubleshooting

  • Disconnect power before resistance, continuity or component replacement work.
  • Verify capacitor discharge before touching a circuit.
  • Use probes, meters and PPE rated for the measurement category and voltage.
  • Use a current-limited, isolated low-voltage supply for training and initial power-up.
  • Use an appropriate series current-limiting method when the design permits it.
  • Do not bypass fuses, interlocks or protective-earth connections.
  • Never connect an earth-grounded oscilloscope ground clip to an unknown live node.
  • Do not probe mains circuits unless trained, equipped and following approved safety procedures.
An isolation transformer does not make every circuit safe to touch. High voltage, stored energy and incorrect grounding can still cause fatal electric shock.

15. Practical Lab: Fault Finding in a Low-Voltage Circuit

Objective: Identify an introduced fault in a low-voltage resistor-divider and LED indicator circuit.

Equipment

  • Current-limited low-voltage DC supply.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Resistors, LED and suitable series resistor.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Optional oscilloscope for signal observation.

Procedure

  1. Build and verify the correct circuit before introducing any fault.
  2. Switch off the supply before changing connections.
  3. Introduce one controlled fault, such as an open connection or incorrect resistor value.
  4. Record the observed symptom.
  5. Inspect the wiring and component orientation.
  6. Measure supply voltage and selected node voltages.
  7. Use continuity mode only after power is removed.
  8. Identify the likely fault using measurement evidence.
  9. Correct the fault and repeat the functional test.

17. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is troubleshooting?
A systematic method of detecting, isolating, identifying and correcting faults in a circuit or system.
2. What is the difference between an open circuit and a short circuit?
An open circuit interrupts the intended current path. A short circuit creates an unintended low-resistance path.
3. What is voltage tracing?
Measuring voltage at selected circuit nodes and comparing it with expected values to locate a faulty section.
4. What is signal tracing?
Following a signal through successive circuit stages to determine where it becomes missing or distorted.
5. Why is visual inspection important?
It can reveal burnt components, solder bridges, damaged tracks, reversed components and loose connectors before powered testing.
6. How do you check a diode using a DMM?
With power removed, use diode-test mode. A normal silicon diode often shows a forward drop around 0.5–0.8 V and OL in reverse, subject to device type and in-circuit paths.
7. What can cause excessive power-supply ripple?
A degraded filter capacitor, faulty rectifier, excessive load, poor connections or insufficient filtering.
8. What is root-cause analysis?
Determining the underlying reason for a failure rather than only correcting its visible symptom.
9. Why should continuity tests be performed with power removed?
The meter's resistance/continuity circuitry is not intended for energized circuits. External voltage can produce incorrect readings or damage the instrument.
10. What should be done after repairing a faulty circuit?
Repeat the original functional and electrical tests under the required operating conditions, then document the verified result.

18. Numerical and Practical Exercises

  1. A 12 V supply feeds a 10 kΩ and 10 kΩ unloaded divider. Calculate the expected output voltage.
  2. A 5 V supply is connected to a 100 Ω load. Calculate the load current.
  3. A regulator input is 12 V and its output is 5 V at 0.2 A. Calculate its approximate power dissipation, ignoring quiescent current.
  4. An amplifier input is 100 mVpp and output is 1 Vpp. Calculate voltage gain.
  5. Describe a systematic procedure for locating an open connection in an LED circuit.
  6. A power supply has a correct DC average but excessive ripple. List the measurements and components you would investigate.
  7. Explain how you would distinguish between an amplifier fault and an input signal source fault.

19. Key Takeaways

  • Follow a systematic fault-isolation procedure rather than replacing components randomly.
  • Start with visual inspection and safe power checks.
  • Use voltage tracing to locate abnormal DC nodes.
  • Use signal tracing to identify the stage where a waveform becomes abnormal.
  • Understand the limitations of in-circuit component testing.
  • Confirm the root cause and verify the repaired circuit.
  • Document test conditions, measurements and corrective actions.
