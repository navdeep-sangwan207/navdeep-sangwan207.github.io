Lesson 14: Circuit Fault Finding and Troubleshooting

Practical Fault Diagnosis, Testing and Repair Techniques

1. Introduction

Circuit troubleshooting is the systematic process of identifying, diagnosing and correcting faults in electronic circuits.

Electronic circuits may fail because of damaged components, incorrect connections, power supply problems, poor solder joints, overheating, electrical noise or improper operating conditions.

A technician or electronics engineer uses test instruments and circuit knowledge to locate the fault without unnecessarily replacing components.

Learning Objective: Develop a systematic troubleshooting approach using a digital multimeter, oscilloscope, function generator and component datasheets.

2. Types of Electronic Circuit Faults

1. Open Circuit Fault

An open circuit occurs when the electrical path is interrupted.

Possible causes:

  • Broken wire or PCB track.
  • Disconnected connector.
  • Cracked solder joint.
  • Damaged fuse.
  • Open resistor or component terminal.

2. Short Circuit Fault

A short circuit occurs when two points that should have a voltage difference are unintentionally connected through a low-resistance path.

Possible causes:

  • Solder bridges between PCB tracks.
  • Shorted capacitor.
  • Damaged semiconductor junction.
  • Insulation failure.
  • Incorrect wiring.

3. Intermittent Fault

An intermittent fault occurs irregularly and may disappear when the circuit is touched, heated, cooled or moved.

Possible causes:

  • Loose connectors.
  • Cracked solder joints.
  • Damaged cables.
  • Thermal expansion.
  • Mechanical vibration.

4. Component Parameter Fault

A component may still function but its electrical parameters may have changed.

Examples:

  • Resistor value drift.
  • Capacitor capacitance loss.
  • Increased capacitor ESR.
  • Reduced transistor gain.
  • Increased semiconductor leakage.

3. Systematic Troubleshooting Approach

A systematic troubleshooting method prevents unnecessary component replacement and reduces repair time.

Step 1: Understand the Problem

  • Determine what the circuit is supposed to do.
  • Identify the symptoms.
  • Check when the problem occurs.
  • Review the circuit diagram and specifications.

Step 2: Visual Inspection

  • Look for burnt components.
  • Check damaged PCB tracks.
  • Inspect connectors and wiring.
  • Look for loose or cracked solder joints.
  • Check for bulging or leaking capacitors.

Step 3: Check the Power Supply

  • Verify input voltage.
  • Check output voltage.
  • Check supply polarity.
  • Measure ripple where necessary.
  • Check for excessive current consumption.

Step 4: Measure Circuit Signals

  • Measure important node voltages.
  • Check component input and output signals.
  • Use an oscilloscope to inspect waveform behavior.
  • Compare measurements with expected values.

Step 5: Locate and Correct the Fault

  • Identify the faulty section.
  • Test suspected components.
  • Repair connections or replace defective components.
  • Check component ratings before replacement.

Step 6: Verify Operation

  • Power up under controlled conditions.
  • Verify output voltage and current.
  • Check operation under the specified load.
  • Monitor temperature and stability.

5. Voltage Measurement Troubleshooting

Voltage measurement helps identify whether the circuit receives the correct supply and whether individual stages operate correctly.

Procedure

  1. Set the multimeter to the correct voltage mode.
  2. Connect the black probe to the circuit reference ground.
  3. Connect the red probe to the test point.
  4. Record the measured voltage.
  5. Compare it with the expected value.
  6. Investigate any significant difference.

Example: 5V Regulated Supply

Expected output voltage = 5 V

Measured output voltage = 3.2 V

Voltage difference = 5 - 3.2

Difference = 1.8 V

Possible causes include excessive load, insufficient input voltage, regulator overheating, damaged regulator IC or incorrect circuit connections.

6. Resistance and Continuity Testing

Resistance and continuity tests are generally performed with the circuit switched OFF and capacitors safely discharged.

Resistance Testing

  1. Disconnect power from the circuit.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely.
  3. Select resistance mode on the multimeter.
  4. Measure across the component.
  5. Compare the reading with its nominal value and tolerance.

Continuity Testing

Continuity mode checks whether a low-resistance electrical path exists between two points.

In-circuit resistance readings can be affected by parallel components and semiconductor paths. If a reading is uncertain, isolate one component terminal before testing.

7. Signal Tracing Method

Signal tracing is a troubleshooting technique in which the signal is followed through different stages of a circuit.

Example: Amplifier Circuit

Procedure

  1. Apply a known input signal.
  2. Observe the input waveform at TP1.
  3. Check the signal after the amplifier stage at TP2.
  4. Check the output stage at TP3.
  5. Identify the first stage where the expected signal is missing or distorted.
  6. Investigate components and connections in that stage.
Signal tracing helps narrow down the faulty section instead of testing every component individually.

8. Common Resistor Faults

Resistors can fail because of overheating, excessive current, voltage stress or mechanical damage.

Fault Possible Symptom Testing Method
Open resistor No current through the intended path. Resistance measurement.
Changed resistance Incorrect bias or output voltage. Compare measured value with tolerance.
Burnt resistor Discoloration or damaged body. Visual and resistance inspection.
Intermittent connection Unstable circuit operation. Inspect terminals and solder joints.

Before replacing a burnt resistor, identify why it overheated. A shorted semiconductor or incorrect supply voltage may cause the replacement to fail again.

9. Common Capacitor Faults

Capacitors are used for filtering, coupling, decoupling, timing and energy storage.

Common Capacitor Failures

  • Open capacitor.
  • Shorted capacitor.
  • Reduced capacitance.
  • Increased ESR.
  • Electrolyte leakage.
  • Bulging or damaged capacitor body.

Testing Methods

  • Visual inspection.
  • Capacitance measurement.
  • ESR measurement.
  • Resistance behavior observation.
  • Oscilloscope ripple measurement.
Always discharge capacitors safely before resistance or capacitance testing. Large capacitors can retain dangerous energy even after equipment is switched OFF.

10. Diode and Transistor Faults

Diode Faults

  • Open diode.
  • Shorted diode.
  • Excessive reverse leakage.
  • Incorrect polarity or installation.

Use the multimeter diode-test mode to check forward conduction and reverse blocking behavior.

BJT Faults

  • Base-emitter junction short.
  • Base-collector junction open.
  • Collector-emitter short.
  • Reduced current gain.
  • Excessive leakage.

MOSFET Faults

  • Gate-source short.
  • Gate-drain short.
  • Drain-source short.
  • Damaged gate insulation.
  • Excessive ON-state resistance.

Component pinout and expected test readings must be checked against the specific manufacturer's datasheet.

11. Power Supply Troubleshooting

Power supply faults are common causes of electronic equipment malfunction.

Problem Possible Causes Checks
No output Fuse failure, open connection, failed rectifier or regulator. Inspect input, fuse and power stages.
Low output Overload, low input or regulator problem. Measure input and output under load.
High ripple Filter capacitor failure or rectifier problem. Measure ripple using an oscilloscope.
Overheating Excessive load, poor cooling or incorrect input. Check current and temperature.
Unstable output Poor connections, faulty feedback or unsuitable capacitors. Check connections and waveform stability.

12. Grounding and Ground Loop Problems

Ground provides a voltage reference and return-current path in an electronic circuit.

Incorrect grounding can produce unstable operation, measurement errors, unwanted noise and signal distortion.

Common Grounding Problems

  • Loose ground connection.
  • Broken ground PCB track.
  • High resistance in ground wiring.
  • Improper grounding layout.
  • Unwanted current flowing through shared ground paths.

Ground Loop

A ground loop can occur when equipment is connected through multiple ground paths that have different electrical potentials.

The resulting unwanted current may introduce hum or interference into sensitive signal circuits.

Troubleshooting Methods

  • Inspect ground connections.
  • Check continuity of ground paths with power OFF.
  • Use appropriate grounding and shielding practices.
  • Separate sensitive signal returns from high-current paths where the design requires it.
  • Use differential measurement techniques when appropriate.

13. Electrical Noise and Interference

Electrical noise is an unwanted signal that affects the normal operation or measurement of an electronic circuit.

Types of Noise

  • Thermal noise.
  • Shot noise.
  • Flicker noise.
  • Switching noise.
  • Electromagnetic interference (EMI).

Common Sources

  • Switch-mode power supplies.
  • Motors and relays.
  • High-frequency switching circuits.
  • Long unshielded signal cables.
  • Radio-frequency sources.

Noise Reduction Techniques

  • Use proper PCB layout.
  • Keep sensitive signal paths away from switching nodes.
  • Use suitable decoupling capacitors.
  • Use shielded cables where appropriate.
  • Reduce unnecessary ground-loop paths.
  • Use suitable filtering and grounding methods.

14. EMI and EMC Troubleshooting

Electromagnetic interference (EMI) is unwanted electromagnetic energy that can disturb electronic equipment.

Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) describes the ability of equipment to operate correctly in its electromagnetic environment without causing unacceptable interference.

Common EMI Problems

  • False switching in digital circuits.
  • Noise in analog sensor signals.
  • Unwanted resets in embedded systems.
  • Communication errors.
  • Distorted measurement signals.

Practical Solutions

  • Use proper PCB grounding and return paths.
  • Keep high-current loops small.
  • Use suitable decoupling and filtering.
  • Provide shielding where required.
  • Use appropriate cable routing.
  • Apply ferrite components where the design requires them.

15. Thermal Troubleshooting

Excessive temperature can damage semiconductors, capacitors, resistors and other electronic components.

Common Causes

  • Excessive current.
  • High power dissipation.
  • Insufficient heatsinking.
  • Poor ventilation.
  • Incorrect component selection.
  • Short circuit or overload.

Power Dissipation Example

P = V x I

Voltage across component = 5 V
Current = 0.2 A

P = 5 x 0.2

P = 1 W

The component must be selected and operated within its electrical and thermal ratings.

Thermal Inspection Methods

  • Temperature measurement using a suitable probe.
  • Thermal camera inspection.
  • Infrared thermometer.
  • Visual inspection for overheating damage.

16. Practical Troubleshooting Example

Problem: 5V Regulated Power Supply Produces Only 3V

Consider a low-voltage regulated supply using a rectifier, filter capacitor and 7805 voltage regulator.

Expected Operation

  • Input DC supply is within the regulator's operating range.
  • Filter capacitor smooths rectified voltage.
  • 7805 produces approximately 5V output within its specifications.

Diagnostic Procedure

  1. Switch OFF the supply and inspect the circuit.
  2. Check for incorrect connections and damaged components.
  3. Check for an output short circuit with power disconnected and capacitors discharged.
  4. Power the circuit using a suitable current-limited source.
  5. Measure the voltage at the regulator input.
  6. Measure the voltage at the regulator output.
  7. Measure output voltage with and without the load.
  8. Check regulator temperature and input ripple.
  9. Compare all readings with the datasheet and circuit design.

Possible Findings

Observation Possible Fault
Input voltage too low Rectifier, transformer or input supply problem.
Input correct, output low under load Overload, insufficient thermal performance or regulator fault.
Output low even without load Incorrect wiring, regulator pinout or damaged IC.
Output contains excessive ripple Filter capacitor or rectifier problem.
Output becomes normal after load removal Excessive load current or downstream short.
Do not replace the regulator immediately. First verify its input voltage, ground, output load, pin connections and thermal operating conditions.

17. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Identify a fault in a low-voltage analog circuit using a digital multimeter and oscilloscope.

Required Equipment

  • Low-voltage DC regulated power supply.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope.
  • Resistors and capacitors.
  • Diodes and transistor.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

Procedure

  1. Build a simple low-voltage circuit using a resistor, LED and transistor.
  2. Verify component polarity and connections.
  3. Apply a current-limited DC supply.
  4. Observe the circuit output.
  5. Measure supply and transistor terminal voltages.
  6. Introduce a controlled fault with power OFF, such as a disconnected wire.
  7. Apply power again and observe the symptom.
  8. Use voltage and continuity measurements to locate the fault.
  9. Switch OFF the supply and correct the connection.
  10. Verify normal circuit operation.

Observation Table

Test Expected Result Actual Result
Supply voltage As per circuit design Record
LED operation Normal ON/OFF response Record
Transistor voltage Depends on switching state Record
Continuity Correct electrical connections Record
Final operation Normal circuit operation Record

Expected Result

The learner should be able to identify the introduced fault using systematic measurements and restore normal circuit operation.

18. Troubleshooting Best Practices

  • Understand the circuit before testing.
  • Read the component datasheets.
  • Start with visual inspection.
  • Check the power supply before signal stages.
  • Use the correct measurement range and instrument.
  • Record measured values.
  • Compare actual readings with expected values.
  • Change one thing at a time where practical.
  • Identify the root cause before replacing components.
  • Verify operation under normal load conditions.

20. Lesson Summary

  • Troubleshooting identifies and corrects electronic circuit faults.
  • Common faults include open, short, intermittent and parameter faults.
  • Visual inspection is an important first step.
  • Voltage, resistance and continuity measurements help locate faults.
  • Signal tracing identifies the faulty circuit stage.
  • Capacitors, resistors, diodes and transistors can fail in different ways.
  • Grounding problems and EMI can cause unstable circuit operation.
  • Thermal inspection helps identify overheating components.
  • Root-cause analysis prevents repeated failures.
Placement Tip: Practice troubleshooting a 5V regulated supply, LED driver and transistor switching circuit. Be prepared to explain your measurement sequence and why you selected each test instrument.

21. Electrical Safety Precautions

  • Disconnect power before resistance and continuity testing.
  • Discharge capacitors safely before handling the circuit.
  • Use a current-limited power supply for initial testing.
  • Check multimeter probe connections before measuring.
  • Never measure resistance on an energized circuit.
  • Use correctly rated test instruments and probes.
  • Avoid touching exposed conductors during powered testing.
  • Use suitable protective equipment and follow laboratory procedures.
Mains-powered and high-voltage circuits require appropriate training, isolation, rated instruments and safety procedures. Do not troubleshoot energized mains circuits using ordinary low-voltage laboratory methods.
