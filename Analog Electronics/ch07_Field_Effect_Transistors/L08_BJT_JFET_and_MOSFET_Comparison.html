Lesson 8: BJT, JFET and MOSFET Comparison

Understand the differences in construction, operation, input impedance, characteristics, biasing and practical applications of semiconductor transistors.

1. Introduction

BJTs, JFETs and MOSFETs are semiconductor devices used for amplification, switching and signal control. Although all three can control current, they differ in their construction, controlling mechanism, input characteristics and operating requirements.

  • BJT: Bipolar Junction Transistor.
  • JFET: Junction Field Effect Transistor.
  • MOSFET: Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor.
The most important distinction is that a BJT is a current-controlled device in its basic operating model, while JFETs and MOSFETs are voltage-controlled devices.

2. Basic Construction Comparison

A. BJT Construction

A BJT consists of three semiconductor regions arranged as emitter, base and collector. It contains two PN junctions and uses both electrons and holes in its operation.

B. JFET Construction

A JFET consists of a semiconductor channel between source and drain. A reverse-biased PN junction gate controls the channel width.

C. MOSFET Construction

A MOSFET contains source and drain regions separated by a semiconductor channel region. An insulated gate controls channel conductivity through an electric field.

Simplified comparison of the semiconductor structures of BJT, JFET and MOSFET devices.

4. Current Equations and Important Parameters

A. BJT Current Relationship

In a BJT operating in the forward-active region, collector current is approximately proportional to base current.

IC ≈ β IB

IE = IC + IB

β = IC / IB

B. JFET Drain Current

For a common N-channel JFET operating in its saturation region, the Shockley equation gives the approximate drain current.

ID = IDSS (1 − VGS / VGS(off))²

Where IDSS is the drain current at VGS = 0 and VGS(off) is the signed gate-source cutoff voltage for the N-channel device.

C. MOSFET Drain Current

For an ideal long-channel N-channel enhancement MOSFET in saturation:

ID = (kn / 2)(VGS − VTH)²

Valid when VGS > VTH and VDS ≥ VGS − VTH.

5. Input Impedance Comparison

Input impedance determines how much current a device draws from the preceding circuit.

Device Input Characteristics Practical Implication
BJT Base-emitter junction is forward biased in normal active operation. Requires base drive current.
JFET Gate-source junction is normally reverse biased. Very small gate current and high input impedance.
MOSFET Gate is insulated by a dielectric. Very high DC input resistance, with capacitive gate-drive requirements.
High input resistance does not mean that a MOSFET can be switched instantly without current. Its gate capacitance must be charged and discharged during switching.

7. Advantages and Disadvantages

A. BJT

Advantages
  • Good transconductance for a given bias current.
  • Can provide high gain in analog amplifier circuits.
  • Widely used in discrete analog electronics.
Disadvantages
  • Requires base drive current.
  • Input impedance is generally lower than that of FET devices.
  • Thermal stability and biasing require attention.

B. JFET

Advantages
  • High input impedance.
  • Low gate current under normal reverse-bias conditions.
  • Useful for low-noise and high-input-impedance circuits.
Disadvantages
  • Normally-on behavior can complicate some switching applications.
  • Limited range of available device types compared with MOSFETs.
  • Gate voltage must remain within the rated junction limits.

C. MOSFET

Advantages
  • Very high DC input resistance.
  • Low gate-drive power in steady state.
  • Available in enhancement and depletion modes.
  • Suitable for high-density integrated circuits.
  • Excellent switching capabilities in many power applications.
Disadvantages
  • Gate oxide can be damaged by excessive voltage or ESD.
  • Gate capacitance affects switching performance.
  • Power MOSFETs may require heat sinks and careful thermal design.

8. Practical Applications Comparison

Application Common Device Choice Reason

Small-signal amplification BJT, JFET or MOSFET Choice depends on noise, impedance, gain and power requirements.

9. Numerical Comparison – BJT and MOSFET Drive

A. BJT Base Current

Given:

  • Required collector current IC = 100 mA
  • Assumed forced beta = 10
IB = IC / forced beta

IB = 100 mA / 10

IB = 10 mA

The controller or driver must provide the required base current while keeping the transistor within its ratings.

B. MOSFET Gate Drive

A MOSFET does not require continuous ideal DC gate current to remain ON. However, gate charge must be supplied during switching.

Approximate average gate-drive current:

IG(avg) ≈ QG / tsw

Where QG is total gate charge and tsw is the charging time.

For example, if total gate charge is 20 nC and the desired charging time is 100 ns:

IG(avg) = 20 nC / 100 ns

IG(avg) = 0.2 A

This is an approximate average current during the charging interval, not a continuous DC current. Actual gate-driver design must consider the gate-charge curve, Miller plateau, driver resistance and switching waveform.

10. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is the main difference between BJT and FET?

BJT operation involves both electrons and holes and is commonly controlled by base current. FET operation uses majority carriers and is controlled by gate voltage.

Q2. Why is a MOSFET called a unipolar device?

Because its channel current is primarily carried by one type of majority carrier: electrons in NMOS or holes in PMOS.

Q3. Which device has the highest DC input impedance?

A MOSFET generally has extremely high DC input resistance because its gate is insulated from the semiconductor.

Q4. Why does a BJT require base current?

Base current supports carrier injection and transport through the transistor in normal forward-active operation.

Q5. What is the difference between JFET and MOSFET gate construction?

A JFET uses a reverse-biased PN junction gate, whereas a MOSFET uses an insulated gate separated from the semiconductor by a dielectric.

Q6. Which transistor is widely used in CMOS technology?

Enhancement-mode NMOS and PMOS transistors.

Q7. Why are power MOSFETs used in switching converters?

Suitable power MOSFETs offer low ON resistance, high switching performance and convenient voltage-controlled gate drive.

Q8. Can a MOSFET gate draw current?

It draws transient current while its capacitance is charged or discharged, and small leakage may exist in practical devices.

Q9. What is the major thermal concern in BJT circuits?

Increasing temperature can increase collector current and cause thermal runaway if the circuit lacks suitable stabilization.

Q10. Which transistor would you select for a high-impedance sensor input?

A JFET or MOSFET may be suitable because of its high input impedance. The final choice depends on noise, leakage, bandwidth and voltage requirements.

12. Lesson Summary

  • BJT is a bipolar device; JFET and MOSFET are unipolar devices.
  • BJT is commonly treated as a current-controlled device.
  • JFET and MOSFET are voltage-controlled devices.
  • JFET uses a reverse-biased PN junction gate.
  • MOSFET uses an insulated gate.
  • MOSFET has very high DC input resistance, but its gate capacitance affects switching.
  • BJT requires base drive current during normal operation.
  • JFETs are useful in high-input-impedance and low-noise circuits.
  • MOSFETs are widely used in CMOS logic, switching circuits and power electronics.
  • Device selection depends on gain, impedance, noise, speed, power and thermal requirements.
