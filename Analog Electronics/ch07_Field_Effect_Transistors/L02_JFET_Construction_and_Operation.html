2. Introduction to JFET

A Junction Field Effect Transistor (JFET) is a three-terminal, voltage-controlled semiconductor device. It uses a reverse-biased PN junction to control the width and conductivity of a semiconductor channel.

The three terminals are:

Gate (G)A semiconductor region forming a PN junction with the channel. It controls the channel using reverse bias.
Source (S)The terminal through which majority carriers enter the channel under the usual operating convention.
Drain (D)The terminal through which majority carriers leave the channel under the usual operating convention.
Important: In normal JFET operation, the gate-channel PN junction is reverse biased. Therefore, gate current is very small, apart from reverse leakage.

3. Construction of N-Channel JFET

An N-channel JFET consists of a narrow N-type semiconductor bar that forms the conducting channel. Two heavily doped P-type regions are formed on opposite sides of the N-type channel. These P-type regions are electrically connected to form the gate terminal.

  • The N-type bar forms the channel.
  • The two P-type regions form the gate junction.
  • Ohmic contacts at the two ends form the source and drain.
  • The gate-channel PN junction is normally reverse biased.

The two P-type gate regions are connected together. Their junction with the N-type channel forms the gate-channel PN junction.

4. Construction of P-Channel JFET

A P-channel JFET uses a P-type semiconductor bar as the channel. Two N-type regions are formed on opposite sides of the channel and connected together to create the gate.

  • P-type material forms the channel.
  • N-type regions form the gate.
  • Holes are the majority carriers.
  • The gate-channel junction is reverse biased during normal operation.

5. PN-Junction and Depletion Region

When a P-type gate region forms a PN junction with an N-type channel, a depletion region develops around the junction. This region contains very few mobile charge carriers.

When the gate-channel junction is reverse biased:

  1. The reverse-bias voltage increases the electric field across the PN junction.
  2. The depletion region becomes wider.
  3. The effective conducting channel becomes narrower.
  4. The channel resistance increases.
  5. For a given drain-source voltage, drain current decreases.
More reverse gate bias → Wider depletion region → Narrower channel → Lower ID

The depletion region is not a physical insulating sheet. It is a region depleted of mobile carriers, with fixed ionized dopants and an internal electric field.

6. Biasing of an N-Channel JFET

For the usual N-channel JFET operating condition:

  • The drain is at a positive voltage relative to the source.
  • The gate is at a voltage equal to or lower than the source.
  • The gate-channel PN junction is reverse biased.
  • Electrons drift from source toward drain.
  • Conventional drain current ID flows from drain toward source.
The gate must be negative relative to the source for an N-channel JFET under normal reverse-bias operation. At VGS = 0, the gate junction is not forward biased, and the device conducts according to its drain-source voltage.

7. Operation of N-Channel JFET

Case 1: VGS = 0 and small VDS

With zero gate-to-source voltage, the gate-channel junction has no externally applied reverse bias relative to the source. As VDS increases, the reverse bias across the gate-channel junction becomes greater near the drain end because the channel potential rises toward the drain. The depletion region becomes wider near the drain than near the source.

Case 2: Increasing VDS

The depletion region expands more strongly near the drain end. The conducting channel narrows along its length, and drain current increases less rapidly as the device approaches pinch-off.

Case 3: Pinch-off

At a sufficiently large drain-source voltage for a given gate voltage, the channel becomes strongly constricted near the drain end. The drain current approaches a nearly constant value in the saturation region. This condition is called pinch-off.

Case 4: Applying negative VGS

A negative gate-source voltage increases reverse bias along the gate-channel junction. The depletion region becomes wider, reducing the available channel width and lowering drain current. A sufficiently negative VGS brings the device close to cutoff.

Important distinction: JFET pinch-off is the onset of the drain-current saturation region as VDS increases. It does not mean drain current becomes zero. The gate-source cutoff voltage is the gate voltage at which the channel is effectively cut off. In the Shockley model, this is represented by VGS(off) or VP.

10. JFET Pinch-Off and Cutoff

Pinch-off voltage

Pinch-off is the drain-source operating condition at which the channel becomes sufficiently constricted near the drain end that the drain current approaches saturation.

Gate-source cutoff voltage

The gate-source voltage at which the channel is effectively cut off. For an N-channel JFET, it is negative relative to the source. Datasheets may specify this parameter as VGS(off) or VGS(cutoff).

For an N-channel JFET: VGS(off) is negative
Some textbooks use VP for both a pinch-off-related voltage magnitude and a gate cutoff voltage, depending on the stated convention. Always identify whether VP is signed or specified as a positive magnitude.

11. Worked Numerical Questions

Example 1: Calculate VGS

Gate voltage VG = −2 V and source voltage VS = 1 V.

VGS = VG − VS = −2 − 1 = −3 V

Example 2: Calculate VDS

Drain voltage VD = 10 V and source voltage VS = 2 V.

VDS = VD − VS = 10 − 2 = 8 V

Example 3: Determine gate bias

An N-channel JFET has source voltage 0 V and gate voltage −1.5 V.

VGS = −1.5 V

The gate-channel PN junction is reverse biased under the usual operating conditions.

Example 4: Estimate gate current

A reverse-biased JFET gate junction has a specified leakage current of 20 nA. What is the gate current?

IG ≈ 20 nA

This small leakage is not exactly zero. The value depends on device specification and temperature.

12. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective

Observe how gate-source voltage changes the drain current of an N-channel JFET.

Equipment

  • N-channel JFET with datasheet.
  • Current-limited DC supply.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Drain and gate resistors selected according to the device ratings.
  • Breadboard or suitable test fixture.

Procedure

  1. Identify the JFET terminals using its datasheet.
  2. Connect the source to the circuit reference node.
  3. Apply a positive drain-source supply through a suitable current-limiting resistor.
  4. Set the gate voltage initially to the source potential.
  5. Measure VGS, VDS and drain current.
  6. Apply progressively more negative VGS values.
  7. Record the corresponding drain currents.
  8. Plot ID against VGS and observe the decrease in drain current.

14. Placement Interview Questions

1. What is a JFET?

A voltage-controlled semiconductor device that uses a reverse-biased PN-junction gate to control channel current.

2. Name the terminals of a JFET.

Gate, source and drain.

3. What forms the channel in an N-channel JFET?

An N-type semiconductor bar.

4. What is the gate material in an N-channel JFET?

P-type semiconductor regions.

5. Which carriers conduct in an N-channel JFET?

Electrons.

6. Why is the gate junction reverse biased?

To control depletion-region width while keeping gate current very small.

7. What happens when negative VGS increases in magnitude?

The depletion region widens, the channel narrows and drain current decreases.

8. What is pinch-off?

The channel-constriction condition near the drain end at which drain current approaches saturation as VDS increases.

9. Does drain current become zero at pinch-off?

No. Pinch-off marks the onset of the current-saturation region; the current remains nonzero.

10. What is gate cutoff voltage?

The gate-source voltage at which the channel is effectively cut off.

11. What are the majority carriers in a P-channel JFET?

Holes.

12. What is the main difference between JFET and MOSFET gate structures?

A JFET uses a PN-junction gate; a MOSFET uses an insulated gate.

13. Why is JFET gate current small?

The gate-channel PN junction is reverse biased during normal operation.

14. What happens if a JFET gate junction is forward biased?

Gate current increases significantly and the device is no longer operating under its normal reverse-biased gate condition.

15. What is IDSS?

The drain current at VGS = 0 when the device is in its specified drain-current saturation condition.

16. Lecture Summary

  • A JFET is a three-terminal voltage-controlled device.
  • The gate forms a PN junction with the channel.
  • The gate-channel junction is normally reverse biased.
  • Reverse bias increases depletion-region width.
  • Increasing depletion width reduces the effective channel width.
  • N-channel JFETs conduct primarily through electrons.
  • P-channel JFETs conduct primarily through holes.
  • Pinch-off marks the onset of drain-current saturation, not zero current.
  • Gate cutoff voltage determines when the channel is effectively switched off.
