2. Why MOSFET Drivers Are Required

A MOSFET gate behaves approximately as a capacitive load. A controller GPIO can sometimes directly drive a small MOSFET, but the GPIO must be capable of charging and discharging the gate sufficiently fast and to the required voltage.

Gate-driver functions

  • Provide adequate gate voltage.
  • Supply and remove gate charge quickly.
  • Reduce switching time when appropriate.
  • Improve switching control.
  • Help manage switching losses and EMI.
  • Provide additional protection in some driver ICs.
A gate driver is not mandatory for every MOSFET. A GPIO can directly drive a suitable MOSFET when the drive voltage, current, switching speed, GPIO capability and circuit conditions are appropriate.

3. Low-Side NMOS Driver Circuit

A low-side NMOS is commonly used to switch a DC load. The MOSFET is connected between the load and ground.

Working

  1. GPIO LOW: MOSFET OFF.
  2. GPIO HIGH: MOSFET turns ON if gate drive is adequate.
  3. Current flows through the load, drain, MOSFET channel and ground.
  4. Gate resistor influences switching transitions.
  5. Pull-down resistor ensures the gate remains LOW when the driver is disconnected or high impedance.

4. Gate Resistor Selection

A series gate resistor is used to influence the transient gate current and switching behavior.

A simplified initial peak current estimate is:

IG ≈ VDRIVE/RG

This is only a rough initial estimate. The actual current depends on driver output impedance, gate voltage, MOSFET capacitance and gate-charge behavior.

Example

  • Driver voltage = 5V
  • Gate resistor = 50Ω

IG ≈ 5/50

IG ≈ 0.1A = 100mA

This is a simplified estimate of initial drive current, not continuous current.

Factors affecting RG

  • MOSFET gate charge.
  • Driver peak-current capability.
  • Switching frequency.
  • Desired transition time.
  • Ringing and EMI.
  • PCB layout and parasitic inductance.

5. Gate Pull-Down Resistor

A pull-down resistor is connected between gate and source in a low-side NMOS circuit.

Purpose

  • Prevents gate floating.
  • Provides a defined OFF state.
  • Discharges the gate when the controller output becomes high impedance.
  • Helps ensure predictable startup and reset behavior.

Typical starting values

10kΩ to 100kΩ may be used in many circuits, but the value depends on leakage, driver behavior, switching requirements, noise and power consumption.

6. High-Side Switching

High-side switching places the transistor between the positive supply and the load.

PMOS high-side switch

  • PMOS source is commonly connected to the positive supply.
  • Gate near source voltage: OFF.
  • Gate pulled sufficiently below source: ON.
  • Gate control must be referenced to the source.

N-channel high-side switching

An N-channel MOSFET on the high side may require the gate voltage to rise above the source voltage to turn ON. A dedicated high-side driver, bootstrap circuit or charge pump may be required, depending on topology.

Do not assume an N-channel MOSFET high-side switch can be fully driven by a ground-referenced GPIO. The required VGS must be maintained relative to the moving source voltage.

7. MOSFET Switching an LED

LED resistor calculation

R = (VS − VF − VDS)/ILED

Example

  • Supply = 5V
  • LED forward voltage = 2V
  • Target current = 10mA
  • MOSFET ON voltage drop assumed negligible for this estimate

R ≈ (5 − 2)/0.01

R ≈ 300Ω

A standard resistor such as 330Ω could be considered, with the actual LED current verified.

8. MOSFET Switching a Relay

A relay coil is inductive. When current through the coil is interrupted, the collapsing magnetic field can produce a voltage transient.

Relay driver operation

  1. GPIO HIGH drives the MOSFET gate.
  2. MOSFET turns ON.
  3. Current flows through the relay coil.
  4. Relay energizes.
  5. When MOSFET turns OFF, the flyback diode provides a current path for the coil current as it decays.
The flyback diode must be connected with its cathode to the coil's positive supply and its anode to the low-side switching node. Incorrect polarity can cause a supply short. Select diode current and reverse-voltage ratings appropriately. A simple diode may slow relay release; other suppression methods may be needed when faster release is required.

9. MOSFET Switching a DC Motor

DC motors are inductive loads. Low-side MOSFET switching is commonly used for simple ON/OFF control and PWM control.

Requirements

  • MOSFET rated for motor voltage and current.
  • Suitable gate drive.
  • Transient protection.
  • Current and thermal margin.
  • Appropriate wiring and decoupling.
  • Flyback diode or other suitable suppression when appropriate to the topology.

For a brushed DC motor controlled with PWM, the motor's inductance and mechanical inertia help smooth current and speed response. The switching circuit still needs suitable protection and current handling.

10. Flyback Diode Operation

Protection options

  • Flyback diode.
  • TVS diode.
  • RC snubber.
  • Other application-specific suppression networks.

Protection choice depends on the load, switching speed, voltage ratings and desired transient behavior.

11. PWM Load Control

PWM switches the MOSFET rapidly to control average power delivered to the load.

For an ideal switch with a resistive load, the average switched voltage is approximately D × VSupply. The actual load response depends on its characteristics.

12. Gate-Drive Power

Gate-drive power is approximately:

Pgate ≈ Qg × VGS × f

Example

  • Qg = 40nC
  • VGS = 10V
  • f = 100kHz

Pgate = 40 × 10⁻⁹ × 10 × 100 × 10³

Pgate ≈ 0.04W

This represents an approximate gate-drive energy requirement per second. It does not include all driver losses or other MOSFET losses.

13. MOSFET Conduction Loss

Pcond = ID² × RDS(on)

Example

  • Load current = 4A
  • RDS(on) = 40mΩ

Pcond = 4² × 0.04

Pcond = 16 × 0.04

Pcond = 0.64W

ON-state voltage drop:

VDS = ID × RDS(on) = 4 × 0.04 = 0.16V

14. Switching Loss

Approximate switching-overlap loss:

Pswitch ≈ ½ × VDS × ID × (tr + tf) × f

Example

  • VDS = 12V
  • ID = 3A
  • tr + tf = 100ns
  • f = 20kHz

Pswitch ≈ 0.5 × 12 × 3 × 100 × 10⁻⁹ × 20 × 10³

Pswitch ≈ 0.036W

Actual switching loss depends on waveforms, gate drive, load, parasitics, reverse recovery, Miller effect and circuit layout.

16. MOSFET Selection Checklist

  • VDS rating with appropriate margin.
  • Drain current and realistic thermal conditions.
  • RDS(on) at actual VGS.
  • Qg and driver capability.
  • Maximum VGS rating.
  • Package and thermal resistance.
  • Safe operating area.
  • Body diode behavior.
  • Transient protection requirements.
  • Load type: resistive, inductive or capacitive.
  • Switching frequency and desired speed.
  • PCB layout, grounding and decoupling.

17. Practical Lab: Relay Driver

Objective

Build and test a low-side NMOS relay driver using a flyback diode.

Components

  • Logic-level NMOS.
  • Low-voltage DC relay.
  • Flyback diode.
  • Gate resistor.
  • Gate pull-down resistor.
  • GPIO or low-voltage signal source.
  • Low-voltage DC supply.
  • Multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Identify MOSFET pinout from datasheet.
  2. Connect source to circuit ground.
  3. Connect relay coil between positive supply and drain.
  4. Connect flyback diode across the relay coil with cathode to positive and anode to drain.
  5. Connect gate through the series gate resistor.
  6. Connect pull-down between gate and source.
  7. Apply a LOW gate signal and observe relay OFF.
  8. Apply a HIGH gate signal and observe relay activation.
  9. Measure VGS and VDS.
  10. Observe the behavior when switching the relay OFF.

Expected result

  • Gate LOW: relay OFF.
  • Gate HIGH: relay ON if the MOSFET is adequately driven.
  • Flyback diode helps limit the coil turn-off voltage.
  • Proper gate resistors ensure predictable control.
Use low-voltage DC only. Do not use mains voltage. Observe relay, diode, MOSFET and supply ratings.

21. Summary

  • Gate drivers provide adequate gate voltage and current for switching.
  • Low-side NMOS switching is a common load-control topology.
  • PMOS devices can simplify high-side switching.
  • N-channel high-side switching often requires a specialized driver.
  • Gate resistor controls switching behavior.
  • Pull-down resistors prevent floating gates.
  • Flyback diodes protect against inductive turn-off transients.
  • Conduction, switching and gate-drive losses must be evaluated.
  • Thermal design and transient protection are important.
