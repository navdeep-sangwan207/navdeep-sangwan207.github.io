Lesson 9: Power MOSFETs and Practical Applications

Understand power MOSFET construction, datasheet parameters, conduction and switching losses, gate driving, thermal design and real-world applications.

1. Introduction to Power MOSFETs

A power MOSFET is a MOSFET designed to control relatively high voltages and currents in power electronic circuits. It is widely used as an electronic switch in DC-DC converters, motor drives, battery systems, inverters and power supplies.

Unlike a small-signal MOSFET, a power MOSFET is designed with a structure and package suitable for handling greater electrical power and dissipating heat.

Important characteristics:
  • High drain-current capability.
  • Specified drain-to-source voltage rating.
  • Low ON-state resistance in suitable devices.
  • Voltage-controlled insulated gate.
  • Fast switching capability when properly driven.

2. Power MOSFET Construction

Most discrete power MOSFETs use a vertical structure. Current flows mainly vertically through the semiconductor die, allowing a large current-carrying area.

An N-channel power MOSFET commonly contains source regions near the top surface, a gate structure, a body region and a vertical drift region leading toward the drain at the bottom.

Simplified conceptual cross-section. Actual power MOSFET cells contain repeated structures and detailed body-contact regions.

Important Regions

Region Function
Source Supplies majority carriers to the channel.
Gate Controls channel formation through an electric field.
Body Forms part of the MOSFET cell structure and contains the source region.
Drift region Supports much of the OFF-state drain voltage.
Drain Provides the drain-current connection.

3. Types of Power MOSFETs

A. N-Channel Power MOSFET

  • Uses electrons as majority carriers in the conducting channel.
  • Turns ON when the gate is sufficiently positive relative to the source.
  • Widely used in low-side switching and power converters.
  • Often offers lower RDS(on) than a comparable P-channel device with similar voltage rating and die area.

B. P-Channel Power MOSFET

  • Uses holes as majority carriers in the conducting channel.
  • Turns ON when the gate is sufficiently negative relative to the source.
  • Commonly used for high-side switching in simpler circuits.
  • Often has higher ON resistance than a comparable N-channel device.

4. Important Power MOSFET Datasheet Parameters

A datasheet provides the electrical and thermal limits needed to select a MOSFET for a practical circuit.

Parameter Meaning Design Importance
VDS(max) Maximum drain-to-source voltage Must exceed the maximum expected drain voltage, including transients.
ID Rated continuous drain current under specified conditions Check temperature, package and thermal limitations.
RDS(on) ON-state drain-to-source resistance Determines conduction loss.
VGS(max) Maximum permitted gate-to-source voltage Must not be exceeded.
VGS(th) Gate threshold voltage Indicates the beginning of channel conduction at a small test current, not full enhancement.
Qg Total gate charge Important for gate-driver sizing and switching speed.
Ciss Input capacitance Provides a useful small-signal indication of gate loading.
Qrr Body-diode reverse-recovery charge Important in some hard-switching converter topologies.
RθJC Junction-to-case thermal resistance Used for thermal calculations.
Never select a MOSFET using only its maximum current rating. Check RDS(on) at the actual gate voltage, package thermal limits, safe operating area and switching characteristics.

5. Conduction Loss in a Power MOSFET

When a MOSFET is ON, current flows through its channel resistance. This produces conduction power loss.

Pcond = ID² × RDS(on)

VDS(on) = ID × RDS(on)

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Drain current = 10 A
  • RDS(on) = 0.02 Ω

Step 1: Calculate conduction loss.

Pcond = 10² × 0.02

Pcond = 2 W

Step 2: Calculate ON-state voltage drop.

VDS(on) = 10 × 0.02

VDS(on) = 0.2 V

The example assumes the stated RDS(on) applies at the actual gate voltage and junction temperature. In practice, RDS(on) increases as junction temperature rises.

6. Switching Losses

During switching transitions, the MOSFET may simultaneously have significant drain current and drain-to-source voltage. This causes switching power loss.

Psw ≈ ½ × VDS × ID × (tr + tf) × fs

Where:

  • VDS = Drain-to-source voltage
  • ID = Drain current
  • tr = Current/voltage transition rise time used in the approximation
  • tf = Current/voltage transition fall time used in the approximation
  • fs = Switching frequency

This is a simplified hard-switching estimate. Actual switching loss depends on the voltage and current waveforms, gate resistance, gate charge, load type, parasitic inductance and capacitance.

Other Switching-Related Losses

  • Gate-drive loss.
  • Body-diode conduction loss.
  • Body-diode reverse-recovery loss.
  • Output-capacitance charging and discharging loss.
  • Parasitic ringing and transient losses.

7. Gate Driver Circuit

A gate driver supplies the current needed to charge and discharge the MOSFET gate capacitance. It helps control switching speed and allows the MOSFET to reach its intended gate voltage.

A gate driver provides the gate voltage and transient current needed to switch a power MOSFET effectively.

Why Is a Gate Driver Required?

  • To provide sufficient gate charging and discharging current.
  • To improve switching speed where required.
  • To reduce excessive switching losses.
  • To drive MOSFETs whose gate voltage is higher than the controller supply.
  • To provide level shifting or isolation in suitable topologies.
Approximate gate-drive power:

Pgate ≈ QG × Vdrive × fs

Here QG is the total gate charge per switching cycle, Vdrive is the gate-drive voltage swing and fs is the switching frequency.

8. Thermal Management and Heat Sink Selection

Power MOSFETs generate heat because of conduction and switching losses. If junction temperature becomes excessive, the device may become unreliable or fail.

TJ = TA + PD × RθJA

Where:

  • TJ = Junction temperature
  • TA = Ambient temperature
  • PD = Total power dissipation
  • RθJA = Junction-to-ambient thermal resistance

Thermal Example

Given:

  • Ambient temperature = 40°C
  • Power dissipation = 2 W
  • Junction-to-ambient thermal resistance = 30°C/W
TJ = 40 + (2 × 30)

TJ = 100°C

The calculated junction temperature must remain below the maximum junction temperature specified in the datasheet, with a suitable design margin.

Thermal Resistance Path

Heat flows from the semiconductor junction through the package and heat sink into the surrounding air.

When a heat sink is used, the approximate thermal relationship is:

TJ = TA + PD(RθJC + RθCS + RθSA)

9. Body Diode in Power MOSFETs

A conventional discrete power MOSFET contains an intrinsic body diode due to its internal semiconductor structure. For a typical N-channel device, the diode conducts from source toward drain when forward biased.

For a typical N-channel discrete power MOSFET, the body-diode anode is at the source and its cathode is at the drain.

The body diode can conduct during certain switching intervals, especially in inductive and synchronous converter circuits. Its forward voltage, reverse-recovery behavior and thermal limits must be considered.

10. Practical Applications of Power MOSFETs

A. DC-DC Converters

Power MOSFETs act as high-speed switches in buck, boost and other switching converter topologies.

B. Motor Drivers

MOSFETs are used in motor driver circuits to control motor current, direction and speed. H-bridge circuits commonly use four switching devices.

C. Inverters

MOSFETs switch DC power to generate an AC waveform in suitable low- and medium-voltage inverter applications.

D. Battery Management and Protection

MOSFETs are used for battery isolation, charge/discharge control, reverse-polarity protection and electronic circuit protection.

E. Relay and Solenoid Drivers

An NMOS low-side switch can control relay coils and solenoids. Suitable flyback or clamp protection is required.

11. Practical MOSFET Selection Checklist

  1. Determine the maximum supply and transient voltage.
  2. Select a suitable VDS rating with design margin.
  3. Calculate maximum continuous and peak drain current.
  4. Check RDS(on) at the actual gate-drive voltage and temperature.
  5. Check total gate charge and switching frequency.
  6. Check package thermal resistance and heat-sink requirements.
  7. Review safe operating area and pulsed current limitations.
  8. Check body-diode and reverse-recovery characteristics.
  9. Verify VGS(max) and provide suitable gate protection.
  10. Confirm that the device is appropriate for the circuit topology.

12. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is a power MOSFET?

A power MOSFET is a MOSFET designed to switch or control relatively high voltages and currents in power electronic circuits.

Q2. What is RDS(on)?

It is the drain-to-source resistance of a properly enhanced MOSFET in its ON state.

Q3. What is conduction loss?

It is the power dissipated due to current flowing through the MOSFET's ON-state resistance. It is approximately ID²RDS(on).

Q4. What is switching loss?

It is power dissipated during switching transitions when the MOSFET has both drain current and drain-to-source voltage.

Q5. Why is a gate driver used?

It provides the required gate voltage and transient current to charge and discharge the gate efficiently.

Q6. What is a body diode?

It is the intrinsic PN junction diode present in a conventional discrete power MOSFET structure.

Q7. Why is a heat sink required?

A heat sink reduces thermal resistance to ambient and helps maintain the junction temperature within safe limits.

Q8. What is gate charge?

Gate charge is the amount of charge required to change the MOSFET gate voltage and establish the desired switching state.

Q9. What is a logic-level MOSFET?

It is a MOSFET whose datasheet specifies suitable ON resistance at relatively low gate-drive voltages.

Q10. Why does RDS(on) increase with temperature?

In typical power MOSFETs, increased junction temperature raises channel resistance, increasing conduction loss.

14. Lesson Summary

  • Power MOSFETs are designed for switching and controlling electrical power.
  • Most discrete power MOSFETs use a vertical semiconductor structure.
  • Important ratings include VDS, ID, RDS(on), VGS(max) and gate charge.
  • Conduction loss is approximately ID²RDS(on).
  • Switching loss occurs during voltage and current transitions.
  • Gate drivers provide the required gate charging and discharging current.
  • Thermal design is essential for reliable operation.
  • The intrinsic body diode affects switching behavior in practical circuits.
  • Power MOSFETs are used in converters, motor drives, inverters and battery systems.
  • Always verify datasheet ratings and safe operating conditions before selection.
