2. MOSFET Biasing

Biasing establishes the DC operating conditions of a MOSFET. The gate-source voltage controls the channel and influences drain current.

Important DC quantities

  • VGS: Gate-source voltage.
  • VDS: Drain-source voltage.
  • ID: Drain current.
  • VTH: Threshold voltage.
  • VOV: Overdrive voltage.

For an enhancement NMOS, a gate voltage sufficiently above the threshold voltage is required to establish channel conduction. The exact current depends on device parameters and operating conditions.

The drawing is conceptual. A complete bias circuit also needs a gate bias source or network, and the gate must not be left floating.

3. Enhancement MOSFET Biasing

In enhancement-mode MOSFETs, the channel is induced by the gate voltage.

NMOS

  • Positive VGS is required to create an inversion channel in an enhancement NMOS.
  • VGS below threshold ideally means cutoff.
  • Increasing VGS generally increases drain current.

PMOS

  • Negative VGS relative to its source is used to turn on an enhancement PMOS.
  • The source is commonly connected toward the positive supply in high-side circuits.
  • Gate voltage must be controlled relative to the source, not relative to ground alone.

4. DC Load Line

For an NMOS connected to a drain resistor RD and supply VDD:

VDD = ID RD + VDS

VDS = VDD − ID RD

When ID = 0:

VDS = VDD

When VDS = 0, the ideal resistor-limited current intercept is:

ID = VDD/RD

The Q-point is the actual operating point determined by the device characteristics, gate bias and external circuit.

5. MOSFET as a Switch

A MOSFET is widely used as an electronic switch. In switching applications, the device is generally designed to operate between cutoff and a low-resistance ON-state.

Switch stateGate conditionDrain condition
OFFVGS below turn-on levelID approximately zero except leakage
ONGate driven adequatelyLow VDS when operated with low RDS(on)
Important distinction: In a MOSFET switch, the desired ON state is generally in the low-VDS ohmic/triode region. MOSFET saturation, as defined by the analog transistor model, is not synonymous with a fully ON switching state.

6. Low-Side NMOS Switching Circuit

A low-side NMOS switch controls a load connected between the positive supply and the MOSFET drain.

Working

  1. GPIO output is LOW: VGS is approximately zero and the MOSFET is OFF.
  2. GPIO output is HIGH: VGS increases and the MOSFET turns ON if the device is suitable for that gate voltage.
  3. Load current flows from supply through the load, into the drain, through the channel and to ground.

7. Gate Resistor and Pull-Down Resistor

Gate resistor (RG)

A resistor placed in series with the gate can limit transient gate current, control switching speed and reduce ringing or EMI.

Gate pull-down resistor

A resistor from gate to source keeps the MOSFET OFF when the driver or MCU pin is disconnected, reset or high impedance.

Typical conceptual circuit values may use a gate resistor of 10–100Ω and a pull-down resistor of 10–100kΩ. These are starting values only and must be selected based on the actual circuit.

The MOSFET gate is capacitive. It does not behave like a steady DC current input, but the gate driver must supply and remove charge during switching.

9. PWM Switching

Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) controls the average power delivered to a load by changing the fraction of time the switch is ON.

Duty cycle = TON/T × 100%

For a simple resistive load switched by an ideal transistor, the average voltage is approximately:

VAVG = D × VSupply

where D is duty cycle as a fraction between 0 and 1.

For motors, LEDs and other loads, the relationship between PWM duty cycle and perceived brightness, speed or power depends on load dynamics and the circuit. VAVG alone may not describe the actual load response.

10. Conduction Loss

When the MOSFET is fully driven in its low-resistance ON state:

Pcond = ID² × RDS(on)

Example

  • ID = 5A
  • RDS(on) = 30mΩ = 0.03Ω

Pcond = 5² × 0.03

Pcond = 25 × 0.03

Pcond = 0.75W

ON-state voltage drop:

VDS = ID × RDS(on) = 5 × 0.03 = 0.15V

11. Switching Loss

Approximate hard-switching loss:

Pswitch ≈ ½ × VDS × ID × (tr + tf) × f

Example

  • VDS = 24V
  • ID = 3A
  • tr + tf = 200ns
  • f = 25kHz

Pswitch ≈ 0.5 × 24 × 3 × 200 × 10⁻⁹ × 25 × 10³

Pswitch ≈ 0.18W

Actual switching losses may differ substantially because of gate charge, driver impedance, parasitic inductance, load dynamics, Miller plateau, reverse recovery and waveform overlap.

12. Gate-Drive Power

A rough gate-drive power estimate is:

Pgate ≈ Qg × VGS × f

where:

  • Qg = total gate charge.
  • VGS = gate-drive voltage.
  • f = switching frequency.

Example

  • Qg = 30nC
  • VGS = 10V
  • f = 50kHz

Pgate = 30 × 10⁻⁹ × 10 × 50 × 10³

Pgate = 0.015W

13. Thermal Considerations

The junction temperature depends on power dissipation, ambient temperature, thermal resistance and cooling conditions.

TJ ≈ TA + P × RθJA

Example

  • Ambient temperature TA = 35°C
  • Estimated device power P = 1.5W
  • RθJA = 40°C/W (illustrative)

TJ ≈ 35 + 1.5 × 40

TJ ≈ 95°C

RθJA depends heavily on PCB copper, package, mounting and airflow. Use datasheet thermal information and verify temperature under real conditions.

14. MOSFET Switching Applications

  • LED switching and PWM brightness control.
  • DC motor switching and speed control.
  • Relay coil drivers.
  • Solenoid control.
  • Battery-powered load switching.
  • Power converters.
  • Embedded-system output control.
  • Digital logic interfacing with suitable logic-level MOSFETs.

15. Practical Lab: MOSFET LED Switch

Objective

Implement a low-side NMOS switch and observe its ON and OFF states.

Components

  • Logic-level NMOS suitable for the available GPIO voltage.
  • LED.
  • LED current-limiting resistor.
  • Gate resistor.
  • Gate pull-down resistor.
  • Low-voltage DC supply.
  • MCU GPIO or function generator.
  • Breadboard and multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Identify the MOSFET terminals from the datasheet.
  2. Connect source to ground.
  3. Connect the load between the positive supply and drain.
  4. Connect GPIO to gate through a gate resistor.
  5. Connect a pull-down resistor between gate and source.
  6. Ensure the MCU and power circuit share ground where appropriate.
  7. Apply LOW to the gate and observe the OFF state.
  8. Apply HIGH and observe the ON state.
  9. Measure VGS and VDS in both states.
  10. Apply PWM and observe load behavior.

Expected Results

  • LOW gate voltage: MOSFET OFF.
  • HIGH gate voltage: MOSFET ON, if adequately driven.
  • PWM: load responds to the duty cycle depending on its characteristics.
  • VDS in ON-state should be relatively low when the device is appropriately selected and driven.
Use low-voltage DC only. Do not connect this experimental circuit to mains. Observe current limits, MOSFET ratings and LED polarity.

19. Summary

  • Biasing establishes the MOSFET operating condition.
  • Gate-source voltage controls channel formation and drain current.
  • Switching applications typically use cutoff and a low-resistance ON state.
  • Gate resistors control switching behavior.
  • Pull-down resistors prevent floating gates.
  • PWM controls average load power in many applications.
  • Conduction, switching and gate-drive losses must be considered.
  • Thermal design and protection are critical to reliability.
