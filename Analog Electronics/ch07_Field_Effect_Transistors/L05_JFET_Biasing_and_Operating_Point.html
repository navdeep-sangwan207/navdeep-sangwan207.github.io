Lesson 5: MOSFET Construction, Types and Operation

Understand MOSFET structure, enhancement and depletion modes, and the working principle of NMOS and PMOS devices.

1. Introduction to MOSFET

MOSFET stands for Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor. It is a voltage-controlled semiconductor device in which the electric field produced by the gate voltage controls the current flowing between the drain and source terminals.

A MOSFET has an insulated gate. A thin insulating layer separates the gate electrode from the semiconductor body. Because of this insulation, the ideal DC gate current is approximately zero.

Important: MOSFETs are widely used in digital logic circuits, CMOS processors, switching power supplies, motor controllers, and electronic switching applications.

2. MOSFET Construction

A conventional MOSFET consists of a semiconductor substrate, two heavily doped regions called source and drain, a thin gate insulating layer, and a gate electrode placed above the insulating layer.

Four MOSFET Terminals

Terminal Symbol Function
Gate G Controls the formation or conductivity of the channel.
Drain D Terminal through which conventional drain current enters or leaves, depending on device polarity.
Source S Terminal from which majority carriers enter the channel.
Body / Bulk B Semiconductor substrate or body terminal.

Basic NMOS Structure

The gate is separated from the semiconductor by a thin insulating layer. The source and drain are heavily doped n-type regions in a p-type body.

Important Construction Points

  • The gate is electrically insulated from the semiconductor by a dielectric layer.
  • Traditional MOS processes commonly use silicon dioxide as the gate dielectric.
  • The source and drain regions are heavily doped to provide low-resistance connections.
  • The channel is the conducting path between source and drain.
  • The body terminal may be connected internally to the source in many discrete MOSFETs.
  • In integrated circuits, the body may be a separately controlled terminal.

3. Types of MOSFET Based on Channel Polarity

MOSFETs are classified as N-channel and P-channel devices according to the type of carriers that conduct through the channel.

A. N-Channel MOSFET (NMOS)

  • Typically constructed with a p-type substrate.
  • Contains heavily doped n-type source and drain regions.
  • Electrons are the majority carriers in the conducting channel.
  • An enhancement-mode NMOS normally requires a positive gate-to-source voltage to form a channel.

B. P-Channel MOSFET (PMOS)

  • Typically constructed with an n-type substrate or well.
  • Contains heavily doped p-type source and drain regions.
  • Holes are the majority carriers in the conducting channel.
  • An enhancement-mode PMOS normally requires a negative gate-to-source voltage to form a channel.
Parameter NMOS PMOS
Typical body type p-type n-type
Source and drain n+ regions p+ regions
Majority channel carriers Electrons Holes
Enhancement-mode gate voltage Positive VGS Negative VGS
Remember: NMOS and PMOS describe channel polarity. Enhancement and depletion describe operating mode. These are two different classifications.

4. Types of MOSFET Based on Operating Mode

Based on the presence or absence of a conducting channel at zero gate-to-source voltage, MOSFETs are classified into two major types.

A. Depletion-Mode MOSFET

In a depletion-mode MOSFET, a physical conducting channel already exists between the source and drain when the gate-to-source voltage is zero. Therefore, it can conduct at VGS = 0 when a suitable drain-to-source voltage is applied.

  • It is normally ON under the usual zero-gate-bias condition.
  • The gate voltage can reduce the channel conductivity.
  • For an N-channel depletion MOSFET, negative VGS depletes the channel.
  • Positive VGS can enhance its channel conductivity.
  • P-channel depletion MOSFETs operate with opposite voltage polarities.

B. Enhancement-Mode MOSFET

In an ideal enhancement-mode MOSFET, a conducting channel is absent at VGS = 0. An appropriate gate voltage creates an inversion channel that allows current to flow between the source and drain.

  • It is normally OFF at zero gate-to-source voltage.
  • For an N-channel device, a sufficiently positive VGS creates the channel.
  • For a P-channel device, a sufficiently negative VGS creates the channel.
  • The gate voltage controls the channel conductivity.

Comparison of Enhancement and Depletion MOSFETs

Parameter Enhancement Mode Depletion Mode
Channel at VGS = 0 Absent in the ideal device Present
Normal condition Normally OFF Normally ON
Operation Gate voltage creates a channel Gate voltage depletes or enhances an existing channel
Common use CMOS logic and switching Specialized switching and circuit applications

5. Working Principle of N-Channel Enhancement MOSFET

An N-channel enhancement MOSFET is normally OFF at zero gate-to-source voltage. Applying a positive voltage to the gate attracts electrons toward the region below the gate oxide.

Case 1: VGS = 0 V

  • No inversion channel is formed in the ideal enhancement device.
  • The source and drain are separated by the p-type substrate region.
  • The device is OFF under normal operating conditions.
  • Drain current is approximately zero, apart from leakage currents.

At zero gate bias, the ideal enhancement-mode NMOS has no inversion channel connecting source and drain.

Case 2: Positive VGS Below Threshold

When a small positive gate voltage is applied, electrons are attracted toward the gate-oxide interface. However, if VGS is below the threshold voltage VTH, a strong inversion channel has not yet formed.

Condition:

0 < VGS < VTH

The device is below its specified threshold condition for strong inversion.

Case 3: VGS Greater Than Threshold Voltage

When the positive gate-to-source voltage reaches and exceeds the threshold voltage, enough electrons gather near the oxide interface to form an N-type inversion channel.

  • An inversion channel connects the source and drain regions.
  • Applying a suitable positive VDS causes drain current to flow.
  • Increasing the gate voltage above threshold generally increases channel conductivity.

A positive gate voltage above threshold creates an inversion channel below the gate oxide.

Threshold condition for an enhancement NMOS:

VGS > VTH

Here, VTH is normally positive for an N-channel enhancement MOSFET.

Important distinction: Threshold voltage indicates the onset of strong inversion under specified test conditions. It does not mean that the MOSFET is fully ON or has a low drain-to-source resistance.

6. Working Principle of P-Channel Enhancement MOSFET

A P-channel enhancement MOSFET operates with voltage polarities opposite to those of an N-channel enhancement MOSFET.

  • At VGS = 0, the ideal device is OFF.
  • A negative gate-to-source voltage attracts holes toward the gate-oxide interface.
  • When the magnitude of VGS exceeds the magnitude of the threshold voltage, a P-type inversion channel forms.
  • A suitable negative VDS allows conventional drain current to flow in the corresponding direction.
Threshold condition for an enhancement PMOS:

VGS < VTH

Equivalently, |VGS| > |VTH| for the usual negative threshold voltage.

7. Working Principle of Depletion-Mode MOSFET

A depletion-mode MOSFET has a physical channel even when VGS = 0. The gate voltage changes the number of carriers in that channel.

N-Channel Depletion MOSFET

  • At VGS = 0, a conducting N-type channel exists.
  • Applying a negative VGS repels electrons from the channel region and reduces its conductivity.
  • At a sufficiently negative gate voltage, the channel can be depleted and the device approaches cutoff.
  • Applying a positive VGS attracts additional electrons and enhances channel conductivity.

P-Channel Depletion MOSFET

  • A conducting P-type channel exists at zero gate bias.
  • Positive VGS depletes holes and reduces channel conductivity.
  • Negative VGS enhances the channel conductivity.
Device Gate voltage effect Result
N-channel depletion Negative VGS Depletes channel
N-channel depletion Positive VGS Enhances channel
P-channel depletion Positive VGS Depletes channel
P-channel depletion Negative VGS Enhances channel

8. MOSFET Gate Insulation and Input Characteristics

The gate is separated from the semiconductor by an insulating dielectric. Therefore, an ideal MOSFET draws no steady-state DC gate current.

However, a practical MOSFET has gate capacitance. During switching, current is required to charge and discharge this capacitance.

Ideal DC gate current:

IG ≈ 0 A

Capacitive gate current:

iG = C × (dV/dt)

Where C represents the relevant effective gate capacitance.

Important Practical Points

  • Gate insulation gives MOSFETs very high DC input resistance.
  • Gate capacitance affects switching speed.
  • Gate charge must be supplied or removed to switch the device.
  • Excessive gate-to-source voltage can damage the gate dielectric.
  • Static electricity can damage sensitive MOSFET gates.
ESD Protection: Handle MOSFETs using appropriate electrostatic discharge precautions. Use an ESD-safe work surface, grounded wrist strap, and suitable storage or packaging. Always check the datasheet for the maximum gate-to-source voltage rating.

10. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is MOSFET?

MOSFET stands for Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor. It is a voltage-controlled device with an insulated gate that controls current through a semiconductor channel.

Q2. Why is MOSFET called a voltage-controlled device?

Because the gate-to-source voltage controls the formation or conductivity of the channel between the drain and source.

Q3. Why does a MOSFET have high input resistance?

Its gate is insulated from the semiconductor by a dielectric layer, resulting in extremely small ideal DC gate current.

Q4. What are the four terminals of a MOSFET?

Gate, Drain, Source, and Body (Bulk).

Q5. What is the difference between NMOS and PMOS?

NMOS uses an electron-conducting channel, whereas PMOS uses a hole-conducting channel. Their usual enhancement-mode gate voltage polarities are opposite.

Q6. What is an enhancement-mode MOSFET?

It is a MOSFET in which a conducting channel is absent at zero gate-to-source voltage in the ideal normally-off device. An appropriate gate voltage creates the channel.

Q7. What is a depletion-mode MOSFET?

It is a MOSFET with an existing conducting channel at zero gate-to-source voltage. Gate voltage can deplete or enhance its conductivity.

Q8. What is threshold voltage?

Threshold voltage is the gate-to-source voltage at which a sufficiently strong inversion channel begins to form under specified measurement conditions.

Q9. Why is a MOSFET sensitive to static electricity?

Electrostatic discharge can create a high voltage across the thin gate dielectric and damage its insulating layer.

Q10. Does a MOSFET gate draw current?

Ideally, steady-state DC gate current is zero. In practical circuits, small leakage may exist, and transient current is required to charge and discharge the gate capacitance.

12. Lesson Summary

  • MOSFET is a voltage-controlled field-effect transistor with an insulated gate.
  • Its four terminals are Gate, Drain, Source, and Body.
  • NMOS uses an electron-conducting channel; PMOS uses a hole-conducting channel.
  • Enhancement-mode MOSFETs are normally OFF at zero gate bias.
  • Depletion-mode MOSFETs have a conducting channel at zero gate bias.
  • An NMOS enhancement device requires a sufficiently positive VGS to form its channel.
  • A PMOS enhancement device requires a sufficiently negative VGS to form its channel.
  • The insulated gate gives high input resistance, but gate capacitance affects switching.
  • ESD precautions are important when handling MOSFETs.
