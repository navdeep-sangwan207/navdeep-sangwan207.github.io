Lesson 7: MOSFET Biasing and Switching Circuits

Learn MOSFET operating-point selection, gate biasing, load switching, low-side and high-side switching, and practical circuit design.

1. Introduction to MOSFET Biasing

MOSFET biasing is the process of applying suitable DC voltages to the gate, drain, and source terminals to establish the required operating condition.

The operating point, also called the Q-point, determines the DC drain current and drain-to-source voltage when no input signal is applied. Proper biasing is important for stable amplifier operation.

Two important applications:
  • Amplifier operation: MOSFET is generally biased in the saturation region.
  • Switching operation: MOSFET is driven between cutoff and the low-resistance ON state.

2. DC Load Line and Operating Point

Consider an NMOS common-source circuit with a drain resistor RD connected to the supply VDD and the source connected to ground.

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law:

VDD = ID RD + VDS

Therefore:

VDS = VDD − ID RD

This equation represents the DC load line. The intersection of the load line and the MOSFET output characteristic gives the operating point.

The Q-point is determined by the intersection of the DC load line and the selected MOSFET output characteristic.

3. Fixed Gate Bias

In fixed gate bias, a constant DC voltage is applied to the gate through a suitable bias network. The source may be connected to ground for a simple NMOS circuit.

Simplified fixed-gate-bias NMOS circuit. The gate bias source and drain supply must share a suitable reference ground.

For a grounded-source circuit:

VGS = VGG

VDS = VDD − ID RD

The gate voltage establishes the operating condition, while the drain resistor converts drain current into a drain voltage.

Fixed gate bias is simple, but the operating point can vary with threshold voltage, temperature, and device-to-device variations. It is not automatically a stable bias arrangement.

4. Voltage-Divider Gate Bias

A voltage-divider network uses two resistors to establish a DC gate voltage. Because the MOSFET gate draws very little steady-state DC current, the divider voltage is approximately determined by the resistor values.

The divider establishes the gate voltage. In a practical circuit, the drain resistor and source resistor are connected in the corresponding drain and source paths.

For an ideal divider with negligible gate current:

VG = VDD × R2 / (R1 + R2)

If a source resistor RS is used, the source voltage rises as current flows through it. This creates negative feedback and improves bias stability.

VS = ID RS

VGS = VG − VS

VGS = VG − ID RS

5. Source Resistor and Self-Bias Stabilization

A source resistor provides negative feedback. If drain current increases, the source voltage increases. This reduces VGS and opposes the original increase in drain current.

Negative Feedback Process

  1. Assume drain current ID increases.
  2. Source voltage VS = ID RS increases.
  3. Gate-to-source voltage VGS = VG − VS decreases.
  4. The reduced VGS opposes the increase in drain current.
  5. The operating point becomes less sensitive to device variations.
A source resistor is useful for improving the stability of a MOSFET amplifier bias point. A bypass capacitor may be connected across it in some amplifier designs to reduce AC degeneration while retaining DC stabilization.

6. MOSFET as an Electronic Switch

A MOSFET can operate as an electronic switch by moving between cutoff and a low-resistance ON condition.

Switch Condition Operating State Typical Behavior
OFF Cutoff Very small drain current
ON Low-resistance conduction Small drain-to-source voltage at the required load current
For switching applications, the objective is usually to operate the MOSFET with low conduction loss when ON. The triode/ohmic region is therefore commonly used for the fully enhanced ON state. Do not assume that merely exceeding VTH guarantees a low RDS(on).

7. NMOS Low-Side Switching Circuit

A low-side switch places the N-channel MOSFET between the load and ground. The load is connected between the positive supply and the MOSFET drain.

When the gate receives a suitable ON voltage relative to the source, current flows from the supply through the load and MOSFET to ground.

Working

When the control signal is LOW:

  • VGS is below threshold.
  • The MOSFET is OFF.
  • Load current is approximately zero, apart from leakage.

When the control signal is HIGH:

  • VGS becomes sufficiently high to enhance the MOSFET.
  • The MOSFET enters its low-resistance ON condition.
  • Current flows through the load and MOSFET.
Approximate ON-state voltage:

VDS ≈ ID × RDS(on)

Conduction power loss:

PD ≈ ID² × RDS(on)

8. PMOS High-Side Switching Circuit

A high-side switch places a P-channel MOSFET between the positive supply and the load. It controls the supply connection to the load.

A PMOS high-side switch turns ON when its gate is sufficiently below its source voltage.

Working

  • When gate voltage is approximately equal to source voltage, VGS ≈ 0 and the PMOS is OFF.
  • When the gate is pulled sufficiently below the source, VGS becomes negative and the PMOS turns ON.
  • Current flows from the positive supply through the PMOS and load.
A PMOS high-side switch connected to a supply higher than the control logic voltage may require a level-shifting or gate-driver circuit. Never apply a gate voltage that exceeds the device's rated VGS limits.

9. Gate Resistors and Protection Components

Practical MOSFET switching circuits often use additional components to improve switching behavior and protect the device.

Component Purpose
Gate resistor Controls gate charging and discharging current and helps manage switching transients.
Gate-to-source pull-down resistor Keeps an NMOS OFF when the control signal is floating.
Gate-to-source pull-up resistor Keeps a PMOS OFF when its control signal is floating.
Flyback diode Provides a current path for inductive loads when switched OFF.
Gate protection circuit Helps protect the gate against excessive voltage or transients.

Flyback Diode for Inductive Loads

Relays, solenoids, and many motors are inductive loads. When current through an inductor is interrupted, it can generate a high voltage spike. A suitable flyback diode across a DC inductive load provides a path for the current and helps protect the switching transistor.

Select the flyback diode based on load current, reverse voltage, switching frequency, and the required release time. A simple diode may slow relay release; a TVS or other clamp may be appropriate in some designs.

10. Numerical Example – NMOS Switching Circuit

Given:

  • Supply voltage VDD = 12 V
  • Load resistance RL = 6 Ω
  • MOSFET ON resistance RDS(on) = 0.1 Ω

Assume the MOSFET is fully enhanced and the load is resistive.

Step 1: Calculate Load Current

I = VDD / (RL + RDS(on))

I = 12 / (6 + 0.1)

I ≈ 1.967 A

Step 2: Calculate MOSFET Voltage Drop

VDS = I × RDS(on)

VDS = 1.967 × 0.1

VDS ≈ 0.197 V

Step 3: Calculate MOSFET Conduction Loss

PD = I² × RDS(on)

PD = (1.967)² × 0.1

PD ≈ 0.387 W

The example assumes a constant ON resistance of 0.1 Ω at the actual gate voltage and operating temperature. In a real design, use the datasheet's RDS(on) specification and check thermal limits.

11. Important Practical Design Considerations

  • Choose a MOSFET with a suitable drain-to-source voltage rating.
  • Check continuous and pulsed drain-current ratings.
  • Check RDS(on) at the actual gate drive voltage.
  • Do not assume a MOSFET is fully enhanced merely because VGS exceeds VTH.
  • Check gate charge and switching time for high-frequency applications.
  • Use a suitable gate driver when the microcontroller cannot provide sufficient drive.
  • Provide a proper heat sink if the power dissipation requires it.
  • Check the safe operating area and transient thermal limits.
  • Use a flyback or clamp circuit for inductive loads where required.
  • Ensure the gate has a defined OFF state using a pull-up or pull-down resistor.

12. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is MOSFET biasing?

MOSFET biasing is the application of suitable DC voltages and currents to establish the required operating point.

Q2. What is the Q-point?

The Q-point is the quiescent operating point, specified mainly by the DC drain current ID and drain-to-source voltage VDS.

Q3. Why is a source resistor used in MOSFET biasing?

It provides negative feedback and reduces the sensitivity of drain current to device and temperature variations.

Q4. How does an NMOS work as a low-side switch?

The load is connected between the positive supply and the drain. Applying a suitable positive gate-to-source voltage turns the MOSFET ON and allows current to flow to ground.

Q5. How does a PMOS work as a high-side switch?

The PMOS is placed between the positive supply and the load. It turns ON when its gate is sufficiently below its source voltage.

Q6. What is RDS(on)?

RDS(on) is the drain-to-source resistance when the MOSFET is properly enhanced and operating in its ON state.

Q7. Why is a gate resistor used?

It controls gate charging and discharging current and can help reduce ringing and electromagnetic interference.

Q8. Why is a pull-down resistor used with an NMOS?

It ensures the gate remains at a defined low voltage when the controller output is disconnected or in a high-impedance state.

Q9. Why is a flyback diode used?

It provides a path for inductive load current when the switching device turns OFF, limiting the voltage spike.

Q10. What is the difference between a logic-level MOSFET and a standard MOSFET?

A logic-level MOSFET is designed to achieve specified low RDS(on) at relatively low gate drive voltages. Always verify the actual RDS(on) specifications in the datasheet.

14. Lesson Summary

  • MOSFET biasing establishes a suitable DC operating point.
  • The Q-point is described by the DC drain current and drain-to-source voltage.
  • Fixed gate bias and voltage-divider bias are common biasing arrangements.
  • A source resistor provides negative feedback and improves bias stability.
  • NMOS devices are commonly used for low-side switching.
  • PMOS devices can be used for high-side switching.
  • RDS(on) determines an important part of MOSFET conduction loss.
  • Gate resistors, pull-up or pull-down resistors, and flyback protection improve practical circuit behavior.
  • Datasheet ratings, thermal limits, and safe operating area must always be checked.
