2. What is an Instrumentation Amplifier?

An instrumentation amplifier is a precision differential amplifier designed to amplify a small voltage difference between two input signals while rejecting voltage common to both inputs.

It is especially useful when the wanted signal is small and may be accompanied by common-mode interference, such as electrical noise picked up by sensor wires.

High Input Impedance

Minimizes loading of the sensor or measurement source.

High CMRR

Rejects common-mode signals such as interference appearing on both inputs.

Accurate Gain

Gain can be adjusted using a resistor in many three-op-amp designs.

Low Offset

Precision INA devices are designed for accurate low-level measurements.

3. Differential and Common-Mode Signals

Consider two input voltages V1 and V2.

Vd = V2 − V1
Vcm = (V1 + V2) / 2

Vd is the differential input voltage. Vcm is the common-mode voltage.

The two input signals can be expressed as:

V1 = Vcm − Vd/2
V2 = Vcm + Vd/2
The desired measurement is usually Vd. An ideal instrumentation amplifier amplifies Vd and completely rejects Vcm.

Example

If V1=2.499 V and V2=2.501 V:

Vd = 2.501 − 2.499 = 0.002 V = 2 mV
Vcm = (2.499 + 2.501)/2 = 2.500 V

The amplifier must accurately amplify the 2 mV difference even though both input voltages are around 2.5 V.

4. Three-Op-Amp Instrumentation Amplifier

A widely used instrumentation amplifier architecture contains two non-inverting input amplifiers followed by a differential amplifier.

This diagram is a functional architecture illustration rather than a complete pin-by-pin wiring schematic. The gain equations below define the standard topology: two equal feedback resistors R, a gain resistor RG between the first-stage inverting nodes, followed by a matched-ratio difference amplifier.

Stage 1: Input Amplifiers

  • A1 and A2 are non-inverting amplifiers.
  • Both sensor inputs connect directly to non-inverting terminals.
  • Two equal feedback resistors R connect each output to its inverting input.
  • RG connects the two inverting input nodes.
  • Changing RG adjusts the differential gain of the first stage.

Stage 2: Differential Amplifier

The third op-amp subtracts the two first-stage output voltages. The four-resistor network must have accurately matched ratios for good common-mode rejection.

5. Derivation of Three-Op-Amp INA Gain

For ideal op-amps operating with negative feedback, the inverting input voltages approximately equal their corresponding non-inverting input voltages.

Let the input voltages be V1 and V2, the two equal feedback resistors be R, and the gain-setting resistor be RG.

For the upper first-stage amplifier:

VO1 = V1 + (R/RG)(V1 − V2)

For the lower first-stage amplifier:

VO2 = V2 + (R/RG)(V2 − V1)

Subtracting the first-stage outputs:

VO2 − VO1 = (1 + 2R/RG)(V2 − V1)

The difference amplifier has a gain of R4/R3 when its resistor ratios are matched.

Vout = (R4/R3)(VO2 − VO1)

Overall Differential Gain

Ad = (1 + 2R/RG) × (R4/R3)
Vout = Ad × (V2 − V1)
The gain equation assumes ideal op-amps, equal first-stage feedback resistors, and a properly matched differential amplifier resistor network. Real devices have offset, bias-current, gain-error and common-mode limitations.

6. Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR)

CMRR measures how effectively a differential amplifier rejects a signal common to both inputs.

CMRR = |Ad / Acm|

Where:

  • Ad = differential-mode gain.
  • Acm = common-mode gain.

CMRR is commonly expressed in decibels:

CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(|Ad/Acm|)

A higher CMRR indicates better rejection of common-mode signals.

Example

If differential gain is 1000 and common-mode gain is 0.01:

CMRR = 1000/0.01 = 100000
CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(100000) = 100 dB
CMRR = 100,000 or 100 dB

7. Effect of Resistor Matching

The differential amplifier stage requires accurate resistor-ratio matching. If the ratios differ, some common-mode voltage appears at the output.

  • Resistor tolerance affects common-mode rejection.
  • Temperature drift can change resistor ratios.
  • Precision matched resistor networks improve CMRR.
  • Integrated instrumentation amplifiers often use carefully matched internal resistor networks.
A high-quality first stage alone does not guarantee high overall CMRR. The subtraction stage and its resistor matching are also critical.

8. Gain-Setting Resistor Design

For the standard three-op-amp topology:

Ad = (1 + 2R/RG)(R4/R3)

If the differential stage has unity gain, R4/R3=1:

Ad = 1 + 2R/RG

Rearranging to calculate RG:

RG = 2R/(Ad − 1)

This allows the first-stage gain to be set using one resistor.

11. Design a Three-Op-Amp INA

Problem: Design an instrumentation amplifier with a differential gain of 101. Use R=10 kΩ in the first stage and a unity-gain difference amplifier.

Given:

Ad = 101, R = 10 kΩ, R4/R3 = 1

Using:

RG = 2R/(Ad − 1)
RG = (2 × 10 kΩ)/(101 − 1)
RG = 20000/100 = 200 Ω
Required gain-setting resistor RG = 200 Ω

12. Input and Output Limitations

An instrumentation amplifier cannot amplify every possible combination of input voltage and gain. Its internal stages must remain within their operating limits.

Input Common-Mode Range

The input common-mode voltage must remain within the specified input range of the device. This range depends on the amplifier architecture and supply voltage.

Output Swing

The output cannot exceed the permitted output swing. A calculated output voltage outside the allowed range will clip or saturate.

Gain-Bandwidth and Noise

High gain may reduce available bandwidth. Input noise, offset voltage, bias current and source impedance also affect small-signal measurements.

Always check the actual instrumentation amplifier datasheet for input common-mode range, output swing, supply requirements, gain range, reference-pin limits and bandwidth. Do not assume that every INA can measure signals close to either supply rail.

13. Reference Pin and Output Offset

Many integrated instrumentation amplifiers provide a REF pin. This pin allows the output level to be shifted by a reference voltage.

Vout = Ad(V2 − V1) + VREF

This simplified equation applies to the ideal transfer relationship of devices whose REF pin is specified to add directly to the output. Follow the selected device's datasheet for REF pin loading and drive requirements.

Applications include shifting a bipolar sensor signal into the input range of a unipolar ADC.

14. Sensor Interface Applications

Strain Gauge

Amplifies the small differential voltage generated by a strain-gauge bridge.

Load Cell

Amplifies bridge output voltage for weighing systems.

Thermocouple

Amplifies small sensor voltages, with suitable offset and filtering considerations.

Pressure Sensor

Conditions differential outputs from bridge-based pressure sensors.

Biomedical Signals

Amplifies low-level differential signals while rejecting common-mode interference, subject to safety and isolation requirements.

Industrial Measurement

Provides precision signal conditioning for sensors in electrically noisy environments.

15. Load Cell Signal Conditioning Example

Suppose a load cell produces a differential output of 2 mV at a particular load. An instrumentation amplifier is configured for a gain of 200.

Vout = Ad × Vd
Vout = 200 × 2 mV = 400 mV
Ideal output = 400 mV, assuming zero REF voltage and no saturation.

In a real system, check the load-cell excitation, rated sensitivity, bridge resistance, amplifier input range, offset, noise, filtering and ADC input range.

17. Practical Laboratory

Objective

Build and test a three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier and measure its differential gain and common-mode response.

Components and Equipment

  • Three suitable op-amps or an instrumentation amplifier IC.
  • Precision resistors and gain-setting resistor.
  • Dual regulated supply, if required by the selected circuit.
  • Function generator or two adjustable DC sources.
  • Digital multimeter and oscilloscope.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

Procedure

  1. Build the first stage with equal feedback resistors and the selected RG.
  2. Connect the first-stage outputs to a matched-ratio difference amplifier.
  3. Apply two nearly equal input voltages.
  4. Measure V1, V2 and output voltage.
  5. Calculate differential input and measured gain.
  6. Apply the same voltage to both inputs to test common-mode response.
  7. Calculate CMRR from the measured differential and common-mode gains, accounting for measurement limits.
  8. Repeat with a different RG and compare the gain.

Expected Result

The output should follow the amplified input difference. When equal common-mode signals are applied to both inputs, the output should ideally remain unchanged apart from offset and non-ideal errors.

Use low-voltage, current-limited supplies and verify the op-amp pinout and supply ratings before powering the circuit. Do not connect a live mains signal to a breadboard experiment.

19. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is an instrumentation amplifier?

A precision differential amplifier with high input impedance and high common-mode rejection.

2. Why is it used in sensor circuits?

It amplifies small differential sensor signals while rejecting common-mode interference.

3. How many op-amps are used in the conventional three-op-amp INA?

Three: two input amplifiers and one differential amplifier.

4. What is the role of RG?

It sets the first-stage differential gain.

5. What is differential voltage?

The voltage difference between the two inputs, V2−V1.

6. What is common-mode voltage?

The average of the two input voltages, (V1+V2)/2.

7. Define CMRR.

The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain.

8. Why is CMRR expressed in dB?

To represent a very large gain ratio on a convenient logarithmic scale.

9. What happens if the difference amplifier resistors are mismatched?

Common-mode rejection decreases and common-mode signals can appear at the output.

10. What is the advantage of high input impedance?

It reduces loading of high-impedance sensors and measurement sources.

11. What is the purpose of the REF pin?

It provides an output reference or level-shift input on devices designed with that feature.

12. Can an INA amplify a signal of any common-mode voltage?

No. The input common-mode range and supply constraints must be respected.

13. What is the effect of increasing RG?

For the standard three-op-amp topology, increasing RG reduces first-stage gain.

14. Why is an INA preferred over a basic differential amplifier?

It typically provides higher input impedance and better precision, with convenient gain adjustment.

15. Name two applications of an instrumentation amplifier.

Load-cell measurement and strain-gauge signal conditioning.

16. What limits the maximum output voltage?

The output swing capability of the op-amp and the available supply voltage.

20. Numerical Practice

  1. An INA has Ad=500 and Vd=3 mV. Calculate the ideal output for VREF=0.
  2. Calculate CMRR in dB if Ad=2000 and Acm=0.02.
  3. For R=20 kΩ and RG=1 kΩ, calculate first-stage differential gain.
  4. An INA has gain 101, R=10 kΩ and a unity-gain difference stage. Calculate RG.
  5. If V1=1.998 V and V2=2.002 V, calculate Vd and Vcm.
  6. Explain why a calculated output may not be obtained when the amplifier is powered from a low-voltage single supply.
Show answers
  1. Vout=500×3 mV=1.5 V.
  2. CMRR=2000/0.02=100,000; CMRR=100 dB.
  3. Ad=1+2(20/1)=41.
  4. RG=2×10 kΩ/(101−1)=200 Ω.
  5. Vd=4 mV; Vcm=2.000 V.
  6. The output may exceed the available output swing, or the input common-mode voltage may be outside the device's permitted range.

21. Lecture Summary

  • An instrumentation amplifier amplifies a differential signal and rejects common-mode voltage.
  • The conventional architecture uses three op-amps.
  • The first stage provides high input impedance and adjustable gain.
  • The second stage subtracts the two first-stage outputs.
  • The standard overall gain is (1+2R/RG)(R4/R3).
  • CMRR indicates the ability to reject common-mode signals.
  • Precision resistor matching is important for high CMRR.
  • Input common-mode range and output swing must be checked during design.
  • Instrumentation amplifiers are widely used in sensor and measurement systems.
