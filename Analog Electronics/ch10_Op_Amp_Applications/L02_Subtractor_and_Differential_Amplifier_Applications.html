2. What is a Differential Amplifier?

A differential amplifier amplifies the difference between two input voltages while ideally rejecting a signal that is common to both inputs.

Vdiff = V2 − V1

Its output ideally depends on the difference between the two input signals rather than their absolute values.

Differential signal

The voltage difference between two input terminals.

Common-mode signal

A signal that appears equally on both input terminals.

Noise rejection

Common-mode rejection helps suppress interference shared by both inputs.

Applications

Sensor measurements, bridge circuits, signal conditioning and instrumentation.

3. Single Op-Amp Subtractor Circuit

The basic subtractor uses four resistors and one op-amp.

Standard resistor connections

ResistorConnection
R1V1 to inverting input
R2Output to inverting input
R3V2 to non-inverting input
R4Non-inverting input to ground
The SVG is conceptual. In the standard subtractor, R1 and R2 form the inverting path, while R3 and R4 form the non-inverting divider. The output feedback is connected through R2 to the inverting node.

4. Derivation of Subtractor Output

First, calculate the voltage at the non-inverting terminal using the divider R3 and R4.

V+ = V2 × R4/(R3 + R4)

Under ideal op-amp assumptions and negative feedback:

V− ≈ V+

Applying KCL at the inverting node:

(V1 − V−)/R1 = (V− − Vout)/R2

Rearranging:

Vout = (1 + R2/R1)V− − (R2/R1)V1

Substitute the non-inverting input voltage:

Vout = (1 + R2/R1)[V2 × R4/(R3+R4)] − (R2/R1)V1

5. Matched Resistor Ratio Condition

For the standard subtractor, if the resistor ratios are matched:

R2/R1 = R4/R3

Then the output equation simplifies to:

Vout = (R2/R1)(V2 − V1)

The circuit amplifies the difference between V2 and V1.

Matching resistor ratios is essential for good common-mode rejection. Precision resistor networks can provide better ratio matching than individual ordinary resistors.

6. Solved Numerical Problem 1

Given:

  • R1 = R3 = 10 kΩ
  • R2 = R4 = 100 kΩ
  • V1 = 1 V
  • V2 = 1.5 V

Step 1: Verify resistor ratios

R2/R1 = 100/10 = 10
R4/R3 = 100/10 = 10

The ratios are matched.

Step 2: Calculate output

Vout = 10(V2 − V1)
Vout = 10(1.5 − 1)
Vout = 5 V

7. Solved Numerical Problem 2

Given:

  • R1 = 5 kΩ
  • R2 = 20 kΩ
  • R3 = 5 kΩ
  • R4 = 20 kΩ
  • V1 = 2 V
  • V2 = 2.5 V

Matched ratios:

R2/R1 = 20/5 = 4
R4/R3 = 20/5 = 4
Vout = 4(2.5 − 2)
Vout = 2 V

9. Differential and Common-Mode Voltages

Two input signals can be decomposed into differential and common-mode components.

Vdiff = V2 − V1
Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2

The input voltages can be expressed in terms of these components:

V1 = Vcm − Vdiff/2
V2 = Vcm + Vdiff/2

Numerical

V1 = 2.00 V and V2 = 2.10 V.

Vdiff = 2.10 − 2.00 = 0.10 V
Vcm = (2.10 + 2.00)/2 = 2.05 V

11. Common-Mode Rejection

Common-mode rejection describes the ability of a differential amplifier to reject common-mode signals.

CMRR = Ad/Acm
CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(Ad/Acm)

Where:

  • Ad = differential gain
  • Acm = common-mode gain

Numerical

Ad = 1000 and Acm = 0.1.

CMRR = 1000/0.1 = 10000
CMRR(dB) = 20 log10(10000) = 80 dB

13. Resistor Mismatch and Common-Mode Error

Common-mode rejection in a practical subtractor is affected by the resistor ratios. If the ratios are mismatched, the common-mode signal may appear at the output.

  • Resistor tolerance affects ratio matching.
  • Op-amp common-mode gain contributes to error.
  • Input offset voltage can introduce output error.
  • Bias current can cause voltage drops across input resistors.
  • Temperature changes can alter resistor ratios.
For accurate low-level measurements, use precision matched resistor networks or an instrumentation amplifier where appropriate.

14. Differential Amplifier Applications

Bridge Sensors

Amplifies the voltage difference from bridge measurement circuits.

Temperature Measurement

Amplifies small differential sensor signals.

Industrial Signal Measurement

Measures voltage differences between signal lines.

Noise Rejection

Rejects shared interference when the circuit has sufficient CMRR.

Battery Monitoring

Measures differential voltages within suitable input and common-mode limits.

Analog Signal Conditioning

Provides differential signal scaling before further processing.

16. Practical Laboratory

Objective

Construct and analyze a subtractor circuit using a single op-amp.

Components

  • Op-amp suitable for the selected supply.
  • R1, R3 = 10 kΩ.
  • R2, R4 = 100 kΩ.
  • Low-voltage DC signal sources.
  • Multimeter.
  • Regulated power supply.

Procedure

  1. Connect the op-amp supply according to the datasheet.
  2. Connect R1 between V1 and the inverting input.
  3. Connect R2 between output and the inverting input.
  4. Connect R3 between V2 and the non-inverting input.
  5. Connect R4 between the non-inverting input and ground.
  6. Verify the resistor ratios.
  7. Apply V1 and V2.
  8. Calculate the expected output.
  9. Measure the output using a multimeter.
  10. Change V1 and V2 while keeping their common-mode voltage approximately constant.
  11. Observe how the output changes with differential voltage.

Expected Result

With matched resistor ratios, the output approximates the amplified difference between V2 and V1.

Use a low-voltage experiment. Verify the op-amp pinout, supply voltage and common-mode limits before powering the circuit.

18. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is a differential amplifier?

An amplifier that amplifies the voltage difference between two inputs.

2. What is a subtractor?

A circuit that produces an output proportional to the difference between two input voltages.

3. What is the output equation of a matched subtractor?

Vout = (R2/R1)(V2 − V1).

4. What is the resistor matching condition?

R2/R1 = R4/R3.

5. What is differential voltage?

Vdiff = V2 − V1.

6. What is common-mode voltage?

Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2.

7. What is CMRR?

The ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain.

8. What is CMRR in dB?

20 log10(Ad/Acm).

9. Why are matched resistor ratios important?

They improve the circuit's differential behavior and common-mode rejection.

10. What happens when resistor ratios are mismatched?

Common-mode signals may produce an output error.

11. Where are differential amplifiers used?

Sensor interfaces, bridge circuits, measurement systems and signal conditioning.

12. Why is an instrumentation amplifier often preferred for low-level sensors?

It typically provides high input impedance, precise gain control and better common-mode rejection than a basic four-resistor subtractor, depending on implementation.

13. Can a differential amplifier reject all noise?

No. Rejection depends on CMRR, source balance, resistor matching, frequency, layout and operating conditions.

14. What is the effect of input offset voltage?

It introduces an error in the differential input and may produce an output offset.

15. What happens if the common-mode input voltage exceeds the allowed range?

The op-amp may behave incorrectly, become nonlinear or violate device ratings.

16. Why is a differential amplifier useful for bridge sensors?

It amplifies the small voltage difference generated by the bridge.

17. What is the difference between differential gain and common-mode gain?

Differential gain amplifies the difference between the inputs; common-mode gain represents amplification of a shared signal.

18. What practical factors affect CMRR?

Resistor matching, op-amp CMRR, frequency, temperature, layout and source impedance.
