2. What is an Electronic Filter?

An electronic filter is a circuit that allows selected frequency components of a signal to pass while attenuating unwanted frequency components.

Low-Pass Filter (LPF)

Passes frequencies below the cutoff frequency and attenuates higher frequencies.

High-Pass Filter (HPF)

Passes frequencies above the cutoff frequency and attenuates lower frequencies.

Band-Pass Filter

Passes a selected range of frequencies.

Band-Stop Filter

Attenuates a selected range of frequencies.

This lecture focuses on first-order active low-pass and high-pass filters.

3. Passive vs Active Filters

Active filters are widely used in instrumentation, audio systems, sensor interfaces and signal-conditioning circuits.

4. First-Order Active Low-Pass Filter

A first-order active low-pass filter can be built using an RC network connected to the non-inverting input of an op-amp configured as a non-inverting amplifier.

The RC network determines the cutoff frequency. The op-amp provides passband gain and buffering.

Cutoff Frequency

fc = 1 / (2πRC)

At the cutoff frequency, the output magnitude is approximately 0.707 times its passband magnitude, which corresponds to −3 dB relative to the passband.

Passband Gain

For a non-inverting op-amp configuration:

Av = 1 + (Rf/Rg)

Transfer Function

H(s) = Av / (1 + sRC)

Frequency response magnitude:

|H(jω)| = Av / √(1 + (f/fc)²)

Phase response:

φ = −tan⁻¹(f/fc)

5. First-Order Active High-Pass Filter

A first-order active high-pass filter uses a series capacitor and a resistor to ground at the non-inverting op-amp input. The op-amp provides gain and buffering.

Cutoff Frequency

fc = 1 / (2πRC)

Passband Gain

Av = 1 + (Rf/Rg)

Transfer Function

H(s) = Av(sRC) / (1 + sRC)

Frequency response magnitude:

|H(jω)| = Av(f/fc) / √(1 + (f/fc)²)

Phase response:

φ = 90° − tan⁻¹(f/fc)

6. Frequency Response of a Low-Pass Filter

At frequencies much lower than fc, the low-pass filter operates in its passband. At frequencies above fc, the output gradually decreases.

FrequencyApproximate magnitude relative to passband
0.1fc0.995
0.5fc0.894
fc0.707
2fc0.447
10fc0.0995
A first-order low-pass filter has an asymptotic roll-off of −20 dB/decade above the cutoff frequency.

7. Frequency Response of a High-Pass Filter

At frequencies much lower than fc, the high-pass filter attenuates the input. Above fc, the output approaches its passband gain.

FrequencyApproximate magnitude relative to passband
0.1fc0.0995
0.5fc0.447
fc0.707
2fc0.894
10fc0.995
A first-order high-pass filter has an asymptotic roll-off of +20 dB/decade below the cutoff frequency as frequency increases toward the passband.

8. Bode Plot and Decibel Gain

A Bode magnitude plot represents gain in decibels against logarithmic frequency.

Gain(dB) = 20 log10(|Vout/Vin|)

For an active filter with passband voltage gain Av:

Gain(dB) = 20 log10(Av)

At cutoff, the gain is 3 dB below the passband gain.

Example: if the passband gain is 2:

20 log10(2) = 6.02 dB

At cutoff:

6.02 − 3.01 = 3.01 dB

9. Worked Numerical Example – Low-Pass Filter

Design a first-order active low-pass filter with R=10 kΩ, C=10 nF and op-amp passband gain Av=2.

Step 1: Calculate cutoff frequency.

fc = 1/(2π × 10,000 × 10×10⁻⁹)
fc ≈ 1591.5 Hz

Step 2: Calculate gain in decibels.

Gain = 20log10(2) ≈ 6.02 dB

Step 3: Calculate output/input ratio at cutoff.

|H| = 2/√2 = 1.414
Cutoff frequency ≈ 1.59 kHz | Passband gain = 2 | Gain at cutoff ≈ 1.414

10. Worked Numerical Example – High-Pass Filter

Design a first-order active high-pass filter with R=15 kΩ, C=100 nF and Av=3.

fc = 1/(2π × 15,000 × 100×10⁻⁹)
fc ≈ 106.1 Hz

At cutoff:

|H| = 3/√2 ≈ 2.121
Cutoff frequency ≈ 106.1 Hz | Passband gain = 3 | Gain at cutoff ≈ 2.121

14. Applications of Active LPF and HPF

Audio Systems

Low-pass filters suppress high-frequency noise; high-pass filters remove low-frequency rumble and DC components.

Sensor Interfaces

Filters remove unwanted noise before analog-to-digital conversion.

Signal Conditioning

Filters restrict signals to a required frequency range.

Biomedical Equipment

Filters help isolate useful signal components from interference.

Communication Systems

Filters select useful frequency components and reduce unwanted signals.

Embedded Systems

Analog filters can condition sensor signals before they reach a microcontroller ADC.

15. Practical Design Considerations

  • Select R and C values to achieve the required cutoff frequency.
  • Consider capacitor tolerance and resistor tolerance.
  • Choose an op-amp with sufficient gain-bandwidth product.
  • Check slew rate for the maximum signal amplitude and frequency.
  • Verify input common-mode range and output swing.
  • Use supply decoupling capacitors close to the op-amp supply pins.
  • Check the load connected to the output.
  • Consider noise, PCB layout and grounding.
  • For high-order filters, select the required topology and damping factor.
The simple first-order equations assume an ideal op-amp and an unloaded RC network. Real op-amp bandwidth and component tolerances can shift the measured response.

16. Practical Laboratory

Objective

Construct first-order active low-pass and high-pass filters and measure their frequency responses.

Components

  • Op-amp IC suitable for the selected supply.
  • Resistors and capacitors.
  • Function generator.
  • Dual regulated power supply if required.
  • Oscilloscope and digital multimeter.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

Experiment A: Low-Pass Filter

  1. Calculate the expected cutoff frequency from the selected R and C.
  2. Build the RC input network and non-inverting amplifier stage.
  3. Apply a constant-amplitude sine wave.
  4. Measure the output at frequencies below cutoff.
  5. Increase the frequency through the cutoff point.
  6. Measure output amplitude at frequencies above cutoff.
  7. Calculate gain in decibels and plot the response.

Experiment B: High-Pass Filter

  1. Build the series-capacitor and shunt-resistor input network.
  2. Apply a constant-amplitude sine wave.
  3. Measure the output at low frequencies.
  4. Increase frequency through the cutoff point.
  5. Record the passband response.
  6. Compare the measured and calculated cutoff frequencies.

Expected Result

The low-pass filter attenuates high frequencies. The high-pass filter attenuates low frequencies. Both have approximately 0.707 of their passband magnitude at cutoff.

18. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is an active filter?

A filter that uses active components such as op-amps along with resistors and capacitors.

2. What is a low-pass filter?

A filter that passes lower frequencies and attenuates frequencies above its cutoff.

3. What is a high-pass filter?

A filter that passes higher frequencies and attenuates frequencies below its cutoff.

4. What is cutoff frequency?

The frequency at which a first-order filter's magnitude is 0.707 of its passband magnitude.

5. Why is cutoff called the −3 dB point?

Because 20log10(0.707) is approximately −3.01 dB.

6. What is the cutoff frequency formula for a first-order RC filter?

fc=1/(2πRC).

7. What is the passband gain of a non-inverting op-amp?

Av=1+Rf/Rg.

8. What is the roll-off rate of a first-order low-pass filter?

−20 dB per decade above cutoff.

9. What is the roll-off rate of a first-order high-pass filter?

+20 dB per decade below cutoff as frequency increases toward the passband.

10. What is a Bode plot?

A plot of magnitude or phase response against logarithmic frequency.

11. Why are active filters useful?

They provide buffering and can provide voltage gain without inductors in common RC implementations.

12. Can an active filter provide voltage gain?

Yes, depending on its op-amp configuration and feedback network.

13. What limits the maximum frequency of an active filter?

Op-amp gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, output swing and circuit parasitics.

14. What happens to a low-pass output far above cutoff?

Its magnitude decreases at approximately 20 dB per decade for a first-order filter.

15. What happens to a high-pass output far below cutoff?

Its magnitude is strongly attenuated.

16. Why use a high-pass filter at an amplifier input?

To block DC and unwanted low-frequency components.

17. What is the phase shift of a first-order low-pass filter at cutoff?

Approximately −45°.

18. What is the phase shift of a first-order high-pass filter at cutoff?

Approximately +45° for the stated non-inverting configuration.

20. Lecture Summary

  • Active filters use op-amps and RC networks.
  • Low-pass filters attenuate high-frequency signals.
  • High-pass filters attenuate low-frequency signals.
  • The first-order RC cutoff frequency is 1/(2πRC).
  • The non-inverting op-amp gain is 1+Rf/Rg.
  • The cutoff magnitude is approximately 0.707 of passband magnitude.
  • First-order filters have a 20 dB/decade asymptotic slope.
  • Practical performance depends on component and op-amp limitations.
