2. What is a Band-Pass Filter?

A band-pass filter (BPF) allows a selected range of frequencies to pass while attenuating frequencies below and above that range.

A band-pass filter is useful when the required signal occupies a limited frequency range, such as a sensor signal or a communication channel.

3. What is a Band-Stop Filter?

A band-stop filter attenuates a selected range of frequencies while allowing frequencies below and above that range to pass.

A narrow band-stop filter is commonly called a notch filter. It is useful for removing a specific unwanted frequency, such as mains hum.

4. Important Filter Parameters

Lower Cutoff Frequency (fL)

The lower boundary of the passband for a band-pass filter.

Upper Cutoff Frequency (fH)

The upper boundary of the passband for a band-pass filter.

Center Frequency (f0)

The geometric mean of lower and upper cutoff frequencies for the standard band-pass response.

Bandwidth (BW)

The difference between upper and lower cutoff frequencies.

Quality Factor (Q)

A measure of selectivity relative to center frequency and bandwidth.

BW = fH − fL
f0 = √(fL × fH)
Q = f0 / BW
For a conventional second-order band-pass response, the cutoff frequencies are the half-power points relative to the peak response. The geometric-mean center frequency and Q definition apply to that standard response.

5. Active Band-Pass Filter Using Cascaded RC Stages

A simple active band-pass filter can be formed by cascading a first-order high-pass stage and a first-order low-pass stage, with an op-amp buffer or gain stage between or after them.

High-Pass Stage

fL = 1 / (2πR1C1)

This stage attenuates frequencies below its cutoff.

Low-Pass Stage

fH = 1 / (2πR2C2)

This stage attenuates frequencies above its cutoff.

Overall Response

For ideal isolated first-order stages, the overall magnitude is the product of the two stage magnitudes and the amplifier gain:

|H| = Av × [rL/√(1+rL²)] × [1/√(1+rH²)]

where rL=f/fL and rH=f/fH.

The cascade is a useful practical band-pass arrangement, but its peak frequency and −3 dB bandwidth are not exactly the same as those of a standard second-order band-pass section. Loading and stage interaction can also change the response. Use a buffer or an appropriately designed active topology when isolation is required.

6. Band-Pass Filter: Center Frequency and Q

For a standard second-order band-pass filter, the center frequency is the geometric mean of the two half-power frequencies.

f0 = √(fL × fH)
BW = fH − fL
Q = f0/BW

A higher Q indicates a narrower bandwidth relative to the center frequency. A lower Q indicates a wider bandwidth.

Q valueRelative bandwidthResponse
0.5WideLow selectivity
1ModerateModerate selectivity

7. Second-Order Band-Pass Transfer Function

A common normalized second-order band-pass transfer function is:

H(s) = K(sω0/Q) / [s² + sω0/Q + ω0²]

where:

  • K is the peak magnitude gain at the center frequency.
  • ω0=2πf0 is the center angular frequency.
  • Q is the quality factor.

At the center frequency, the magnitude is K. The magnitude at each half-power frequency is K/√2.

Multiple-feedback and state-variable op-amp circuits are common ways to implement second-order active band-pass filters. Their component equations depend on the exact circuit topology.

8. Twin-T Notch Filter

A twin-T network combines a low-pass T network and a high-pass T network in parallel. When balanced, the two paths cancel the signal at a particular frequency.

Balanced Twin-T Component Relationships

  • Two series resistors in the resistive T: R and R.
  • Shunt resistor in the resistive T: R/2.
  • Two series capacitors in the capacitive T: C and C.
  • Shunt capacitor in the capacitive T: 2C.
f0 = 1 / (2πRC)

This is the ideal notch frequency for the balanced passive twin-T network under the stated component relationships.

An op-amp can buffer the twin-T network or provide positive feedback to improve notch selectivity. The resulting Q and stability depend on the specific active topology and feedback factor.

9. Notch Filter Transfer Function and Q

An idealized second-order notch response can be represented by:

H(s) = (s² + ω0²) / (s² + (ω0/Q)s + ω0²)

At s=jω0, the numerator becomes zero, producing an ideal null at the notch frequency. A real circuit has finite attenuation because of component tolerances, op-amp limitations and parasitic effects.

Q = f0 / BW

For a notch filter, bandwidth is measured between the two specified stopband boundary frequencies, usually the half-power points relative to the passband for a standard second-order response.

10. Worked Numerical – Band-Pass Filter

A band-pass filter has lower cutoff frequency 500 Hz and upper cutoff frequency 2 kHz. Calculate center frequency, bandwidth and Q.

Step 1: Center frequency

f0 = √(500 × 2000) = 1000 Hz

Step 2: Bandwidth

BW = 2000 − 500 = 1500 Hz

Step 3: Quality factor

Q = 1000/1500 = 0.667
Center frequency = 1 kHz | Bandwidth = 1.5 kHz | Q ≈ 0.667

11. Worked Numerical – Twin-T Notch Filter

A balanced twin-T notch filter uses R=10 kΩ and C=10 nF. Calculate its ideal notch frequency.

f0 = 1/(2πRC)
f0 = 1/[2π × 10,000 × 10×10⁻⁹]
f0 ≈ 1591.5 Hz
Ideal notch frequency ≈ 1.59 kHz

15. Applications of Band-Pass and Notch Filters

Audio Equalizers

Band-pass filters isolate selected audio bands for processing.

Biomedical Instrumentation

Band-pass filters can isolate useful signal ranges, while notch filters can suppress mains interference.

Communication Systems

Band-pass filters select channels or frequency bands.

Industrial Sensors

Filters help reject unwanted vibration and electrical interference.

Power-Line Interference Removal

Notch filters can attenuate 50 Hz or 60 Hz interference.

Instrumentation

Frequency-selective filtering improves measurement signal quality.

16. Practical Design Considerations

  • Choose the required center frequency and bandwidth before selecting components.
  • Use precision resistors and capacitors when narrow bandwidth or deep notch rejection is required.
  • Check op-amp gain-bandwidth product and slew rate.
  • Check output swing, input common-mode range and supply voltage.
  • Consider the loading between cascaded stages.
  • Use buffers when stage interaction changes the expected response.
  • Keep sensitive filter nodes away from switching noise and high-current traces.
  • Verify actual response using a function generator and oscilloscope or network analyzer.
  • For mains-frequency rejection, consider safety, grounding and isolation requirements.
A notch filter designed for 50 Hz or 60 Hz interference must be tuned to the actual interference frequency. Component tolerances can reduce the depth of the notch substantially.

17. Practical Laboratory

Objective

Construct and test a band-pass filter and a twin-T notch filter.

Components and Equipment

  • Suitable op-amp and regulated power supply.
  • Resistors and capacitors of selected values.
  • Function generator.
  • Oscilloscope or AC voltmeter.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.

Experiment A: Band-Pass Filter

  1. Choose the desired lower and upper cutoff frequencies.
  2. Calculate the RC values for the high-pass and low-pass stages.
  3. Construct the high-pass stage.
  4. Construct the low-pass stage and use buffering if required.
  5. Apply a constant-amplitude sine wave.
  6. Sweep frequency from below fL to above fH.
  7. Record output amplitude and calculate gain at each frequency.
  8. Plot the frequency response and identify the passband.

Experiment B: Notch Filter

  1. Select R and C values using the desired notch frequency.
  2. Construct the balanced twin-T network.
  3. Apply a sine wave and sweep around the calculated notch frequency.
  4. Observe the minimum output amplitude.
  5. Measure the actual notch frequency and compare it with the calculated value.
  6. Investigate the effect of component mismatch.

Expected Result

The band-pass filter passes a selected frequency range. The notch filter produces strong attenuation around its designed rejection frequency.

19. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is a band-pass filter?

A filter that passes a selected range of frequencies and attenuates frequencies outside it.

2. What is a notch filter?

A narrow band-stop filter that strongly attenuates a selected frequency range.

3. What is bandwidth?

The difference between upper and lower cutoff frequencies.

4. How is center frequency calculated?

For a standard second-order band-pass response, f0=√(fL×fH).

5. Define quality factor.

Q=f0/BW for the standard second-order response.

6. What does a high Q indicate?

A narrow bandwidth relative to the center frequency.

7. What is a twin-T network?

A combination of two T-shaped RC networks that produces a notch response when balanced.

8. What is the ideal notch frequency of a balanced twin-T network?

f0=1/(2πRC), using the standard balanced component relationships.

9. Why is an op-amp used in an active filter?

For buffering, isolation and voltage gain.

10. What is a multiple-feedback band-pass filter?

An active filter topology using an op-amp and a feedback network to create a second-order band-pass response.

11. What is the difference between band-pass and band-stop filters?

A band-pass filter passes the selected band; a band-stop filter rejects it.

12. Why are precision components important in notch filters?

Component mismatch reduces the depth of the rejection notch and shifts its frequency.

13. How can a 50 Hz interference signal be removed?

A suitably designed 50 Hz notch filter can attenuate the interference.

14. What happens when Q increases in a standard second-order band-pass filter?

Bandwidth decreases relative to center frequency.

15. What is the role of a buffer between filter stages?

It reduces loading interaction and helps preserve the intended individual stage responses.

16. What practical parameters limit active filter performance?

Gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, noise, output swing and component tolerances.

21. Lecture Summary

  • A band-pass filter passes a selected range of frequencies.
  • A notch filter rejects a selected range of frequencies.
  • Bandwidth is fH−fL.
  • For a standard second-order band-pass response, center frequency is √(fL×fH).
  • Quality factor is f0/BW.
  • A cascaded HPF and LPF can form a practical band-pass arrangement.
  • A balanced twin-T network can produce a notch response.
  • Precision components and op-amp limitations affect real filter performance.
