1. Learning Objectives

  • Follow a systematic method for designing op-amp circuits.
  • Select a suitable op-amp based on electrical specifications.
  • Calculate gain, resistor values and output voltage.
  • Understand supply voltage, input common-mode range and output swing.
  • Evaluate bandwidth, slew rate and stability.
  • Interpret important op-amp datasheet specifications.
  • Test circuits using a multimeter, function generator and oscilloscope.
  • Diagnose common op-amp circuit faults.
  • Prepare for electronics design and troubleshooting interviews.

2. Op-Amp Circuit Design Workflow

Practical circuit design should begin with electrical requirements, not with selecting random components.

Recommended sequence

  1. Define signal amplitude, frequency range and required output.
  2. Choose inverting, non-inverting, buffer, comparator, filter or another suitable topology.
  3. Calculate gain and select practical resistor and capacitor values.
  4. Choose an op-amp that satisfies supply, input, output, bandwidth and noise requirements.
  5. Build and test the circuit in stages.
  6. Compare measured performance with calculated performance.

3. Define the Circuit Requirements

Before selecting an op-amp, document the required electrical specifications.

ParameterDesign question
Input voltage rangeWhat is the minimum and maximum input voltage?
Output voltage rangeWhat output range must the circuit produce?
Signal frequencyWhat is the minimum and maximum operating frequency?
GainWhat voltage gain is required?
Supply voltageIs the circuit powered from a single or dual supply?
Input impedanceHow much loading can the source tolerate?
AccuracyWhat offset, gain error and noise are acceptable?
LoadWhat output resistance and load current are required?
Example requirement: amplify a 100 mV peak sensor signal to 1 V peak over a specified frequency range. The nominal voltage gain is 10, but supply and output headroom must also be checked.

4. Selecting the Correct Op-Amp

Supply Voltage

Check the permitted supply range and whether single-supply or dual-supply operation is supported.

Input Common-Mode Range

Verify that the input voltage remains within the allowed range for the selected supply.

Output Swing

Check how close the output can approach each supply rail for the required load.

Gain-Bandwidth Product

Check whether the op-amp can provide the required closed-loop gain at the operating frequency.

Slew Rate

Check the maximum output voltage rate of change.

Input Offset Voltage

Important when measuring small DC signals or using high closed-loop gain.

Input Bias Current

Important with high source impedance and large feedback resistors.

Noise

Check voltage noise, current noise and noise bandwidth for low-level signals.

Do not select an op-amp only because it is labelled rail-to-rail or high speed. Verify its actual datasheet specifications at the intended supply voltage, load and temperature.

5. Designing an Inverting Amplifier

The inverting amplifier produces an output with opposite polarity to the input.

Av = Vout/Vin = −Rf/Rin
Rf = |Av| × Rin

Design Example

Required gain = −5 and Rin=10 kΩ.

Rf = 5 × 10 kΩ = 50 kΩ
Choose Rf = 50 kΩ for an ideal gain of −5.

6. Designing a Non-Inverting Amplifier

The output is in phase with the input signal.

Av = 1 + Rf/Rg
Rf = (Av − 1)Rg

Design Example

Required gain = 11 and Rg=10 kΩ.

Rf = (11 − 1) × 10 kΩ = 100 kΩ
Choose Rf = 100 kΩ and Rg = 10 kΩ.

7. Designing a Voltage Follower

A voltage follower connects the output directly to the inverting input and applies the input signal to the non-inverting input.

Av = 1

It is used for impedance buffering, isolation between stages and driving a suitable load without heavily loading the source.

8. Gain-Bandwidth Product

For a dominant-pole, internally compensated op-amp, the gain-bandwidth product is approximately constant over its useful closed-loop operating range.

Closed-loop bandwidth ≈ GBW / Noise Gain

For a non-inverting amplifier, noise gain is approximately its closed-loop gain. For an inverting amplifier, noise gain is 1+Rf/Rin, not simply the magnitude of its signal gain.

Example

An op-amp has GBW=1 MHz and an inverting amplifier uses Rf=90 kΩ and Rin=10 kΩ.

Noise gain = 1 + 90/10 = 10
Bandwidth ≈ 1 MHz/10 = 100 kHz
Approximate closed-loop bandwidth = 100 kHz.
This is a first-order approximation. Actual bandwidth depends on the device, compensation, loading and circuit topology.

9. Slew Rate Requirement

Slew rate is the maximum rate at which an op-amp output voltage can change.

SR = max |dVout/dt|

For a sinusoidal output:

SRrequired = 2πfVpeak

Example

Required output: 5 V peak at 20 kHz.

SR = 2π × 20,000 × 5
SR ≈ 0.628 V/µs
Choose an op-amp with adequate slew-rate margin above 0.628 V/µs.

10. Feedback Resistor and Component Selection

  • Choose resistor values that provide the required gain without excessive loading.
  • Very large resistors increase sensitivity to input bias current, leakage and noise.
  • Very small resistors increase output current demand.
  • Use stable, suitable-tolerance resistors for accurate gain.
  • Choose capacitors with suitable tolerance, voltage rating and dielectric characteristics.
  • Check the op-amp's capacitive-load stability when driving a large capacitor.
  • Do not assume that larger feedback resistors always improve performance.
A common starting range for many low-frequency general-purpose designs is several kΩ to tens of kΩ, but the optimum values depend on the op-amp, signal source, noise, bandwidth and load.

11. Power Supply and Decoupling

Op-amp supply pins need a low-impedance supply path. Local bypass capacitors reduce supply noise and help prevent unwanted oscillation.

  • Place decoupling capacitors close to the IC supply pins.
  • Use the datasheet-recommended capacitor values and layout.
  • Provide a short return path to the appropriate ground or supply reference.
  • Use bulk capacitance where the board's supply distribution requires it.

13. Practical Circuit Testing Procedure

Before Power-On

  1. Verify the IC part number and pin configuration.
  2. Check the circuit against the schematic.
  3. Measure resistor values and check capacitor polarity where applicable.
  4. Check supply polarity and current limit.
  5. Inspect for shorts between supply rails and ground.

After Power-On

  1. Measure supply voltage directly at the IC pins.
  2. Measure input and output DC voltages.
  3. Apply a small known input signal.
  4. Measure gain and phase relationship.
  5. Increase signal amplitude gradually.
  6. Sweep frequency to check bandwidth and distortion.
  7. Check for unexpected oscillation with an oscilloscope.

14. Debugging Methodology

Step 1: Verify Supply

Confirm correct supply voltage, polarity and ground reference.

Step 2: Verify Input

Measure the signal at the IC input pin, not only at the source.

Step 3: Verify Feedback

Check feedback resistor connections and continuity.

Step 4: Check Operating Range

Check input common-mode voltage and output swing.

Step 5: Inspect Waveform

Look for clipping, oscillation, noise and slew-rate distortion.

Step 6: Isolate Stages

Test individual circuit stages to locate the fault.

19. Practical Laboratory: Inverting Amplifier Design and Debugging

Objective

Design, construct and test an inverting amplifier and investigate its gain, output swing and frequency response.

Components and Equipment

  • Suitable general-purpose op-amp.
  • 10 kΩ input resistor and selected feedback resistor.
  • Regulated power supply compatible with the IC.
  • Function generator and oscilloscope.
  • Digital multimeter and breadboard.

Procedure

  1. Design an inverting amplifier for a gain of −5.
  2. Choose Rin=10 kΩ and calculate Rf.
  3. Check the selected op-amp's supply and input/output limitations.
  4. Construct the circuit and verify the wiring before powering it.
  5. Apply a low-amplitude sine wave.
  6. Measure input and output amplitudes.
  7. Verify the gain and phase inversion.
  8. Increase input amplitude gradually and observe clipping.
  9. Increase frequency and observe bandwidth-related changes.
  10. Record measured results and compare with calculations.

Expected Result

The output should be approximately five times the input amplitude and inverted in phase, provided the circuit operates within its linear voltage, current and frequency limits.

20. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is an operational amplifier?

A high-gain differential voltage amplifier commonly used with feedback to implement analog functions.

2. What is negative feedback?

Feedback that opposes the input difference and establishes a controlled closed-loop response.

3. What is the gain of an inverting amplifier?

Av=−Rf/Rin.

4. What is the gain of a non-inverting amplifier?

Av=1+Rf/Rg.

5. What is a voltage follower?

A unity-gain buffer with output connected to the inverting input.

6. Define gain-bandwidth product.

For a dominant-pole op-amp, it approximates the product of closed-loop noise gain and bandwidth over the useful operating range.

7. What is slew rate?

The maximum rate of change of the output voltage.

8. What causes slew-rate distortion?

The required output rate of change exceeds the op-amp's slew-rate capability.

9. What is input offset voltage?

The small differential input voltage required to make the output zero in a real op-amp under specified conditions.

10. What is input bias current?

The current flowing into or out of the input terminals of the op-amp.

11. Why are decoupling capacitors used?

They provide local supply bypassing and reduce supply impedance and noise.

12. Why does an op-amp output saturate?

The required output exceeds the available output swing or the circuit is driven outside its linear operating conditions.

13. What is phase margin?

A measure of relative stability in a feedback system, evaluated at the unity loop-gain frequency.

14. Why can capacitive loads cause instability?

They add phase shift and can reduce feedback-loop phase margin.

15. What is rail-to-rail output?

An output stage designed to swing close to one or both supply rails, within specified load-dependent limits.

16. What is the difference between GBW limitation and slew-rate limitation?

GBW is a small-signal frequency limitation; slew rate is a large-signal rate-of-change limitation.

17. What should be checked first when an op-amp circuit does not work?

Supply voltage, pinout, ground reference, input conditions and feedback connections.

18. Why should the datasheet be checked before circuit construction?

To verify supply, input, output, frequency, load and stability limits.

21. Numerical Practice

  1. An inverting amplifier uses Rin=4.7 kΩ and Rf=47 kΩ. Calculate its gain.
  2. A non-inverting amplifier requires gain 21. If Rg=5 kΩ, calculate Rf.
  3. An op-amp has GBW=2 MHz and non-inverting gain 20. Estimate its closed-loop bandwidth.
  4. Calculate the slew rate required for a 3 V peak sine wave at 50 kHz.
  5. An inverting amplifier has gain −10 and input 0.3 V. Calculate the ideal output.
  6. Explain why an op-amp powered from ±5 V may not produce a 6 V peak output.
Show answers
  1. Av=−47/4.7=−10.
  2. Rf=(21−1)×5 kΩ=100 kΩ.
  3. BW≈2 MHz/20=100 kHz.
  4. SR=2π×50,000×3≈0.942 V/µs.
  5. Vout=−10×0.3=−3 V.
  6. The required output exceeds the available supply rails and output swing.

22. Lecture Summary

  • Begin circuit design by defining electrical requirements.
  • Select an op-amp based on supply, input, output, bandwidth, slew rate and noise.
  • Calculate gain and component values before construction.
  • Check the input common-mode range and output swing.
  • Use datasheet specifications rather than assumptions.
  • Use decoupling capacitors and sound layout practices.
  • Test supply, input, feedback and output systematically.
  • Use a multimeter and oscilloscope to isolate faults.
  • Compare calculated performance with measured results.
