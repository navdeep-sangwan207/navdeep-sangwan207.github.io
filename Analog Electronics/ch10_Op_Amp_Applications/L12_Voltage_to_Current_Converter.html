2. What Is a Voltage-to-Current Converter?

A voltage-to-current converter produces an output current proportional to an applied input voltage. It is also called a transconductance amplifier.

Iout = gm × Vin

Here, gm is transconductance, measured in siemens (S), equivalent to amperes per volt (A/V).

Input

Voltage signal from a sensor, controller or signal source.

Conversion

An op-amp and resistor network establish a controlled current.

Output

Current delivered through a load within the circuit's compliance limits.

3. Basic Op-Amp Voltage-to-Current Converter

A useful basic configuration uses an op-amp to maintain a voltage across a current-sensing resistor. The load current is determined by the input voltage and sensing resistance.

Important: The basic concept is that negative feedback controls the voltage across a known sensing resistor. The resulting current is approximately the voltage across that resistor divided by its resistance. The exact polarity and load connection depend on the chosen circuit topology.

4. Grounded-Load V-I Converter

A grounded-load current source is designed so that one side of the load is connected to ground. The op-amp drives the other side of the load while maintaining a controlled current.

One common implementation uses a transistor as the output current-control element, with a sensing resistor in its feedback path. This avoids requiring the op-amp itself to deliver the entire load current.

Operating principle

  1. The input voltage establishes the desired current.
  2. The op-amp compares the sensing voltage with its reference input.
  3. The feedback loop adjusts the transistor drive.
  4. The transistor changes its conduction to regulate load current.
  5. The current remains approximately constant while the circuit has sufficient voltage headroom.
This is a functional block-level illustration, not a construction-ready transistor schematic. Actual transistor polarity, pinout, resistor connections and feedback polarity must be selected and verified for the chosen design.

5. Floating-Load V-I Converter

In a floating-load configuration, the load is not required to have one terminal grounded. The current-control circuit can regulate current through a load connected between its output terminals.

Grounded Load

One load terminal is connected to the circuit ground. The output stage controls current through the other terminal.

Floating Load

The load is placed within the controlled current path and may be referenced to neither supply ground nor the control input ground.

Floating-load arrangements are useful when the load must be isolated from ground or when the current path is part of a differential interface.

The terms grounded-load and floating-load describe load connection and current-path topology. Always identify the exact circuit before applying a formula.

6. Transconductance and Design Equation

For a current-control topology that establishes a sensing voltage equal to the input voltage:

Iout = Vin / Rs
gm = Iout / Vin = 1 / Rs

This equation assumes the feedback loop forces the sensing resistor voltage to equal the applied control voltage, with the stated polarity and negligible error.

Design Example

Required output current is 2 mA for an input voltage of 1 V.

Rs = Vin / Iout = 1 / 0.002 = 500 Ω
Required sensing resistance = 500 Ω.

7. Current Direction and Polarity

Current direction depends on the circuit's topology and the sign of the input voltage.

Input conditionIdeal current response
Positive input voltageCurrent flows in the designated positive direction.
Zero input voltageIdeal controlled current is zero.
Negative input voltageCurrent reverses direction only if the circuit supports bidirectional operation.
A single-supply current sink may not support negative input commands or reverse current. Bidirectional current control generally requires an appropriate output stage and supply arrangement.

8. Load Compliance Voltage

A current source can regulate its output only while the output stage has enough voltage headroom to force the required current through the load.

Vload = Iout × RL

For a simplified series current path, the required supply headroom must also account for the sensing resistor, transistor voltage drop and op-amp output requirements.

Vrequired ≈ Iout(RL + Rs) + Vheadroom

This is a simplified design estimate, not a universal equation. The exact voltage budget depends on the circuit topology and component operating conditions.

Example

For a 10 mA current through a 100 Ω load:

Vload = 0.01 × 100 = 1 V

If the available output stage cannot provide the required voltage across the load and its control components, the current will fall below the set value.

9. Practical Design Considerations

  • Op-amp input range: The feedback and reference voltages must remain inside the permitted common-mode range.
  • Output swing: The op-amp must provide sufficient drive voltage for the output device.
  • Output current: Use an external transistor when the required load current exceeds the op-amp's capability.
  • Power dissipation: Calculate transistor and sensing-resistor dissipation.
  • Resistor tolerance: Sensing-resistor tolerance directly affects current accuracy.
  • Temperature: Resistor temperature coefficient and transistor thermal behaviour affect current stability.
  • Stability: Compensation may be necessary because of transistor and load capacitances.
  • Protection: Consider short-circuit, reverse-polarity and overtemperature protection.

10. Power Dissipation Calculations

For a sensing resistor:

PRs = I²Rs

For a load:

Pload = I²RL

Example

Current = 20 mA and sensing resistor = 100 Ω.

PRs = (0.02)² × 100 = 0.04 W
Sensing resistor dissipation = 40 mW.

Select a suitable resistor rating with adequate thermal and reliability margin.

13. Applications of Voltage-to-Current Converters

Industrial Transmitters

Voltage-controlled current stages can be used in industrial signal-conditioning systems.

LED Driving

Constant-current circuits regulate LED current within their design limits.

Sensor Interfaces

Convert a voltage representing a measured quantity into a proportional current.

Actuator Control

Provide controlled current to suitable actuator or control interfaces.

Battery Charging

Current-regulation stages are used in appropriately designed charging systems.

Analog Signal Processing

Transconductance stages interface voltage signals with current-mode circuits.

14. Laboratory Experiment

Objective

Verify the relationship between input voltage, sensing resistance and output current.

Components

  • Suitable op-amp current-source circuit or educational V-I converter module.
  • Precision sensing resistor.
  • Suitable load resistor.
  • Regulated power supply, multimeter and breadboard.

Procedure

  1. Build a verified V-I converter circuit using a selected topology.
  2. Check all supply and feedback connections before powering the circuit.
  3. Apply a small positive input voltage.
  4. Measure the voltage across the sensing resistor.
  5. Calculate current using I=Vsense/Rs.
  6. Change the input voltage and repeat the measurement.
  7. Change the load resistance while remaining within the circuit's compliance range.
  8. Compare measured current with the theoretical value.
  9. Record the error and investigate possible causes.

16. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is a voltage-to-current converter?

A circuit that produces an output current proportional to an input voltage.

2. What is another name for a V-I converter?

Transconductance amplifier.

3. What is transconductance?

The ratio of output current to input voltage, measured in siemens.

4. What is the basic current relationship?

For the specified sensing-resistor feedback topology, Iout=Vin/Rs.

5. Why is an op-amp used in a current source?

Negative feedback can regulate the voltage across a sensing resistor and therefore control current.

6. What is a current-sensing resistor?

A resistor used to produce a measurable voltage proportional to current.

7. What is compliance voltage?

The voltage range over which a current source can maintain its specified current.

8. Why may output current decrease when load resistance increases?

The circuit may run out of voltage headroom.

9. Why might an external transistor be required?

To provide load current beyond the op-amp's direct output-current capability.

10. How does sense-resistor tolerance affect accuracy?

Since current is inversely proportional to resistance, resistance error produces current error.

11. What is the unit of transconductance?

Siemens (S), equivalent to A/V.

12. What happens if the output device overheats?

Its parameters may change, protection may activate or the device may fail.

13. Can every V-I converter provide reverse current?

No. Bidirectional current requires a topology and supply arrangement designed for it.

14. Why is feedback important?

It adjusts the output stage to maintain the desired sensing voltage and current.

15. What is the difference between a voltage amplifier and a transconductance amplifier?

A voltage amplifier controls output voltage, while a transconductance amplifier controls output current in response to input voltage.

17. Numerical Practice

  1. A V-I converter has Vin=2 V and Rs=1 kΩ. Calculate output current.
  2. Find Rs for Vin=0.5 V and Iout=5 mA.
  3. Calculate the transconductance if 4 mA is produced by a 2 V input.
  4. Calculate load voltage for 15 mA through 220 Ω.
  5. Calculate sense-resistor power for 20 mA through 150 Ω.
  6. Explain why a current source cannot maintain its set current for unlimited load resistance.
Show answers
  1. I=2/1000=2 mA.
  2. Rs=0.5/0.005=100 Ω.
  3. gm=0.004/2=0.002 S=2 mS.
  4. V=0.015×220=3.3 V.
  5. P=(0.02)²×150=0.06 W=60 mW.
  6. The output stage has finite supply voltage and voltage headroom. Beyond the compliance limit, current regulation fails.

18. Lecture Summary

  • A V-I converter transforms an input voltage into a proportional output current.
  • Transconductance is measured in siemens.
  • For the specified sensing-resistor feedback arrangement, Iout=Vin/Rs.
  • Grounded-load and floating-load configurations serve different connection requirements.
  • Load compliance determines the usable load-resistance range.
  • Sense-resistor tolerance, offset voltage and thermal effects influence accuracy.
  • External transistors can increase current-driving capability.
  • Practical circuits must be checked for stability, dissipation and protection.
