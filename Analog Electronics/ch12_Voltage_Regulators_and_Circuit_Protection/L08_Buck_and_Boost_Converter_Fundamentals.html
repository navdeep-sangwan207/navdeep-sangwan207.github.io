Lesson 08: Current Limiting Circuits

Understand how current limiting circuits protect electronic components and power supplies against excessive load current and overload conditions.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the meaning of current limiting.
  • Identify the need for current protection in electronic circuits.
  • Understand resistor-based current limiting.
  • Study transistor-based current limiting circuits.
  • Calculate current limit values using sense resistors.
  • Understand current limiting in voltage regulators.
  • Differentiate between current limiting and current regulation.

2. Introduction to Current Limiting

Current limiting is a protection technique used to restrict the maximum current flowing through an electronic circuit or load.

When a load draws excessive current, the current limiting circuit prevents the current from increasing beyond a designed threshold.

Why Is Current Limiting Required?

  • To protect electronic components from excessive current.
  • To prevent damage during short-circuit conditions.
  • To protect power supply circuits from overload.
  • To reduce excessive heating in components.
  • To improve circuit reliability and safety.
Current limiting restricts the current to a safe level. It does not necessarily disconnect the power supply.

3. Basic Current Limiting Using a Resistor

A resistor connected in series with a load can limit the current by producing a voltage drop.

The series resistor limits current according to the supply voltage, load voltage and resistor value.

I = (Vsupply − Vload) / R

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Supply voltage = 12 V
  • LED forward voltage = 2 V
  • Desired current = 20 mA
R = (12 − 2) / 0.02

R = 10 / 0.02

R = 500 Ω

A suitable standard resistor value can be selected after checking the LED current and resistor power rating.

4. Transistor-Based Current Limiting

A transistor can be used as a current sensing and control element. When the voltage across a sense resistor becomes sufficiently large, the transistor starts conducting and reduces the drive to the pass transistor.

Conceptual representation of a transistor-based current limiting arrangement. Actual transistor connections depend on the circuit topology.

Working Principle

  1. Load current flows through the current-sensing resistor.
  2. A voltage develops across the sense resistor.
  3. When this voltage reaches the transistor's turn-on threshold, the sensing transistor conducts.
  4. It reduces the drive available to the pass transistor.
  5. The output current is therefore limited.

5. Current Sensing Resistor

A current-sensing resistor, also called a shunt resistor, converts current into a measurable voltage.

Vsense = Iload × Rsense

In a simple BJT current limiter, the sensing transistor begins conducting when the voltage across the sense resistor approaches its base-emitter turn-on voltage.

Ilimit ≈ VBE / Rsense

For a first-order silicon BJT estimate, VBE is often taken as approximately 0.6–0.7 V.

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Approximate transistor turn-on voltage = 0.65 V
  • Sense resistor = 0.33 Ω
Ilimit ≈ 0.65 / 0.33

Ilimit ≈ 1.97 A
This is an approximate threshold calculation, not a precision current limit. Actual current depends on transistor characteristics, temperature, resistor tolerance and circuit topology.

6. Current Limiting in Voltage Regulators

Many voltage regulator ICs include internal current limiting to protect their internal pass transistor and output stage.

Normal Operation

  • The regulator supplies the required load current.
  • The output voltage remains within its regulation range.
  • The current remains below the protection threshold.

Overload Operation

  • The load attempts to draw excessive current.
  • The regulator detects the overload condition.
  • The internal protection circuit limits output current.
  • The output voltage may decrease as the load demand increases.
The current limit value is device-specific. Do not assume that every regulator can continuously supply its advertised maximum current under all thermal conditions.

7. Constant Current Limiting

A constant current limiting circuit attempts to keep output current near a predetermined maximum value when the load demands more current than the setting.

A feedback circuit monitors the voltage across a sensing resistor and adjusts a transistor or pass element to maintain the desired current.

Ilimit = Vsense(reference) / Rsense

This principle is used in current-limited power supplies, LED drivers and battery charging circuits.

8. Foldback Current Limiting

Foldback current limiting reduces the allowed output current as the output voltage falls during a severe overload or short circuit.

Working Principle

  1. During normal operation, the circuit supplies its rated load current.
  2. When the load becomes excessive, the protection circuit responds.
  3. As the output voltage falls, the permitted current is reduced.
  4. During a short circuit, the current can be much lower than the normal current limit.
Foldback protection can reduce power dissipation in the pass transistor during a short circuit. It requires a specifically designed sensing and feedback network.

9. Types of Current Limiting Techniques

Technique Working Principle Typical Use
Series resistor Limits current through resistance. LEDs and simple circuits.
Transistor-based limiter Controls pass transistor drive using current sensing. Linear power supplies.
IC-based limiting Uses internal sensing and protection circuitry. Voltage regulator ICs.
Constant current limiter Maintains current near a preset threshold. LED drivers and chargers.
Foldback limiter Reduces current as output voltage falls. Short-circuit protection.

10. Current Limiting vs Current Regulation

Current Limiting Current Regulation
Restricts current above a set threshold. Maintains a selected current over its operating range.
Often operates during overload conditions. Can operate continuously during normal operation.
Output voltage may fall during limiting. Load voltage adjusts to maintain the set current.
Commonly used for protection. Commonly used for controlled current delivery.

11. Power Dissipation in Sense Resistor

The current-sensing resistor dissipates power as current flows through it.

PR = I² × R

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Current = 2 A
  • Sense resistance = 0.1 Ω
PR = 2² × 0.1

PR = 4 × 0.1

PR = 0.4 W

The resistor should have a suitable power rating with adequate thermal margin. Its tolerance and temperature coefficient also affect current-limit accuracy.

12. Advantages

  • Protects electronic components against excessive current.
  • Reduces damage during overload conditions.
  • Improves power supply reliability.
  • Can be implemented using resistors, transistors or ICs.
  • Useful in battery chargers and LED driver circuits.
  • Can be combined with thermal and short-circuit protection.

13. Limitations

  • Series resistors cause power loss and voltage drop.
  • Transistor-based circuits require correct biasing and thermal design.
  • Sense resistor tolerance affects the current threshold.
  • Current limiting alone may not prevent overheating during prolonged overload.
  • Foldback circuits require additional design complexity.

14. Applications

  • Regulated DC power supplies.
  • Battery charging circuits.
  • LED driver circuits.
  • Motor driver protection.
  • Microcontroller power supply protection.
  • Laboratory bench power supplies.
  • Industrial electronic equipment.

18. Lesson Summary

  • Current limiting restricts excessive current in an electronic circuit.
  • It protects components and power supplies against overload.
  • Resistors, transistors and regulator ICs can implement current limiting.
  • A sense resistor converts current into a measurable voltage.
  • Constant current limiting maintains current near a preset threshold.
  • Foldback limiting reduces current further during severe overload.
  • Sense resistor power dissipation and thermal limits must be considered.
