2. What Is a Zener Diode?

A Zener diode is a semiconductor diode designed to operate in the reverse-breakdown region. When reverse voltage reaches its breakdown voltage, the diode conducts significant reverse current while maintaining an approximately constant voltage across it, provided current remains within the specified operating range.

Two physical mechanisms can contribute to reverse breakdown:

  • Zener breakdown: Strong electric fields cause quantum-mechanical tunnelling, particularly in heavily doped junctions with relatively low breakdown voltages.
  • Avalanche breakdown: Impact ionization produces additional charge carriers, commonly significant at higher breakdown voltages.
A Zener diode is not intended to operate with unlimited reverse current. A series resistor or current-limiting circuit is essential to prevent damage.

3. Zener Diode V-I Characteristics

In forward bias, the Zener diode behaves approximately like an ordinary silicon diode. In reverse bias, current remains small until the breakdown region is reached. Beyond breakdown, a relatively small change in voltage can produce a large change in current.

Conceptual characteristic. The exact curve, breakdown voltage and dynamic resistance depend on the selected diode.

Forward region

Current increases when the diode is sufficiently forward biased.

Reverse leakage

Only a small reverse current flows before breakdown.

Breakdown region

Reverse current rises substantially while diode voltage changes relatively little.

4. Zener Shunt Regulator Circuit

A Zener shunt regulator uses a series resistor to limit current and a reverse-biased Zener diode connected in parallel with the load. Both the Zener diode and load receive approximately the Zener voltage.

How the circuit works

  1. The input voltage supplies current through series resistor RS.
  2. At the output node, current divides between the Zener diode and the load.
  3. The Zener operates in reverse breakdown and holds the output near VZ.
  4. If load current decreases, more current flows through the Zener.
  5. If load current increases, Zener current decreases to supply the additional load current.
IS = IZ + IL
VOUT ≈ VZ
The regulator works only while the Zener current stays within its permitted regulation range and the series resistor and diode remain within their power ratings.

5. Derivation of Zener Regulator Equations

Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law to the input and series resistor:

VIN = IS RS + VZ

Therefore, the current through the series resistor is:

IS = (VIN − VZ) / RS

Applying Kirchhoff's Current Law at the output node:

IS = IZ + IL

For a resistive load:

IL = VZ / RL

Hence:

IZ = IS − IL

Substituting:

IZ = (VIN − VZ)/RS − VZ/RL

Power calculations

PZ = VZ × IZ
PRS = IS² × RS
PL = VZ × IL

Use worst-case operating conditions when selecting component ratings. Zener voltage and current are not perfectly constant in a real device.

6. Step-by-Step Zener Regulator Design

Consider the following design requirements:

  • Input voltage range: 12 V to 15 V.
  • Required output voltage: approximately 5.1 V.
  • Load resistance: 1 kΩ.
  • Selected Zener: 5.1 V, with adequate current and power ratings.

Step 1 – Calculate load current

IL = 5.1 / 1000 = 5.1 mA

Step 2 – Select a minimum Zener current

For this example, assume a design minimum of 5 mA. This is an illustrative design assumption; the actual minimum should be taken from the selected diode's datasheet and required regulation accuracy.

Step 3 – Calculate the maximum series resistance

Minimum input voltage and maximum load current give the minimum Zener current.

RS(max) = (VIN(min) − VZ) / (IL(max) + IZ(min))
RS(max) = (12 − 5.1) / (5.1 mA + 5 mA)
RS(max) ≈ 683 Ω

Choose a standard resistor of 680 Ω.

Step 4 – Check minimum Zener current

At VIN = 12 V:

IS = (12 − 5.1)/680 ≈ 10.15 mA
IZ = 10.15 − 5.1 ≈ 5.05 mA

The assumed minimum current is just satisfied under nominal component values.

Step 5 – Check maximum Zener current

At VIN = 15 V and with the load disconnected:

IZ(max) ≈ (15 − 5.1)/680 = 14.56 mA

Step 6 – Check Zener power

PZ(max) ≈ 5.1 × 14.56 mA = 74.3 mW

A suitable diode must have a power rating above this calculated dissipation with adequate thermal and design margin.

Step 7 – Check series resistor power

PRS(max) = (15 − 5.1)² / 680 ≈ 144 mW

A resistor rated above the calculated power is required; a 0.25 W resistor may be a reasonable starting selection under suitable ambient and derating conditions.

These calculations use nominal Zener voltage and idealized operation. A production design must account for Zener voltage tolerance, dynamic resistance, temperature coefficient, minimum knee current, resistor tolerance, input extremes and thermal derating.

9. Zener Regulator with Changing Load

When the input voltage is fixed, a changing load current changes the current available to the Zener diode.

Load conditionLoad currentZener currentExpected behavior
Light loadSmallHigherMore current flows through Zener.
Increasing loadIncreasingDecreasingZener current supplies less of the total current.
Heavy loadLargeMay fall below knee currentOutput regulation may be lost.
A Zener shunt regulator is most suitable for relatively low-current loads. Its efficiency can be poor because current continues to flow through the Zener and series resistor even at light load.

10. Practical Applications

  • Simple low-current reference voltage circuits.
  • Voltage clamping and input protection circuits.
  • Biasing and reference networks.
  • Low-power regulated supplies.
  • Overvoltage detection and threshold circuits.
  • Reference circuits for transistor and analog stages.

11. Advantages and Limitations

Advantages
  • Simple circuit.
  • Low component count.
  • Low cost.
  • Useful for low-current regulation.
  • Can provide a simple voltage reference.
Limitations
  • Limited load-current capability.
  • Continuous current and power dissipation.
  • Output changes with Zener current and temperature.
  • Dynamic resistance causes imperfect regulation.
  • Not efficient for many higher-power applications.

12. Practical Lab – Build and Test a Zener Regulator

Objective: Construct a low-voltage Zener shunt regulator and measure output voltage as input voltage and load resistance change.

Components and instruments

  • 5.1 V Zener diode with suitable power rating.
  • 680 Ω resistor, appropriately rated.
  • 1 kΩ and 2.2 kΩ load resistors.
  • Current-limited DC bench supply.
  • Digital multimeter and breadboard.

Procedure

  1. Switch off the supply before wiring the circuit.
  2. Connect the series resistor between the positive input and output node.
  3. Connect the Zener cathode to the output node and its anode to ground.
  4. Connect the load resistor in parallel with the Zener diode.
  5. Check the diode orientation and resistor values before applying power.
  6. Set a current-limited input supply to 12 V.
  7. Measure the output voltage across the Zener and load.
  8. Repeat measurements at different input voltages within the design range.
  9. Change the load resistance and observe the output voltage and Zener current.
  10. Record component temperatures and verify power ratings.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a Zener diode?

A diode designed to operate in reverse breakdown for voltage regulation or reference applications.

2. Why is a Zener diode reverse biased in a regulator?

Its reverse-breakdown region provides an approximately constant voltage over a specified current range.

3. What is a shunt regulator?

A regulator in which the regulating element is connected in parallel with the load and diverts excess current.

4. What is the function of the series resistor?

It limits current and drops the excess input voltage.

5. Why is the Zener connected in parallel with the load?

So that its regulated voltage appears across the load.

6. What happens when load current increases?

Zener current decreases because more of the series current is consumed by the load.

7. What happens if Zener current falls below knee current?

The diode may leave its specified regulation region and output voltage can fall.

8. What is Zener dynamic resistance?

The small-signal change in Zener voltage divided by the corresponding change in Zener current in a specified operating region.

9. What is Zener power dissipation?

Approximately the product of Zener voltage and Zener current.

10. Why is a Zener regulator inefficient at light load?

Current continues flowing through the series resistor and Zener even when the load draws little current.

11. Can a Zener diode be used as an overvoltage clamp?

Yes, if its voltage, current, pulse power and thermal ratings suit the application.

12. What is the difference between Zener and avalanche breakdown?

Zener breakdown is associated with strong-field tunnelling, while avalanche breakdown is associated with impact ionization.

13. Why must the Zener power rating be checked?

Excessive dissipation can overheat and damage the diode.

14. What happens if the Zener diode is connected in forward bias?

It behaves approximately like an ordinary silicon diode, with a forward voltage drop rather than its rated Zener voltage.

15. How do you select the series resistor?

Use minimum input voltage and maximum load current to ensure sufficient minimum Zener current, then check maximum input and minimum load for excessive Zener current and power.

16. Why does Zener voltage vary with temperature?

The semiconductor breakdown mechanism and junction characteristics have temperature coefficients.

17. What is the main limitation of a Zener shunt regulator?

Limited current capability and relatively poor efficiency for higher load currents.

18. How would you test a Zener regulator?

Measure input voltage, output voltage, series current, load current and Zener current while varying input and load conditions.

15. Numerical Practice

  1. A 6.2 V Zener diode is connected to a 15 V supply through a 470 Ω resistor. Calculate the series current with the load disconnected.
  2. A 5.1 V Zener regulator supplies a 1 kΩ load. Calculate load current.
  3. The series current is 20 mA and load current is 8 mA. Calculate Zener current.
  4. A Zener operates at 9.1 V and 30 mA. Calculate power dissipation.
  5. A 5.6 V Zener is connected to a 12 V source through 330 Ω. Calculate current with the load disconnected and resistor power.
  6. Explain why a Zener regulator may fail when the load resistance is reduced substantially.
Show answers
  1. IS = (15 − 6.2)/470 = 18.72 mA.
  2. IL = 5.1/1000 = 5.1 mA.
  3. IZ = 20 − 8 = 12 mA.
  4. PZ = 9.1 × 0.03 = 273 mW.
  5. IS = (12 − 5.6)/330 = 19.39 mA. PR = IS² × 330 ≈ 124 mW.
  6. Higher load current reduces the current available to the Zener. If it falls below the required regulation current, output voltage drops.
