Lesson 09: Short-Circuit Protection

Understand short-circuit faults, their effects on electronic circuits and the protection techniques used in regulated power supplies and electronic equipment.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand short-circuit conditions.
  • Identify common causes of short circuits.
  • Understand the effects of excessive fault current.
  • Study fuse and resettable protection devices.
  • Understand electronic current limiting and shutdown.
  • Differentiate between current limiting and circuit disconnection.
  • Learn practical troubleshooting methods.

2. Introduction to Short-Circuit Protection

A short circuit occurs when an unintended low-resistance path is created between two points having different electrical potentials.

The low-resistance path can cause a very large current to flow, depending on the supply voltage and the total resistance and impedance of the circuit.

Common Causes

  • Damaged insulation on wires.
  • Incorrect circuit connections.
  • PCB copper track or solder bridges.
  • Failed electronic components.
  • Loose conductive objects touching circuit terminals.
  • Incorrect polarity or component installation.
A short circuit is different from a normal overload. A short circuit creates an unintended low-impedance path, whereas an overload occurs when a load draws more current than the circuit is designed to supply.

3. Effects of a Short Circuit

  • Excessive current through wires and PCB tracks.
  • Overheating of power supply components.
  • Damage to voltage regulators and transistors.
  • Fuse operation or protection shutdown.
  • Voltage collapse at the power supply output.
  • Possible fire or equipment damage if protection is inadequate.

Fault Current

Ifault = V / Rtotal

A lower total circuit resistance results in a higher fault current for a given supply voltage.

Numerical Example

Consider an ideal 12 V source connected through a total fault-path resistance of 0.5 Ω.

Ifault = 12 / 0.5

Ifault = 24 A
This is an illustrative calculation. Actual fault current is limited by the source, wiring, internal resistance, impedance and any protection device.

5. Fuse-Based Short-Circuit Protection

A fuse is a protective device connected in series with a circuit. It contains a metal element that melts when excessive current flows for a sufficient duration.

Working Principle

  1. Normal load current flows through the fuse element.
  2. During a fault, current increases.
  3. The fuse element heats up due to I²R power dissipation.
  4. When the operating condition exceeds its time-current characteristics, the element melts.
  5. The circuit is disconnected and current stops flowing.
P = I²R

Types of Fuses

  • Fast-acting fuse.
  • Time-delay or slow-blow fuse.
  • Cartridge fuse.
  • Surface-mount fuse.
Fuse selection must consider rated current, voltage, breaking capacity, time-current characteristics and the available fault current. A fuse is not selected using its current rating alone.

6. Resettable PTC Fuse

A resettable PTC (Positive Temperature Coefficient) device increases its resistance significantly when its temperature rises due to excessive current.

Working Principle

  1. Under normal conditions, the PTC device has relatively low resistance.
  2. During excessive current, it heats up.
  3. Its resistance increases sharply.
  4. The circuit current is reduced.
  5. After the fault is removed and the device cools, its resistance generally returns toward its normal value.
A resettable PTC does not create a perfect open circuit. It continues to allow a small current in its high-resistance state, and its hold/trip behavior depends on temperature and device specifications.

7. Electronic Current Limiting Protection

Electronic current limiting uses sensing and control circuitry to restrict output current during overload or short-circuit conditions.

Typical Components

  • Current-sensing resistor.
  • Transistor or MOSFET pass element.
  • Comparator or control amplifier.
  • Reference voltage source.
  • Control and protection circuitry.

Working

  1. Load current is monitored through a sensing element.
  2. The sensing voltage is compared with a reference threshold.
  3. When the current exceeds the limit, the control circuit responds.
  4. The pass element reduces the output current.
  5. The supply remains protected within its designed operating limits.

8. Short-Circuit Protection Using a Regulator IC

Many linear regulator ICs include internal current limiting and thermal shutdown. These features help protect the regulator during short-circuit and overload conditions.

Protection Sequence

  1. A short circuit occurs at the regulator output.
  2. The output current attempts to increase.
  3. The internal current limiter restricts the current.
  4. Power dissipation may increase inside the regulator.
  5. If the junction temperature becomes excessive, thermal protection may reduce or stop operation.
Internal protection does not guarantee unlimited short-circuit survival. Duration, input voltage, package temperature and manufacturer specifications must all be considered.

9. Foldback Current Protection

Foldback protection is a type of current limiting in which the maximum permitted current decreases as the output voltage falls during a severe overload.

Advantages

  • Reduces pass-transistor power dissipation during a short circuit.
  • Can improve the survival of linear power supplies during faults.
  • Provides a lower current under severe output faults.

Limitation

Some loads require a relatively high starting current. Foldback protection may prevent such loads from starting unless the protection design accounts for their startup requirements.

10. Short-Circuit Protection Techniques

Protection Method Operating Principle Reset Method
Fuse Melts and interrupts the circuit. Replace the fuse after correcting the fault.
Resettable PTC Resistance rises with temperature. Usually resets after cooling.
Electronic current limiting Restricts output current. Depends on circuit design.
Electronic shutdown Turns off the supply or pass element. Automatic or manual, depending on design.
Foldback protection Reduces allowed current as output voltage falls. Usually recovers when the fault is removed.

11. Short-Circuit Protection in Embedded Systems

Embedded electronic products use multiple protection methods to protect microcontrollers, sensors, communication interfaces and external loads.

Practical Examples

  • USB power input protection.
  • Microcontroller board power supply protection.
  • Motor driver output protection.
  • Sensor supply protection.
  • Industrial I/O module protection.
  • Battery-powered embedded equipment.
A microcontroller GPIO pin is not designed to supply large load currents. Use a suitable driver and protection circuit for external loads such as motors, relays and lamps.

12. Practical Troubleshooting

Observation Possible Cause Inspection
Fuse repeatedly blows Persistent short circuit or excessive load Inspect wiring, PCB tracks and connected components.
Output voltage becomes zero Protection shutdown or supply fault Check input voltage and protection status.
PTC becomes hot Excessive current or repeated fault Remove the fault and verify PTC ratings.
Regulator overheats High power dissipation during overload Check input-output voltage difference and load current.
Protection does not operate Incorrect component rating or failed protection circuit Inspect the sensing and disconnect circuitry.

Safe Troubleshooting Procedure

  1. Disconnect the power supply before inspecting the circuit.
  2. Check for visible damage, solder bridges and wiring faults.
  3. Use a multimeter to check resistance between supply rails with power removed.
  4. Inspect the fuse and protection components.
  5. Correct the fault before applying power again.
  6. Use a current-limited bench supply for controlled testing where appropriate.

13. Advantages of Short-Circuit Protection

  • Protects electronic components from fault current.
  • Reduces the risk of overheating.
  • Improves power supply reliability.
  • Helps prevent PCB and wiring damage.
  • Improves equipment safety.
  • Reduces repair and maintenance costs.

14. Limitations

  • Protection devices require correct selection and coordination.
  • Fuses must be replaced after operation.
  • PTC devices may continue allowing a small current during a fault.
  • Electronic protection adds design complexity.
  • Thermal stress may still occur before protection responds.
  • Incorrectly designed protection may cause unwanted shutdowns.

17. Lesson Summary

  • A short circuit creates an unintended low-resistance path.
  • It can cause excessive current and component damage.
  • Fuses interrupt the circuit when their operating conditions are exceeded.
  • Resettable PTC devices increase resistance during excessive heating.
  • Electronic protection can limit current or shut down the output.
  • Foldback protection reduces current during severe overloads.
  • Safe troubleshooting requires disconnecting power and identifying the fault before restarting.
