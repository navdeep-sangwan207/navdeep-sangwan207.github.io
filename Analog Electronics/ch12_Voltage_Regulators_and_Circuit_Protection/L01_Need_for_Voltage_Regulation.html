2. What Is Voltage Regulation?

Voltage regulation is the process of maintaining a nearly constant output voltage despite variations in input supply voltage, load current and operating temperature.

Electronic components require suitable supply voltages to operate correctly. For example, a digital circuit designed for a 5 V supply may malfunction if its supply voltage falls significantly or rises beyond its permitted range.

Regulated Output Voltage ≈ Constant

A voltage regulator senses output conditions and controls the power delivered to the load to maintain the desired voltage within its specified operating limits.

3. Why Is Voltage Regulation Necessary?

Input voltage variation

Changes in AC mains voltage, transformer output or upstream DC supply can change the regulator input.

Load current variation

When connected equipment draws more current, the supply voltage may drop due to internal resistance and circuit limitations.

Temperature variation

Semiconductor parameters and reference voltages can change with temperature.

Ripple and noise

Rectifier and switching circuits may introduce unwanted variations into the DC output.

Component tolerances

Resistors, capacitors, transformers and semiconductor devices have manufacturing tolerances.

Transient disturbances

Sudden changes in load current can cause short-duration voltage deviations.

4. Unregulated and Regulated Power Supply

Unregulated supply

An unregulated supply may contain a transformer, rectifier and filter capacitor. Its output changes with input voltage, load current and ripple.

Regulated supply

A regulated supply adds a regulation stage to control the output voltage. A typical linear supply contains a transformer, rectifier, filter and regulator.

ParameterUnregulated supplyRegulated supply
Output voltageVaries with operating conditionsMaintained within specified limits
Load variationMay cause significant voltage changeCompensated within operating limits
RippleDepends on rectifier and filterAdditional ripple rejection provided
ComplexityGenerally lowerHigher due to regulation circuitry
ApplicationsNon-critical loadsSensitive electronic circuits

5. Line Regulation

Line regulation describes how much the output voltage changes when the input voltage changes, while the load and other operating conditions are held constant.

Line Regulation = ΔVOUT / ΔVIN

It is often expressed in mV/V or as a percentage.

Line Regulation (%) = (ΔVOUT / ΔVIN) × 100

Worked numerical example

A regulator has an output voltage of 5.00 V at an input of 9 V. When the input rises to 12 V, the output rises to 5.02 V.

Change in output voltage = 5.02 − 5.00 = 0.02 V.

Change in input voltage = 12 − 9 = 3 V.

Line Regulation = 0.02 / 3 = 6.67 mV/V

Percentage line regulation, using the initial output as reference:

0.02 / 5.00 × 100 = 0.4%
A smaller output change for a given input change generally indicates better line regulation.

6. Load Regulation

Load regulation describes how much the output voltage changes as load current changes while the input voltage is held constant.

Load Regulation (%) = [(VNL − VFL) / VFL] × 100

Where:

  • VNL = output voltage at no load.
  • VFL = output voltage at full load.

Worked numerical example

A regulated supply gives 5.10 V at no load and 5.00 V at full load.

Load Regulation = [(5.10 − 5.00)/5.00] × 100
Load Regulation = 2%

The output voltage decreases by 0.10 V as the load changes from no load to full load.

Some datasheets use different conventions for load regulation, including mV change over a specified current range. Always follow the manufacturer's stated test conditions and definition.

7. Ripple Voltage and Ripple Rejection

Ripple is the periodic AC component remaining in a DC power supply after rectification and filtering.

For a full-wave rectifier, the ripple frequency is twice the AC supply frequency. For a half-wave rectifier, it is equal to the AC supply frequency.

Ripple Factor = Vr(rms) / VDC

Ripple rejection describes how effectively a regulator reduces input ripple at its output. It is often specified as ripple rejection in dB or by power-supply rejection ratio (PSRR), depending on the device and datasheet.

Ripple Rejection (dB) = 20 log₁₀(Vripple,in / Vripple,out)

This expression compares ripple amplitudes at the same frequency under stated conditions.

Worked example

Input ripple = 1 V peak-to-peak and output ripple = 10 mV peak-to-peak.

Ripple Rejection = 20 log₁₀(1/0.01) = 40 dB

9. Types of Voltage Regulators

Zener Shunt Regulator

Uses a reverse-biased Zener diode to maintain an approximately constant voltage across a load.

Series-Pass Transistor Regulator

Uses a transistor as a controlled series element to regulate output voltage.

Linear Regulator IC

Uses an internal reference, error amplifier and pass element to control output voltage.

Switching Regulator

Uses high-frequency switching and energy-storage components for efficient DC conversion.

Linear versus switching regulators

FeatureLinear regulatorSwitching regulator
Operating principleControls voltage drop across a pass elementControls energy transfer through switching
EfficiencyDepends strongly on input/output voltage ratioOften higher, depending on design and operating point
NoiseGenerally low output switching noiseSwitching ripple and EMI require attention
HeatCan dissipate substantial powerOften lower conversion loss, but switching losses remain
ComponentsRelatively simpleRequires inductor, diode or synchronous switch and control circuit
ApplicationsLow-noise and modest power suppliesEfficient step-down and step-up conversion

12. Practical Lab – Testing a Regulated DC Supply

Objective: Measure line regulation, load regulation and output ripple of a regulated DC supply.

Required equipment

  • Low-voltage isolated DC supply or suitable laboratory power supply.
  • Regulator circuit or commercial regulator evaluation board.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope with suitable probe.
  • Resistive load bank or electronic load.
  • Connecting wires and breadboard or PCB.

Part A – Line regulation test

  1. Connect the regulator to a suitable adjustable DC input supply.
  2. Keep the load current constant.
  3. Measure output voltage at the minimum permitted input voltage.
  4. Increase the input voltage in controlled steps within the regulator's ratings.
  5. Record the corresponding output voltage.
  6. Calculate line regulation using the measured values.

Part B – Load regulation test

  1. Set a fixed input voltage within the regulator's specified range.
  2. Measure output voltage with no load, within the datasheet's permitted conditions.
  3. Connect progressively higher load currents without exceeding ratings.
  4. Record output voltage at each load condition.
  5. Calculate load regulation between the specified no-load and full-load points.

Part C – Ripple measurement

  1. Connect the oscilloscope across the regulated output using correct grounding.
  2. Use AC coupling when appropriate to examine small ripple on the DC level.
  3. Measure peak-to-peak ripple under stated load conditions.
  4. Compare ripple before and after the regulator if both test points are safely accessible.

Observation table

TestMeasurementResult
Line regulationInput and output voltage at two input settingsCalculate mV/V
Load regulationNo-load and full-load output voltageCalculate percentage
Output ripplePeak-to-peak output rippleRecord mVpp
TemperatureRegulator case temperature after stabilizationRecord °C
Do not use an exposed mains circuit for this lab. Use an isolated, current-limited low-voltage source and observe the regulator's input, output, current and thermal ratings.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is voltage regulation?

Maintaining a nearly constant output voltage despite changes in input voltage, load current and operating conditions.

2. Why is voltage regulation required?

Electronic circuits require supply voltages within specified operating limits for reliable operation.

3. What is line regulation?

It describes output voltage change resulting from input voltage variation at a fixed load.

4. What is load regulation?

It describes output voltage change as load current changes at a fixed input voltage.

5. What is ripple voltage?

The residual periodic AC component present in a DC supply.

6. What is ripple rejection?

The ability of a regulator to attenuate input ripple at its output.

7. What is the difference between a regulated and unregulated supply?

A regulated supply controls its output voltage within specified limits; an unregulated supply does not provide that control.

8. What causes output voltage to fall under heavy load?

Internal resistance, wiring drop, current limitation, thermal effects and insufficient input headroom can contribute.

9. What is a linear regulator?

A regulator that controls the voltage drop across a series pass element to maintain its output.

10. What is a switching regulator?

A regulator that controls energy transfer using a switching element and energy-storage components.

11. Why can a linear regulator become hot?

It dissipates approximately (VIN − VOUT) × IOUT as pass-element power, apart from other small losses.

12. What is dropout voltage?

The minimum input-to-output voltage difference required for a regulator to maintain its specified regulation.

13. Which regulator is usually more efficient for a large voltage reduction?

A properly designed switching regulator is generally more efficient than a linear regulator for a large voltage reduction.

14. How is line regulation measured?

Change the input voltage while keeping the load fixed and measure the output voltage change.

15. How is load regulation measured?

Change the load current while keeping the input voltage fixed and measure the output voltage change.

16. Why is an oscilloscope used in regulator testing?

It reveals ripple, noise, transient response and oscillation that may not be visible on a multimeter.

17. What is PSRR?

Power-supply rejection ratio describes how effectively a circuit rejects supply disturbances; regulator datasheets may specify ripple rejection or PSRR under particular conditions.

18. What happens if the regulator input falls below its required headroom?

The regulator may enter dropout and its output can fall below the specified regulated value.

15. Numerical Practice

  1. A regulator output changes from 5.00 V to 5.03 V when input voltage changes from 8 V to 14 V. Calculate line regulation in mV/V.
  2. A supply produces 12.2 V at no load and 12.0 V at full load. Calculate percentage load regulation using full-load voltage as reference.
  3. A regulator receives 15 V and produces 5 V at 0.5 A. Calculate approximate pass-element power dissipation.
  4. Input ripple is 500 mV peak-to-peak and output ripple is 5 mV peak-to-peak. Calculate ripple rejection in dB.
  5. Explain why a regulator may fail to maintain output voltage when the input supply decreases.
  6. Compare a linear regulator and a buck converter for converting 12 V to 5 V at 1 A.
Show numerical answers
  1. ΔVOUT = 0.03 V; ΔVIN = 6 V. Line regulation = 5 mV/V.
  2. [(12.2 − 12.0)/12.0] × 100 = 1.67%.
  3. P = (15 − 5) × 0.5 = 5 W, approximately, excluding other losses.
  4. 20 log₁₀(500/5) = 40 dB.
  5. The regulator may enter dropout or violate its minimum input voltage requirement.
  6. A buck converter is generally more efficient at this conversion and current, while a linear regulator is simpler and usually has lower switching noise.

17. Lecture Summary

  • Voltage regulation maintains a stable output voltage within specified operating limits.
  • Input variations, load changes, temperature and ripple affect supply performance.
  • Line regulation measures output response to input changes.
  • Load regulation measures output response to load-current changes.
  • Ripple rejection describes attenuation of input ripple.
  • Linear and switching regulators use different regulation mechanisms and have different trade-offs.
  • Practical regulator testing includes voltage, current, ripple and thermal measurements.
