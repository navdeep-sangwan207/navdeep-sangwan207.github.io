4. Fuse Protection

A fuse is a protective device designed to interrupt a circuit when current and time conditions cause its fusible element to operate.

Fuse Selection

  • Rated current.
  • Rated voltage.
  • Breaking capacity.
  • Time-current characteristic.
  • Ambient temperature.
  • Inrush current.
  • DC versus AC interruption requirements.
A fuse must be selected based on fault current, source capability, wiring, component limits, time-current behavior and safety requirements. A fuse rating alone does not guarantee protection of every semiconductor.

Fuse and Load Current

A fuse rating is not simply a precise current threshold. Fuse operation depends on time, temperature and its characteristic curve. The normal load current, startup current and fault conditions must all be considered.

5. Resettable PTC Protection

A resettable PTC (positive temperature coefficient) device is commonly used for protection against sustained overloads.

Operating Principle

  1. Under normal operating conditions, the PTC has relatively low resistance.
  2. Excessive current causes heating.
  3. As temperature rises, its resistance increases significantly.
  4. Current is reduced to a limited level.
  5. After the fault is removed and the device cools, resistance decreases.
A PTC is not necessarily a complete disconnect. During a fault it may still conduct current. Verify hold current, trip behavior, fault voltage, residual current and recovery conditions.

6. Current Limiting Using a Sense Resistor

Current can be detected by measuring the voltage across a low-value resistor.

VSENSE = I × RSENSE

If a protection circuit triggers when the sense voltage reaches a threshold:

ILIMIT ≈ VTH / RSENSE

Worked Example

A current sensing circuit has a threshold voltage of 100 mV and a sensing resistor of 0.1 Ω.

ILIMIT = 0.1 / 0.1

ILIMIT = 1 A

At 1 A, the sensing resistor dissipates:

P = I²R

P = 1² × 0.1 = 0.1 W

Choose the resistor rating with appropriate margin and verify pulse, thermal and tolerance conditions.

8. TVS Diode Protection

A transient voltage suppression diode is designed to limit transient voltage excursions. It is often used to protect electronic interfaces, power inputs and signal lines.

TVS Parameters

  • Reverse standoff voltage (VRWM).
  • Breakdown voltage (VBR).
  • Clamping voltage (VC) at specified current.
  • Peak pulse power and current.
  • Response characteristics.
  • Unidirectional or bidirectional type.
  • Capacitance for signal-line protection.

TVS Selection

Choose the standoff voltage so the TVS does not conduct significantly during normal operating conditions, while ensuring its clamping behavior is suitable for the protected circuit.

The TVS clamping voltage can be higher than the nominal breakdown voltage under surge current. The protected circuit must withstand the actual clamping voltage, including wiring and parasitic effects. The source impedance and surge energy must also be considered.

Applications

  • DC supply input protection.
  • Automotive low-voltage electronics.
  • Communication interfaces.
  • USB and data interfaces.
  • Electrostatic discharge protection.
  • Transient protection in embedded systems.

9. Crowbar Overvoltage Protection

A crowbar protection circuit typically uses a triggering device, often an SCR, to create a low-impedance path when the monitored voltage exceeds a threshold. The resulting fault current may cause an upstream fuse or current-limited supply to interrupt the circuit.

Operation

  1. Monitor the supply voltage.
  2. When the voltage exceeds the trigger threshold, the triggering circuit activates the SCR.
  3. The SCR creates a low-impedance path across the supply.
  4. The source protection device or current-limited source responds to the resulting current.
  5. The source is interrupted or the crowbar current is limited by the source.

Crowbar Design Considerations

  • Trigger voltage accuracy.
  • SCR current and voltage ratings.
  • Fault current and energy.
  • Fuse or source current-limit coordination.
  • SCR latching and holding current.
  • Supply impedance.
  • Reset behavior.
  • PCB layout and fault handling.
A crowbar is intentionally capable of creating a high fault current. The source, SCR, wiring and upstream protection must be coordinated. Never implement a crowbar without evaluating its fault energy and interrupting conditions.

10. Overvoltage Detection Using a Comparator

A comparator can compare a monitored supply against a reference threshold and generate a signal to shut down a load or regulator.

Voltage Divider

For a divider consisting of R1 from the monitored input to the sense node and R2 from the sense node to ground:

VSENSE = VIN × R2/(R1 + R2)

To detect when VIN reaches a specified threshold, set the divider voltage equal to the comparator reference voltage.

VTH = VREF × (R1 + R2)/R2

Worked Example

  • Reference voltage: 2.5 V
  • R2: 10 kΩ
  • Desired threshold: 10 V
10 = 2.5 × (R1 + 10k)/10k

4 = (R1 + 10k)/10k

R1 = 30 kΩ

For an ideal divider, 30 kΩ and 10 kΩ establish a 2.5 V sense voltage when the monitored input is 10 V.

In practical design, account for reference accuracy, comparator offset, resistor tolerance, input bias, hysteresis, transient response, divider power and shutdown behavior.

11. Reverse-Polarity Protection

Technique 1: Series Diode

A diode placed in series with the supply prevents reverse polarity from reaching the circuit. Its forward voltage drop and power dissipation may be undesirable.

PDIODE = VF × I

Technique 2: MOSFET Protection

A MOSFET-based arrangement can reduce the forward voltage loss compared with a series diode. The exact topology, MOSFET polarity, body diode, gate voltage and operating conditions must be carefully designed.

MethodAdvantagesLimitations
Series diodeSimple and easy to implementForward voltage drop and heat
MOSFETPotentially lower conduction lossMore design and polarity considerations
Bridge rectifierAccepts either input polarityTwo diode drops in the conducting path

14. Practical Laboratory

Objective

Study basic overcurrent and overvoltage protection concepts using low-voltage DC circuits.

Required Equipment

  • Current-limited DC bench supply.
  • Multimeter.
  • Resistors.
  • LED and suitable resistor.
  • Fuse holder and suitable fuse.
  • Comparator or protection module.
  • TVS diode selected for the experimental supply.
  • Oscilloscope.
  • Connecting wires.

Experiment A: Fuse

  1. Build a low-voltage circuit with a fuse in series with the load.
  2. Measure the normal operating current.
  3. Inspect the fuse rating and datasheet.
  4. Discuss how the fuse protects the circuit.
  5. Do not create an uncontrolled short circuit or force the fuse to blow.

Experiment B: Current Sensing

  1. Connect a suitable low-value current sensing resistor.
  2. Measure the voltage across the resistor.
  3. Calculate the current using Ohm's law.
  4. Compare the calculated current with the multimeter reading.
  5. Discuss how a comparator can detect an overload.

Experiment C: Overvoltage Detection

  1. Build a comparator-based voltage detection circuit.
  2. Set a low-voltage threshold using a divider and reference.
  3. Measure the sense voltage.
  4. Use a suitable LED or safe low-voltage output to indicate the threshold crossing.
  5. Increase input voltage gradually within component ratings.
  6. Observe the detection and shutdown signal.

Experiment D: TVS Protection

  1. Study the TVS datasheet.
  2. Identify the standoff voltage and clamping parameters.
  3. Inspect the appropriate connection and polarity.
  4. Discuss how it limits transient voltage.
  5. Do not apply uncontrolled surges to test the TVS.

16. Placement Interview Questions

  1. What is overcurrent protection?
    A technique that limits or interrupts current when it exceeds an acceptable operating level.
  2. What is overvoltage protection?
    A technique that prevents or limits voltage exceeding the permitted range.
  3. What is a fuse?
    A protective device that interrupts current when its fusible element operates under specified conditions.
  4. What is a resettable PTC?
    A temperature-dependent protective device that increases resistance substantially during overheating.
  5. What is a TVS diode?
    A transient voltage suppression semiconductor designed to limit transient voltage excursions.
  6. What is a crowbar circuit?
    An overvoltage protection circuit that creates a low-impedance path, often using an SCR, to trigger source protection.
  7. Why is an SCR used in crowbar circuits?
    It can latch into conduction and sustain the protection path until current falls below its holding condition.
  8. What is current sensing?
    Measuring current directly or indirectly, often through a resistor that generates a proportional voltage.
  9. How is current calculated from a sense resistor?
    I = Vsense/Rsense.
  10. What is a current limit?
    A mechanism that restricts output current to a defined range or limit.
  11. What is reverse-polarity protection?
    A circuit that prevents damage when supply polarity is incorrectly applied.
  12. What is the advantage of MOSFET reverse protection?
    It can reduce conduction loss compared with a series diode, depending on the design.
  13. What is the difference between a fuse and a PTC?
    A fuse permanently interrupts the current path when it operates; a PTC generally recovers after cooling and fault removal.
  14. What is clamping voltage?
    The voltage a protection device limits to under specified conditions, such as a defined surge current.
  15. What is TVS standoff voltage?
    The specified maximum reverse voltage at which the TVS is intended to remain in its non-significant-conduction operating region.
  16. What is TVS breakdown voltage?
    The specified voltage associated with the TVS breakdown region under defined test conditions.
  17. Why can TVS clamping voltage be higher than breakdown voltage?
    Clamping voltage depends on current through the device and its dynamic characteristics.
  18. Why is a comparator used in OVP?
    It compares a monitored voltage with a reference and provides a signal to control protection action.
  19. Why is hysteresis useful in OVP?
    It helps prevent repeated switching near the threshold when the monitored voltage fluctuates.
  20. Why must a crowbar be coordinated with a fuse?
    The crowbar fault current must be handled safely by the source and protection system.
  21. What is ESD protection?
    Protection against electrostatic discharge events that may damage electronic components.
  22. Why is protection placement important?
    Placement affects current paths, parasitic inductance, response and the voltage seen by the protected circuit.
  23. Why is a protection circuit not always sufficient?
    The fault energy, response time, clamping voltage and device ratings may still permit damage.
  24. What is the role of a current-limited power supply?
    It restricts the current available during normal operation or faults, reducing the risk of excessive fault energy.
  25. How do you debug a protection circuit?
    Measure supply, threshold, sense signals, current, output state and temperature; compare them with the datasheet and expected fault behavior.

17. Numerical Practice

Problem 1: Current Sensing

A sense resistor of 0.05 Ω has a voltage of 50 mV across it. Calculate the current.

I = 0.05/0.05 = 1 A

Problem 2: Sense Resistor Power

A 0.1 Ω resistor carries 2 A.

P = I²R

P = 2² × 0.1 = 0.4 W

Problem 3: OVP Divider

A 12 V input is monitored using R1 = 30 kΩ and R2 = 10 kΩ.

VSENSE = 12 × 10/(30+10)

VSENSE = 3 V

Problem 4: Comparator Threshold

A 2 V reference is used with R2 = 10 kΩ. The desired threshold is 8 V.

8 = 2 × (R1+10k)/10k

R1 = 30 kΩ

Problem 5: Diode Loss

A series protection diode has a forward voltage of 0.7 V and carries 0.5 A.

P = 0.7 × 0.5 = 0.35 W

Problem 6: TVS Design Concept

A protected load operates up to 12 V normally. Describe the parameters required to select a TVS diode.

Expected answer: Normal voltage tolerance, standoff voltage, breakdown voltage, clamping voltage at the expected surge current, pulse power, surge energy, source impedance, protected circuit withstand voltage, capacitance, placement and temperature.

  • ☐ Protected load withstand voltage verified.

  • ☐ Hysteresis and response behavior evaluated.

  • ☐ Reverse-polarity protection verified.
  • ☐ PCB layout and placement reviewed.
  • ☐ Thermal conditions evaluated.
  • ☐ Fault conditions tested using safe, controlled methods.
  • ☐ Manufacturer datasheets reviewed.

20. Summary

  • Overcurrent and overvoltage can damage electronic circuits.
  • Fuses interrupt excessive current.
  • PTCs provide resettable overload protection.
  • Sense resistors enable current detection.
  • TVS diodes suppress transient voltage excursions.
  • Crowbar circuits trigger source protection under overvoltage conditions.
  • Comparator circuits can monitor and disconnect excessive voltage.
  • MOSFETs and diodes can provide reverse-polarity protection.
  • Protection devices must be selected using datasheets and fault-energy calculations.
  • Testing and protection coordination are essential for practical reliability.
Placement focus: Explain how you would protect a DC supply against short circuits, overloads, overvoltage, transient surges and reverse polarity. Be able to calculate current-sense voltage, sense resistor power, divider thresholds and protection-device selection parameters.
