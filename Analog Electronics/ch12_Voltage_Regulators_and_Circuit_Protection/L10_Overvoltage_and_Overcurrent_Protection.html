Lesson 10: Overvoltage and Overcurrent Protection

Study protection techniques used to safeguard electronic circuits against excessive supply voltage and current conditions.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand overvoltage and overcurrent conditions.
  • Identify common causes of voltage and current faults.
  • Understand Zener diode and TVS-based protection.
  • Study crowbar protection circuits.
  • Understand fuse, transistor and electronic current protection.
  • Learn comparator-based protection circuits.
  • Understand practical protection in power supplies and embedded systems.

2. Introduction to Overvoltage Protection

Overvoltage is a condition in which the voltage supplied to a circuit exceeds its specified safe operating limit.

Common Causes

  • Failure of a voltage regulator.
  • Incorrect power supply connection.
  • Sudden voltage spikes or transients.
  • Lightning-induced surges.
  • Switching of inductive loads.
  • Faulty power supply feedback circuitry.

Effects of Overvoltage

  • Damage to semiconductor junctions.
  • Breakdown of insulation.
  • Excessive current through electronic components.
  • Overheating and component failure.
  • Damage to microcontrollers and sensitive ICs.

3. Zener Diode Overvoltage Protection

A Zener diode can be connected in reverse bias across a protected supply rail. When the voltage reaches its breakdown region, the diode conducts and helps clamp the voltage.

Conceptual Zener clamp arrangement. A suitable series impedance and correct Zener rating are required to limit current safely.

Working Principle

  1. Under normal conditions, the Zener diode remains below its breakdown voltage.
  2. When the supply voltage rises, the Zener enters breakdown.
  3. It conducts more current and clamps the voltage near its breakdown region.
  4. The series resistor or source impedance limits the Zener current.
Vclamp ≈ VZ
A Zener clamp must be designed for the maximum surge energy, current and power. It is not safe to connect a Zener diode directly across an unrestricted supply.

4. TVS Diode Protection

A Transient Voltage Suppressor (TVS) diode is designed to protect circuits against short-duration voltage transients.

Working Principle

  1. During normal operation, the TVS diode has high impedance.
  2. When a transient exceeds its breakdown region, it conducts strongly.
  3. It diverts surge current away from the protected circuit.
  4. The voltage across the protected line is limited to a specified clamping level under the stated test conditions.

Types of TVS Diodes

  • Unidirectional TVS diode.
  • Bidirectional TVS diode.
Type Typical Application
Unidirectional TVS DC power lines and polarity-sensitive signal lines.
Bidirectional TVS AC lines and signals that swing in both polarities.
Select TVS devices using working standoff voltage, breakdown voltage, clamping voltage, peak pulse current and pulse power ratings. The clamping voltage must be compatible with the protected circuit's absolute maximum ratings.

5. Crowbar Overvoltage Protection

A crowbar circuit protects a load by creating a low-resistance path across the supply when an overvoltage condition is detected. This usually causes an upstream fuse or current-limited source to disconnect or limit the supply.

Common Components

  • SCR (Silicon Controlled Rectifier).
  • Zener diode or reference circuit.
  • Gate trigger network.
  • Fuse or current-limited power source.

Protected Load

An SCR crowbar is connected across the protected supply rail and return, with a trigger circuit that detects overvoltage.

Working Principle

  1. The supply operates normally below the overvoltage threshold.
  2. The sensing circuit detects excessive voltage.
  3. The SCR gate receives a trigger signal.
  4. The SCR turns on and creates a low-impedance path across the supply.
  5. The resulting fault current causes the fuse to open or the upstream protection to act.
Crowbar protection intentionally creates a high-current fault path. It must be coordinated with a correctly rated fuse or current-limited source. Without suitable upstream protection, the SCR and supply can be damaged.

6. Overcurrent Protection

Overcurrent occurs when the current flowing through a circuit exceeds its permitted operating limit.

Common Causes

  • Short circuits.
  • Excessive load current.
  • Faulty electronic components.
  • Motor or actuator overload.
  • Incorrect circuit design.

Effects

  • Excessive heating.
  • Damage to semiconductor devices.
  • PCB track damage.
  • Power supply failure.
  • Reduced component lifetime.

7. Fuse-Based Overcurrent Protection

A fuse is connected in series with the load. When excessive current flows for a sufficient duration, its element melts and interrupts the circuit.

P = I²R

Fuse selection must account for rated current, rated voltage, breaking capacity, time-current behavior and the circuit's normal startup current.

A fuse protects wiring and equipment only when its ratings and interrupting capacity are correctly matched to the available fault current and circuit requirements.

8. Current-Sensing Resistor Protection

A low-value resistor can be connected in series with a load to measure current.

Vsense = Iload × Rsense

A comparator or control circuit monitors this voltage. When it exceeds a reference threshold, the circuit can limit current, switch off a MOSFET or activate an alarm.

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Sense resistor = 0.1 Ω
  • Load current = 2 A
Vsense = 2 × 0.1

Vsense = 0.2 V

The sensing circuit receives a 0.2 V signal that can be compared against a selected current threshold.

9. Comparator-Based Protection

A comparator compares a monitored voltage with a reference voltage. It changes its output state when the monitored voltage crosses the selected threshold.

Overvoltage Detection

  1. A resistor divider samples the supply voltage.
  2. The comparator compares the sampled voltage with a reference.
  3. When the supply exceeds the threshold, the comparator changes state.
  4. The control circuit activates an alarm, relay or shutdown switch.

Overcurrent Detection

  1. A shunt resistor generates a voltage proportional to load current.
  2. The comparator monitors the sense voltage.
  3. When the current threshold is exceeded, its output changes state.
  4. The power switch can be disabled or the current can be limited.
Comparator threshold accuracy depends on reference accuracy, input offset, resistor tolerances, noise and any hysteresis added to prevent rapid switching.

11. Protection in Embedded Systems

Embedded systems often contain sensitive microcontrollers, communication interfaces and external loads. Protection circuits help prevent damage caused by supply faults.

  • TVS protection on communication interfaces.
  • Overvoltage protection on DC input connectors.
  • Fuse or resettable PTC protection on supply rails.
  • Current sensing in motor driver circuits.
  • Comparator-based voltage monitoring.
  • Power-good and undervoltage monitoring circuits.
For interfaces such as UART, I2C, SPI and CAN, select protection components suitable for the interface voltage, signal speed, capacitance and expected transient environment.

13. Advantages

  • Protects circuits against abnormal voltage and current.
  • Reduces the risk of semiconductor damage.
  • Improves power supply reliability.
  • Can provide automatic shutdown or recovery.
  • Supports safer operation of embedded and industrial equipment.
  • Can be implemented using discrete components or ICs.

14. Limitations

  • Protection components require careful rating selection.
  • TVS and Zener devices have finite surge-energy capability.
  • Crowbar circuits require coordinated upstream protection.
  • Comparator circuits can produce false trips if noise is not controlled.
  • Protection circuits add component cost and design complexity.
  • Incorrect threshold selection can cause nuisance shutdowns or inadequate protection.

17. Lesson Summary

  • Overvoltage occurs when the supply exceeds the safe voltage limit.
  • Overcurrent occurs when current exceeds the permitted operating limit.
  • Zener and TVS diodes can clamp excessive voltage.
  • Crowbar circuits use an SCR to trigger supply disconnection.
  • Fuses and electronic current limiters provide overcurrent protection.
  • Comparators can detect abnormal voltage and current conditions.
  • Correct component ratings and protection coordination are essential.
