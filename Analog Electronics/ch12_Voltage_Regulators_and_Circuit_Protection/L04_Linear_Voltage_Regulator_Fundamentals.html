Lesson 04: Series and Shunt Regulators

Understand the construction, working principles, circuit operation and differences between series and shunt voltage regulators.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the basic concept of voltage regulators.
  • Explain the operation of a series voltage regulator.
  • Explain the operation of a shunt voltage regulator.
  • Understand the role of pass and control elements.
  • Compare series and shunt regulators.
  • Identify practical applications and limitations.

2. Introduction

A voltage regulator is an electronic circuit that maintains a nearly constant DC output voltage despite variations in input voltage, load current and operating conditions.

Based on the connection of the regulating element with respect to the load, linear regulators are commonly classified into:

  • Series Voltage Regulator
  • Shunt Voltage Regulator

Both types can use a control circuit and a reference voltage to maintain the desired output voltage.

3. Series Voltage Regulator

In a series voltage regulator, the controlling element is connected in series between the unregulated DC input supply and the load.

The control element adjusts its effective resistance or conduction to control the voltage delivered to the load.

A feedback circuit senses the output voltage and controls the series pass element.

Simplified conceptual representation of a series regulator.

3.3 Working Principle

  1. The unregulated DC supply is applied to the series pass element.
  2. The output voltage is continuously sensed by a feedback circuit.
  3. The feedback circuit compares the sensed voltage with a reference voltage.
  4. An error signal controls the conduction of the pass element.
  5. The output voltage is corrected toward its desired value.

3.4 When Input Voltage Increases

If the input voltage increases, the feedback circuit adjusts the pass element to increase the voltage dropped across it. This helps maintain a nearly constant output voltage.

3.5 When Load Current Increases

When the load draws more current, the output voltage tends to decrease. The feedback circuit responds by increasing the conduction of the pass element, helping restore the output voltage.

4. Shunt Voltage Regulator

In a shunt voltage regulator, the regulating element is connected in parallel with the load.

A series resistor is connected between the input supply and the output node. The shunt element controls the voltage by varying the current flowing through its parallel branch.

DC Input
→
Series Resistor
→
Output Node

The shunt control element and load are connected in parallel across the output.

4.2 Basic Circuit Concept

Simplified conceptual representation of a shunt regulator.

4.3 Working Principle

  1. The input supply provides current through the series resistor.
  2. The load and shunt regulator share the output node.
  3. The shunt element adjusts its current to control output voltage.
  4. When input voltage increases, shunt current increases to absorb additional current.
  5. When load current increases, shunt current decreases to provide more current to the load.
A shunt regulator requires current to flow through its series resistor and shunt branch even when the load current is small. This can cause continuous power dissipation.

5. Series Regulator vs Shunt Regulator

Parameter Series Regulator Shunt Regulator
Regulating Element Connected in series with load Connected in parallel with load
Control Method Controls current through the load path Controls current through the shunt path
Current Handling Pass element carries load current Shunt element carries diverted current
Efficiency Generally higher for suitable load conditions Generally lower due to continuous shunt current
Power Dissipation In series pass element In series resistor and shunt element
Typical Use Linear regulated power supplies Reference and low-current regulation circuits

6. Advantages of Series Regulators

  • Good voltage regulation.
  • Suitable for higher load currents than simple shunt regulators.
  • Generally better efficiency than a comparable shunt arrangement.
  • Widely used in linear power supply circuits.
  • Can provide good load regulation with feedback control.

7. Limitations of Series Regulators

  • Pass transistor dissipates power as heat.
  • Efficiency decreases when the input-output voltage difference is large.
  • Requires thermal management at high load currents.
  • Output voltage cannot exceed the available input voltage in an ordinary linear regulator.
  • Requires adequate input-output headroom.

8. Advantages of Shunt Regulators

  • Simple circuit arrangement.
  • Useful for low-current voltage regulation.
  • Can provide a simple reference or voltage clamp.
  • Can respond quickly to certain output disturbances.
  • Basic circuits are easy to understand and implement.

9. Limitations of Shunt Regulators

  • Continuous current consumption.
  • Power is dissipated even at low load current.
  • Less suitable for high-load-current applications.
  • Series resistor and shunt element must be rated for worst-case conditions.
  • Efficiency can be poor when the load is light.

10. Applications

Series Regulator Applications

  • Linear regulated DC power supplies.
  • Microcontroller power supply circuits.
  • Analog electronic equipment.
  • Industrial control systems.
  • Low-noise power supply sections.

Shunt Regulator Applications

  • Zener diode voltage regulators.
  • Voltage reference circuits.
  • Low-current electronic circuits.
  • Overvoltage clamping circuits.
  • Simple power supply protection circuits.

13. Lesson Summary

  • A series regulator has its controlling element in series with the load.
  • A shunt regulator has its controlling element in parallel with the load.
  • A series regulator controls the voltage drop across its pass element.
  • A shunt regulator controls the current diverted through its shunt branch.
  • Series regulators are generally preferred for higher load-current applications.
  • Shunt regulators are useful for simple, low-current regulation and voltage-reference circuits.
  • Both regulator types must be designed within their voltage, current and thermal ratings.
