2. What is Voltage Regulation?

Voltage regulation is the ability of a power supply or voltage regulator to maintain a nearly constant output voltage despite variations in input voltage, load current and operating conditions.

Electronic circuits require a suitable and stable DC voltage to operate correctly. However, the input supply voltage and load current may change during operation.

A voltage regulator compensates for these variations and maintains the output voltage close to its specified value.

Important: A voltage regulator does not necessarily produce a perfectly constant voltage. It maintains the output within specified limits under defined operating conditions.

Example

Suppose an electronic circuit requires a regulated 5 V DC supply.

If the input voltage changes or the circuit draws different amounts of current, the regulator attempts to maintain the output close to 5 V.

Required output voltage: 5 V DC
Regulator output: Approximately 5 V DC
Purpose: Maintain a stable supply for the connected electronic circuit.

3. Why is Voltage Regulation Required?

In practical power supplies, the output voltage can change due to several factors.

1. Input Voltage Variation

The AC mains voltage may fluctuate. After rectification and filtering, these variations can affect the DC voltage.

2. Load Current Variation

When the connected circuit draws more current, the unregulated output voltage may decrease. When the load current decreases, the output voltage may increase.

3. Temperature Variation

Temperature changes can affect the characteristics of semiconductor devices and electronic components.

4. Component Variations

Resistor tolerances, capacitor characteristics and semiconductor parameters can influence power supply performance.

5. Protection of Electronic Components

Many integrated circuits and microcontrollers require a supply voltage within a specified operating range. Excessive voltage variation can cause malfunction or component damage.

Practical example: A microcontroller designed for a 3.3 V supply may malfunction if its supply voltage falls below its operating range or exceeds its maximum permitted voltage.

4. What is an Unregulated Power Supply?

An unregulated power supply converts an AC input into DC output but does not actively maintain a constant output voltage.

Its output voltage can change with variations in input voltage and load current.

Basic Stages

  1. Transformer: Changes the AC voltage level when required.
  2. Rectifier: Converts AC into pulsating DC.
  3. Filter: Reduces the ripple present in rectified DC.
  4. Load: Receives the filtered DC voltage.

Limitations

  • Output voltage varies with input voltage.
  • Output voltage changes with load current.
  • Ripple may remain in the DC output.
  • Not suitable for many voltage-sensitive electronic circuits without additional regulation.

5. What is a Regulated Power Supply?

A regulated power supply is a power supply that uses a voltage regulator to maintain a nearly constant DC output voltage.

The regulator is generally connected after the rectifier and filter stages.

6. Working Principle of a Voltage Regulator

A voltage regulator controls its output by responding to changes in the input supply voltage or load conditions.

Many regulators use a reference voltage, an error amplifier and a control element.

Basic Feedback Regulator

→

→
Output Voltage
↓

←
Reference and Error Amplifier

The feedback network senses the output and helps the control circuit correct voltage variations.

Step-by-Step Operation

  1. Reference generation: A stable reference voltage is generated inside the regulator.
  2. Output sensing: A feedback network monitors the output voltage.
  3. Error detection: The regulator compares the sensed voltage with the reference voltage.
  4. Correction: The control element adjusts its operation to reduce the output voltage error.
  5. Stable output: The output is maintained close to the required regulated value.
Not every voltage regulator uses the same internal architecture. Simple Zener regulators, linear IC regulators and switching regulators use different regulation techniques.

7. Types of Voltage Regulators

Voltage regulators can be classified into different categories according to their operating principles.

1. Linear Voltage Regulators

Linear regulators control the voltage drop across a series pass element to maintain the required output voltage.

Examples:

  • 78XX positive voltage regulator series.
  • 79XX negative voltage regulator series.
  • LM317 adjustable voltage regulator.
  • Transistor-based series regulators.

2. Switching Voltage Regulators

Switching regulators use high-frequency switching devices and energy-storage components such as inductors and capacitors to control the output voltage.

Common types include:

  • Buck Converter: Steps down DC voltage.
  • Boost Converter: Steps up DC voltage.
  • Buck-Boost Converter: Can step up or step down DC voltage, depending on topology.

3. Classification by Output Voltage

  • Fixed positive voltage regulators.
  • Fixed negative voltage regulators.
  • Adjustable voltage regulators.

8. Important Characteristics of Voltage Regulators

1. Output Voltage

The DC voltage delivered by the regulator to the load. It may be fixed or adjustable.

2. Input Voltage Range

The minimum and maximum input voltage within which the regulator can operate according to its specifications.

3. Output Current

The maximum load current that the regulator can safely supply under specified operating conditions.

4. Line Regulation

It indicates how much the output voltage changes when the input voltage changes while the load conditions remain constant.

5. Load Regulation

It indicates how much the output voltage changes when the load current changes while the input voltage remains constant.

6. Ripple Rejection

It describes the regulator's ability to reduce unwanted AC ripple appearing at its input.

7. Efficiency

It represents the ratio of output power to input power, expressed as a percentage.

Efficiency:
η = (Output Power / Input Power) × 100%

8. Dropout Voltage

Dropout voltage is the minimum difference between input and output voltage required for a regulator to maintain its specified regulation.

A regulator may stop maintaining the specified output when its input voltage falls below the required operating level.

9. Linear Regulator vs. Switching Regulator

Parameter Linear Regulator Switching Regulator
Operating principle Controls voltage drop across a pass element. Uses high-frequency switching.
Efficiency Generally lower when input-output voltage difference is large. Generally higher in properly designed circuits.
Noise Generally low output noise. Switching ripple and EMI require attention.
Circuit complexity Usually simpler. Usually more complex.
Heat dissipation Can be significant. Generally lower for comparable conversion tasks.
Examples 7805, 7812, LM317 Buck and Boost converters

When to Use a Linear Regulator?

  • Low-noise power supply requirements.
  • Small voltage difference between input and output.
  • Low or moderate power applications.
  • Simple circuit design.

When to Use a Switching Regulator?

  • High-efficiency power conversion.
  • Large input-output voltage differences.
  • Battery-powered electronic devices.
  • Higher-power DC-DC conversion.

10. Practical Applications of Voltage Regulators

  • Microcontroller Power Supplies: Providing stable 5 V or 3.3 V supplies.
  • Embedded Systems: Supplying regulated voltage to microcontrollers, sensors and communication modules.
  • Computer Power Supplies: Providing regulated DC voltage rails.
  • Battery-Powered Devices: Maintaining suitable supply voltage as battery voltage changes.
  • Industrial Control Systems: Supplying stable DC voltage to control circuits.
  • Automotive Electronics: Regulating voltage for electronic modules.
  • Instrumentation: Providing stable supplies for measurement circuits.
  • Communication Equipment: Supplying suitable DC voltage to electronic communication circuits.

11. Solved Numerical Example

Example: Power Dissipation in a Linear Regulator

A linear regulator receives an input voltage of 12 V and provides an output voltage of 5 V. The load current is 200 mA. Calculate the approximate power dissipated by the regulator.

Given:

  • Input voltage, Vin = 12 V
  • Output voltage, Vout = 5 V
  • Load current, Iout = 200 mA = 0.2 A
Power dissipated:

P = (Vin - Vout) × Iout

P = (12 - 5) × 0.2

P = 7 × 0.2

P = 1.4 W

Therefore, the regulator dissipates approximately 1.4 W as heat under these conditions.

Practical observation: The regulator may require a suitable heatsink. Actual thermal design must also consider package thermal resistance, ambient temperature and the manufacturer's maximum junction temperature.

14. Lesson Summary

  • Voltage regulation maintains a nearly constant output voltage.
  • Input voltage and load current variations can affect an unregulated power supply.
  • A regulated power supply generally contains transformer, rectifier, filter and regulator stages.
  • A voltage regulator compensates for changes in input and load conditions.
  • Linear and switching regulators are the two major regulator categories.
  • Fixed and adjustable regulators provide different output-voltage options.
  • Important specifications include line regulation, load regulation, efficiency and dropout voltage.
  • Linear regulators can dissipate significant power as heat.
  • Voltage regulators are widely used in embedded systems, industrial electronics and power supplies.
