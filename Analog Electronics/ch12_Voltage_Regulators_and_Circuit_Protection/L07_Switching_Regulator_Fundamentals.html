2. Introduction to Switching Regulators

A switching regulator is a DC-DC power conversion circuit that regulates output voltage by periodically switching a power semiconductor device between conducting and non-conducting states.

Instead of continuously dissipating excess voltage as heat like a basic linear regulator, a switching regulator controls energy transfer using switching devices, inductors, capacitors and sometimes transformers.

Basic principle:
DC Input → High-Speed Switching → Energy Storage and Transfer → Filtered DC Output

Switching regulators are commonly used in:

  • Mobile phones and computers.
  • Embedded systems.
  • Battery-powered devices.
  • Automotive electronics.
  • Industrial control systems.
  • DC power supplies.
  • LED drivers.
  • Telecommunication equipment.

4. Switching Regulator Block Diagram

Functional blocks

  1. Input supply: supplies electrical energy to the converter.
  2. Power switch: rapidly switches the energy path, commonly using a MOSFET.
  3. Energy transfer network: transfers and stores energy using inductors, capacitors and diodes or synchronous switches.
  4. Output filter: reduces switching ripple.
  5. Feedback circuit: senses output and helps control the switching duty cycle.
  6. PWM controller: adjusts switching behavior to maintain the target output.

5. PWM — Pulse Width Modulation

Pulse Width Modulation (PWM) is a control technique in which a periodic switching signal changes its pulse width to control the average energy delivered to a circuit.

\[ D=\frac{T_{ON}}{T} \]
\[ D(\%)=\frac{T_{ON}}{T}\times100 \]
Where:
  • TON = switch ON time
  • T = switching period
  • D = duty cycle

PWM waveform

When duty cycle changes, the time during which the switch conducts changes. The energy delivered to the converter changes accordingly.

Duty cycleMeaning
10%Switch ON for a small portion of each period
25%Switch ON for one-quarter of each period
50%Switch ON for half of each period
75%Switch ON for three-quarters of each period
90%Switch ON for most of each period

6. Switching Frequency and Period

Switching frequency describes how many switching cycles occur per second.

\[ f=\frac{1}{T} \]
\[ T=\frac{1}{f} \]

Example

A converter operates at a switching frequency of 50 kHz. Calculate the switching period.

\[ T=\frac{1}{50,000} \]
\[ T=20\ \mu s \]
The switching period is 20 microseconds.

Higher switching frequency may permit smaller inductors and capacitors, but it can increase switching losses, electromagnetic interference and design difficulty. The optimum frequency depends on the converter and its application.

7. Power Components of a Switching Regulator

7.1 MOSFET

A MOSFET is frequently used as the switching device. The controller drives its gate to control conduction.

  • Low ON-state resistance can reduce conduction losses.
  • Switching speed affects switching losses.
  • Gate-drive voltage must meet device requirements.
  • Voltage, current, SOA and thermal ratings must be considered.

7.2 Inductor

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field when current flows through it.

\[ V_L=L\frac{di}{dt} \]
  • Opposes rapid changes in current.
  • Stores energy during some portions of the switching cycle.
  • Transfers energy during other portions.
  • Inductor saturation can cause excessive current and damage.

7.3 Diode

In non-synchronous converters, a diode may provide a current path when the main switch is OFF.

  • Forward voltage affects conduction losses.
  • Reverse recovery can affect switching behavior.
  • Current and reverse-voltage ratings are important.
  • Schottky diodes are frequently used in certain low-voltage converters.

7.4 Capacitor

Output capacitors store charge and help reduce output voltage ripple.

\[ i_C=C\frac{dv}{dt} \]
  • Capacitance affects ripple and transient response.
  • ESR contributes to ripple and heat.
  • Voltage rating and ripple-current rating matter.
  • Capacitor technology and layout affect performance.

8. Energy Storage and Transfer

Switching converters use energy storage elements to transfer electrical energy between input and output.

During switching

  • The switch changes state periodically.
  • Inductor current changes according to applied voltage and inductance.
  • The converter stores and transfers energy.
  • The output capacitor smooths the delivered energy.
  • The controller adjusts the switching pattern to regulate the output.
Energy stored in an inductor: \[ E_L=\frac12 LI^2 \]
Energy stored in a capacitor: \[ E_C=\frac12 CV^2 \]

10. Ideal Buck Converter Relationship

For an ideal buck converter operating in continuous conduction mode (CCM) under steady-state conditions, the output voltage is related to duty cycle.

\[ V_{OUT}=D V_{IN} \]
\[ D=\frac{V_{OUT}}{V_{IN}} \]

Example

An ideal buck converter has VIN = 12 V and duty cycle D = 0.4. Calculate the output voltage.

\[ V_{OUT}=0.4\times12 \]
VOUT = 4.8 V
This relationship applies to the ideal buck converter in steady-state continuous conduction mode. Actual converter behavior depends on topology, losses, control, load and operating mode.

11. Ideal Boost Converter Relationship

For an ideal boost converter operating in continuous conduction mode under steady-state conditions:

\[ V_{OUT}=\frac{V_{IN}}{1-D} \]
\[ D=1-\frac{V_{IN}}{V_{OUT}} \]

Example

VIN = 5 V and D = 0.5. Calculate the ideal output voltage.

\[ V_{OUT}=\frac{5}{1-0.5} \]
\[ V_{OUT}=\frac{5}{0.5} \]
VOUT = 10 V
This is an ideal CCM relationship, not a promise that a practical boost converter will achieve this output. Switch, diode, inductor, current, control and thermal limitations matter.

13. Switching Regulator Efficiency

Efficiency describes the proportion of input power delivered to the output.

\[ \eta=\frac{P_{OUT}}{P_{IN}}\times100 \]

For a DC output:

\[ P_{OUT}=V_{OUT}I_{OUT} \]
\[ P_{IN}=V_{IN}I_{IN} \]

Worked example

A converter receives 12 V at 1 A and supplies 5 V at 2 A.

\[ P_{IN}=12\times1=12W \]
\[ P_{OUT}=5\times2=10W \]
\[ \eta=\frac{10}{12}\times100 \]
Efficiency = 83.33%

Approximate power loss:

\[ P_{LOSS}=P_{IN}-P_{OUT} \]
Loss = 2 W

Switching loss concept:
Losses can increase as switching frequency increases, but the actual dependence is influenced by device characteristics, transition behavior, topology and control.

16. Electromagnetic Interference (EMI)

Rapid switching edges and high-frequency current loops can generate conducted and radiated electromagnetic interference.

Common EMI reduction techniques

  • Minimize high-frequency switching loops.
  • Use a suitable PCB layout.
  • Place decoupling capacitors close to the appropriate power pins.
  • Use appropriate grounding and return paths.
  • Select suitable inductors and capacitors.
  • Use filtering where needed.
  • Control switching edge rates where the design permits.
  • Validate the design with appropriate measurement equipment.
Switching converter PCB layout is a critical engineering skill. A circuit that works on a schematic can perform poorly when its physical layout creates excessive parasitic inductance, capacitance or noise.

17. Practical Lab: Switching Regulator Observation

Objective

Observe PWM switching, measure input/output power and investigate efficiency using a low-voltage switching converter module.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage DC supply with current limiting.
  • DC-DC converter module.
  • Multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope.
  • Suitable resistive load.
  • Connecting wires.

Procedure

  1. Read the converter module specifications.
  2. Identify VIN, GND and VOUT terminals.
  3. Set the input supply within the rated range.
  4. Initially operate with a suitable light load.
  5. Measure the input voltage.
  6. Measure the output voltage.
  7. Measure input current.
  8. Measure output current.
  9. Calculate input and output power.
  10. Calculate efficiency.
  11. Observe switching waveforms using a properly grounded oscilloscope setup.
  12. Increase load gradually within the module ratings.
  13. Record output voltage and efficiency.
  14. Observe thermal behavior.
  15. Compare performance at different loads.

22. Key Takeaways

  • Switching regulators regulate voltage through periodic switching and controlled energy transfer.
  • PWM controls the switch ON time.
  • Duty cycle is the fraction of a period spent ON.
  • MOSFETs, inductors, diodes and capacitors are important switching converter components.
  • Buck converters step down and boost converters step up in their basic configurations.
  • Switching regulators can offer high efficiency but introduce switching ripple and EMI.
  • PCB layout, feedback stability, thermal design and component selection are critical.
  • Efficiency is calculated using output power divided by input power.
