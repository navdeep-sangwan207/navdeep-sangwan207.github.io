2. Introduction to Charge Carriers

Electric current in a semiconductor is produced by the movement of two types of charge carriers:

  • Electrons: Negatively charged particles that move through the conduction band.
  • Holes: Effective positively charged carriers associated with missing electrons in the valence band.

The relative concentrations of electrons and holes depend on whether the semiconductor is intrinsic or doped.

Electronic charge magnitude:
q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
A hole is not a separate positively charged particle like a proton. It represents the absence of an electron in an otherwise occupied valence-band state and behaves as a mobile positive charge carrier.

3. Majority Carriers

Majority carriers are the charge carriers present in higher concentration in a semiconductor.

  • Electrons are majority carriers in N-type semiconductors.
  • Holes are majority carriers in P-type semiconductors.

3.1 Majority Carriers in N-Type Material

N-type material is formed by adding pentavalent donor impurities such as phosphorus, arsenic or antimony to pure silicon.

Donor atoms contribute electrons to the conduction band. Therefore, the electron concentration becomes much greater than the hole concentration.

Conceptual illustration. The number of symbols does not represent an actual carrier concentration ratio.

3.2 Majority Carrier Concentration in N-Type Material

For a non-compensated N-type semiconductor with sufficiently ionized donors and negligible intrinsic contribution:

n ≈ ND

Where n is electron concentration and ND is donor concentration.

This approximation is valid under common extrinsic-region assumptions. At very low temperatures, very high doping levels or in compensated material, a more complete carrier-statistics calculation may be required.

4. Minority Carriers

Minority carriers are the charge carriers present in lower concentration in a semiconductor.

  • Holes are minority carriers in N-type material.
  • Electrons are minority carriers in P-type material.

4.1 Minority Carriers in N-Type Material

Even though electrons dominate in N-type material, a small number of holes are still present. These holes are generated mainly through thermal generation of electron-hole pairs.

4.2 Minority Carriers in P-Type Material

P-type material contains a small number of electrons even though holes are its majority carriers. These electrons are minority carriers.

Conceptual illustration. The number of symbols does not represent an actual carrier concentration ratio.

5. Carrier Comparison in Different Semiconductors

Semiconductor Electron Concentration Hole Concentration Carrier Relationship
Intrinsic n = ni p = ni n = p
N-Type High Low n > p
P-Type Low High p > n
In an intrinsic semiconductor, electron and hole concentrations are equal. In an extrinsic semiconductor, one carrier type becomes dominant because of doping.

6. Thermal Generation of Electron-Hole Pairs

When a semiconductor absorbs thermal energy, some valence-band electrons gain sufficient energy to move into the conduction band.

This process produces:

  • One free electron in the conduction band.
  • One hole in the valence band.

The electron and hole are generated as a pair. In an intrinsic semiconductor at thermal equilibrium, their concentrations remain equal.

Conceptual energy-band representation of electron-hole pair generation.

At thermal equilibrium, generation and recombination rates balance. The carrier concentrations remain steady even though individual generation and recombination events continue.

7. Recombination of Charge Carriers

Recombination occurs when a conduction-band electron occupies an available hole state in the valence band. The electron-hole pair is removed as mobile carriers.

7.1 Generation and Recombination

Generation Recombination
Produces an electron-hole pair. Removes an electron-hole pair.
Can be caused by thermal or optical energy. May release energy as heat or light, depending on the material and mechanism.
Increases mobile carrier population. Decreases mobile carrier population.

7.2 Important Recombination Mechanisms

  • Band-to-band recombination: An electron recombines directly with a hole.
  • Trap-assisted recombination: Defect or impurity energy levels participate in recombination.
  • Auger recombination: Energy released during recombination is transferred to another carrier.

These mechanisms are important in semiconductor device design, especially in LEDs, photodetectors, solar cells and high-speed devices.

8. Mass-Action Law

At thermal equilibrium, the product of electron concentration and hole concentration in a semiconductor is equal to the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration.

n × p = ni²

Where:

  • n = Electron concentration in cm⁻³
  • p = Hole concentration in cm⁻³
  • ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration in cm⁻³

8.1 For N-Type Semiconductor

If donor doping is sufficiently strong, donors are substantially ionized, and compensation is negligible:

n ≈ ND

p = ni²/n

p ≈ ni²/ND

8.2 For P-Type Semiconductor

Under corresponding assumptions:

p ≈ NA

n = ni²/p

n ≈ ni²/NA
The mass-action law is an equilibrium relationship. Under strong illumination, carrier injection or other nonequilibrium conditions, the equilibrium product relationship cannot be applied directly using the actual carrier concentrations.

9. Carrier Mobility

Mobility describes how easily a charge carrier moves through a semiconductor under an applied electric field.

μ = vd / E

Where:

  • μ = Carrier mobility in cm²/(V·s)
  • vd = Drift velocity in cm/s
  • E = Electric field in V/cm

9.1 Electron and Hole Mobility

In silicon at room temperature and under low-field conditions, electron mobility is generally higher than hole mobility.

Carrier Typical Low-Doping Silicon Mobility at 300 K
Electrons Approximately 1350 cm²/(V·s)
Holes Approximately 480 cm²/(V·s)
These are approximate reference values. Actual mobility depends on doping concentration, temperature, crystal quality and electric field.

10. Semiconductor Conductivity

Semiconductor conductivity depends on the concentrations and mobilities of both electrons and holes.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Where:

  • σ = Conductivity in S/cm
  • q = Electronic charge = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • n = Electron concentration in cm⁻³
  • p = Hole concentration in cm⁻³
  • μn = Electron mobility in cm²/(V·s)
  • μp = Hole mobility in cm²/(V·s)

10.1 Conductivity of N-Type Material

Electron contribution is generally dominant because electron concentration is much greater than hole concentration.

σ ≈ qnμn

10.2 Conductivity of P-Type Material

Hole contribution is generally dominant because hole concentration is much greater than electron concentration.

σ ≈ qpμp

10.3 Resistivity

ρ = 1/σ

If conductivity is expressed in S/cm, resistivity is expressed in Ω·cm.

11. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Minority Carrier Concentration in N-Type Silicon

Given:

  • n = 2 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Find: Hole concentration p.

np = ni²

p = ni²/n

p = (1 × 10¹⁰)²/(2 × 10¹⁶)

p = 0.5 × 10⁴

p = 5 × 10³ cm⁻³

Answer: Minority hole concentration = 5 × 10³ cm⁻³.

Numerical 2: Minority Carrier Concentration in P-Type Silicon

Given:

  • p = 4 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Find: Electron concentration n.

n = ni²/p

n = (1 × 10¹⁰)²/(4 × 10¹⁵)

n = 2.5 × 10⁴ cm⁻³

Answer: Minority electron concentration = 2.5 × 10⁴ cm⁻³.

Numerical 3: Conductivity of N-Type Silicon

Given:

  • n = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • p = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³
  • μn = 1350 cm²/(V·s)
  • μp = 480 cm²/(V·s)
  • q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Find: Conductivity σ.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

σ = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ [(1 × 10¹⁶ × 1350) + (1 × 10⁴ × 480)]

σ ≈ 2.16 S/cm

Answer: Conductivity ≈ 2.16 S/cm.

Numerical 4: Conductivity of P-Type Silicon

Given:

  • p = 5 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
  • n = 2 × 10⁴ cm⁻³
  • μp = 480 cm²/(V·s)
  • μn = 1350 cm²/(V·s)
  • q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Find: Conductivity σ.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

σ = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ [(2 × 10⁴ × 1350) + (5 × 10¹⁵ × 480)]

σ ≈ 0.384 S/cm

Answer: Conductivity ≈ 0.384 S/cm.

Numerical 5: Resistivity

Given: Conductivity σ = 0.384 S/cm.

Find: Resistivity ρ.

ρ = 1/σ

ρ = 1/0.384

ρ ≈ 2.604 Ω·cm

Answer: Resistivity ≈ 2.604 Ω·cm.

Numerical 6: Drift Velocity

Given:

  • Electron mobility μn = 1350 cm²/(V·s)
  • Electric field E = 10 V/cm

Find: Electron drift velocity.

vd = μnE

vd = 1350 × 10

vd = 13,500 cm/s

Answer: Drift velocity = 13,500 cm/s.

12. Effect of Temperature on Carrier Concentration

Temperature influences the carrier concentration and mobility of a semiconductor.

12.1 Intrinsic Semiconductor

As temperature increases, more electrons gain enough thermal energy to cross the band gap. Electron-hole pair concentration increases.

12.2 Extrinsic Semiconductor

Three broad temperature regions are commonly discussed:

  1. Freeze-out region: At low temperature, many dopant atoms are not ionized, so free-carrier concentration is low.
  2. Extrinsic region: Most dopants are ionized. Carrier concentration is mainly determined by doping.
  3. Intrinsic region: At high temperature, thermally generated carriers become significant and may dominate the carrier population.
Increasing temperature generally increases intrinsic carrier concentration. Mobility often decreases with temperature in ordinary room-temperature and higher-temperature operating ranges because of increased lattice scattering.

13. Importance of Minority Carriers in Electronic Devices

Device Importance of Minority Carriers
PN Junction Diode Contribute to reverse saturation current and transient charge storage.
BJT Carrier injection and transport across the base are central to transistor action.
Photodiode Photogenerated carriers are collected to produce photocurrent.
Solar Cell Generated minority carriers can be collected before recombination.
LED Injected electrons and holes recombine, producing light in suitable materials.

13.1 Minority Carrier Lifetime

Minority carrier lifetime is the average time an excess minority carrier exists before recombination.

Excess minority carrier concentration:
Δn(t) = Δn(0)e−t/τ

Here, τ represents the carrier lifetime in this simple exponential-decay model.

Carrier lifetime is important in switching speed, charge storage, diode reverse recovery, photodetectors and solar cells.

14. Practical Troubleshooting and Engineering Relevance

14.1 Why Does a Diode Have Reverse Leakage Current?

Reverse leakage current is strongly associated with minority carriers generated thermally and collected by the junction's electric field. Real diodes may also have surface and defect-related leakage components.

14.2 Why Does Temperature Affect Diode Leakage?

Higher temperature increases thermal carrier generation. This generally increases reverse leakage current.

14.3 Why Does Doping Change Conductivity?

Doping changes carrier concentration. Increasing the majority-carrier concentration generally increases conductivity, although mobility can decrease due to impurity scattering.

14.4 What Can a Multimeter Tell You?

  • A diode-test function can help identify forward conduction and reverse blocking behaviour.
  • Resistance measurements can help detect obvious open or short faults when the circuit is powered off.
  • A normal multimeter cannot directly measure electron or hole concentration in a semiconductor.
Disconnect power before resistance or continuity measurements. Discharge capacitors safely and follow the instrument's specified measurement procedure.

15. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What are charge carriers in a semiconductor?
Electrons and holes are the two charge carriers responsible for conduction in a semiconductor.
Q2. What are majority carriers?
They are the charge carriers present in higher concentration in a semiconductor.
Q3. What are minority carriers?
They are the charge carriers present in lower concentration.
Q4. What are the majority carriers in N-type material?
Electrons.
Q5. What are the minority carriers in N-type material?
Holes.
Q6. What are the majority carriers in P-type material?
Holes.
Q7. What are the minority carriers in P-type material?
Electrons.
Q8. What is the relationship between electron and hole concentrations in intrinsic silicon?
At thermal equilibrium, n = p = ni.
Q9. State the mass-action law.
At thermal equilibrium, np = ni².
Q10. If electron concentration increases at a fixed temperature, what happens to hole concentration at equilibrium?
According to the mass-action law, hole concentration decreases.
Q11. What is carrier mobility?
Mobility is the drift velocity produced per unit electric field.
Q12. Which has higher mobility in silicon, electrons or holes?
Electrons generally have higher low-field mobility than holes.
Q13. What is drift velocity?
It is the average directed velocity acquired by charge carriers under an electric field.
Q14. What is thermal generation?
It is the creation of electron-hole pairs when electrons gain sufficient energy to move into the conduction band.
Q15. What is recombination?
It is the process in which an electron occupies a hole state, removing a mobile electron-hole pair.
Q16. What is minority carrier lifetime?
It is the average time an excess minority carrier exists before recombination.
Q17. Why are minority carriers important in a PN junction?
They contribute to reverse leakage current and influence transient and switching behaviour.
Q18. What happens to intrinsic carrier concentration when temperature increases?
It increases because thermal generation of electron-hole pairs becomes more significant.
Q19. Write the semiconductor conductivity equation.
σ = q(nμn + pμp).
Q20. What is the relationship between conductivity and resistivity?
Resistivity is the reciprocal of conductivity: ρ = 1/σ.
Q21. Why does reverse leakage current increase with temperature?
Higher temperature increases thermally generated carriers, which can contribute to reverse current.
Q22. Does a P-type semiconductor contain electrons?
Yes. Electrons are present as minority carriers.
Q23. Does an N-type semiconductor contain holes?
Yes. Holes are present as minority carriers.
Q24. Can minority carriers become majority carriers?
The majority/minority classification depends on the relative concentrations. Under sufficiently strong injection or nonequilibrium conditions, excess carriers can significantly change those concentrations.
Q25. What is the difference between carrier concentration and carrier mobility?
Carrier concentration describes the number of carriers per unit volume. Mobility describes how readily carriers move under an electric field.
