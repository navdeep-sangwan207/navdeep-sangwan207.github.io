1. Introduction to Extrinsic Semiconductors

An extrinsic semiconductor is a semiconductor whose electrical properties have been modified by intentionally adding a small, controlled quantity of impurity atoms.

This process is known as doping.

Silicon and germanium are commonly used as the base materials for producing extrinsic semiconductors.

Why Do We Need Extrinsic Semiconductors?

  • To increase electrical conductivity.
  • To control the number of free electrons and holes.
  • To create n-type and p-type semiconductor regions.
  • To manufacture PN junction diodes.
  • To manufacture transistors and integrated circuits.
Definition: An extrinsic semiconductor is a semiconductor intentionally doped with suitable impurity atoms to control its electrical conductivity.

2. What is Semiconductor Doping?

Doping is the controlled process of introducing impurity atoms into a pure semiconductor crystal.

The added impurities change the concentration of charge carriers and therefore modify the electrical conductivity of the semiconductor.

3. Types of Extrinsic Semiconductors

Based on the type of impurity added, extrinsic semiconductors are classified into two main types.

It is formed by adding pentavalent impurity atoms to a pure semiconductor.

Electrons become the majority charge carriers.

It is formed by adding trivalent impurity atoms to a pure semiconductor.

Holes become the majority charge carriers.

4. Pentavalent Impurity Atoms

Pentavalent impurity atoms contain five valence electrons in their outermost shell.

When a pentavalent atom is added to a silicon crystal, four of its valence electrons participate in covalent bonding with neighboring silicon atoms.

The fifth electron is weakly bound and can easily become a conduction electron at ordinary temperatures.

Examples

  • Phosphorus (P)
  • Arsenic (As)
  • Antimony (Sb)
Key Concept: Pentavalent impurity atoms donate electrons to the semiconductor and are therefore called donor impurities.

5. Donor Impurity and Donor Energy Level

A donor impurity is a pentavalent atom that supplies an electron to the conduction band of a semiconductor.

After donating an electron, the donor atom becomes a positively charged ion fixed in the crystal lattice.

Donor Energy Level

The additional electron occupies a donor state located slightly below the conduction band.

6. N-Type Semiconductor

An n-type semiconductor is formed by doping pure silicon or germanium with pentavalent impurity atoms.

The additional electrons supplied by donor impurities increase the electron concentration.

Formation of N-Type Semiconductor

  1. Start with a pure silicon crystal.
  2. Add a small quantity of pentavalent impurity.
  3. Four valence electrons form covalent bonds.
  4. The fifth electron is weakly bound to the donor atom.
  5. The electron can enter the conduction band through thermal excitation.

7. Trivalent Impurity Atoms

Trivalent impurity atoms contain three valence electrons in their outermost shell.

When a trivalent atom is added to a silicon crystal, its three valence electrons form three covalent bonds with neighboring silicon atoms.

One bonding position remains incomplete, creating an acceptor state that can accept an electron from a neighboring bond.

Examples

  • Boron (B)
  • Aluminium (Al)
  • Gallium (Ga)
  • Indium (In)
Key Concept: Trivalent impurity atoms accept electrons and are therefore called acceptor impurities.

8. Acceptor Impurity and Acceptor Energy Level

An acceptor impurity is a trivalent atom that can accept an electron from the valence band.

When an electron occupies the acceptor state, a hole is created in the valence band.

After accepting an electron, the acceptor atom becomes a negatively charged ion fixed in the crystal lattice.

Acceptor Energy Level

The acceptor energy level is located slightly above the valence band.

9. P-Type Semiconductor

A p-type semiconductor is formed by doping pure silicon or germanium with trivalent impurity atoms.

The acceptor impurities create holes in the valence band, increasing the hole concentration.

Formation of P-Type Semiconductor

  1. Start with a pure silicon crystal.
  2. Add a small quantity of trivalent impurity.
  3. Three valence electrons participate in covalent bonding.
  4. An acceptor state is formed due to the incomplete bonding position.
  5. An electron from the valence band can occupy this state, leaving a hole behind.

10. Majority and Minority Charge Carriers

In an extrinsic semiconductor, one type of charge carrier has a much higher concentration than the other type.

Majority Carriers

The charge carriers having the higher concentration are called majority carriers.

Minority Carriers

The charge carriers having the lower concentration are called minority carriers.

11. Carrier Concentration in Extrinsic Semiconductors

Carrier concentration depends on doping concentration, temperature and the semiconductor material.

N-Type Semiconductor

For a fully ionized, non-degenerate n-type semiconductor with donor concentration much greater than intrinsic carrier concentration:

n ≈ ND

p = ni² / n

Where ND represents donor concentration.

P-Type Semiconductor

p ≈ NA

n = ni² / p

Where NA represents acceptor concentration.

Assumption: These approximations apply when the dopants are substantially ionized and the semiconductor is non-degenerate. Compensation and high doping require more complete carrier calculations.

12. Law of Mass Action in Extrinsic Semiconductors

At thermal equilibrium, the product of electron and hole concentrations follows the law of mass action.

np = ni²

Example: N-Type Semiconductor

Given:
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • n = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
Calculate hole concentration.
p = ni² / n

p = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (1 × 10¹⁶)

p = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³
Answer: Hole concentration = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³.

Example: P-Type Semiconductor

Given:
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • p = 1 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
Calculate electron concentration.
n = ni² / p

n = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (1 × 10¹⁵)

n = 1 × 10⁵ cm⁻³
Answer: Electron concentration = 1 × 10⁵ cm⁻³.

13. Fermi Level in Extrinsic Semiconductors

The Fermi level represents an energy reference associated with the probability of electron occupation of available states.

Doping changes the position of the Fermi level within the semiconductor band gap.

N-Type Semiconductor

  • The Fermi level moves toward the conduction band.
  • Electron concentration increases.
  • Donor impurities introduce energy levels near the conduction band.

P-Type Semiconductor

  • The Fermi level moves toward the valence band.
  • Hole concentration increases.
  • Acceptor impurities introduce energy levels near the valence band.
N-Type: Fermi Level Near Conduction Band
Intrinsic: Near Mid-Gap
P-Type: Near Valence Band
Placement Point: N-type doping shifts the Fermi level upward toward the conduction band. P-type doping shifts it downward toward the valence band.

15. Semiconductor Doping Techniques

Semiconductor manufacturing uses controlled techniques to introduce impurity atoms into selected regions of a semiconductor wafer.

1. Diffusion

Diffusion introduces dopant atoms into a semiconductor by heating the wafer in a controlled dopant environment. Dopant atoms move into the material due to concentration gradients.

2. Ion Implantation

Ion implantation accelerates dopant ions and directs them into the semiconductor surface. It provides precise control over dopant dose and distribution.

Comparison

Property Diffusion Ion Implantation
Method Thermal diffusion of dopants Accelerated dopant ions
Control Controlled through time and temperature Precise dose and energy control
Crystal Damage Generally low implantation damage Can cause lattice damage
Additional Process Thermal processing Annealing is commonly required
Industry Connection: Diffusion and ion implantation are important semiconductor fabrication processes used in manufacturing integrated circuits and transistors.
