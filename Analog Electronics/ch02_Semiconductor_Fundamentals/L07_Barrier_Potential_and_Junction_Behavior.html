2. What Is Barrier Potential?

Barrier potential is the internal potential difference established across the depletion region of a PN junction. It opposes the continued diffusion of majority carriers across the junction.

When a PN junction is formed:

  1. Electrons diffuse from the N-side towards the P-side.
  2. Holes diffuse from the P-side towards the N-side.
  3. Some electrons and holes recombine near the junction.
  4. Fixed ionized donor and acceptor atoms remain exposed.
  5. The resulting space charge establishes an electric field.
  6. The electric field produces an internal potential difference.
Barrier potential = Built-in potential = Vbi
In this lecture, barrier potential refers to the built-in equilibrium potential of the junction, not the voltage drop measured across a conducting diode under forward bias.

3. Why Does Barrier Potential Develop?

Initially, the carrier concentration difference across the junction causes diffusion. As carriers diffuse and recombine, fixed charged dopant ions become exposed.

  • The P-side depletion region contains negative acceptor ions.
  • The N-side depletion region contains positive donor ions.

These charges establish an electric field directed from the N-side towards the P-side.

Simplified representation of the space-charge region and electric field.

The electric field opposes further majority-carrier diffusion. Equilibrium is established when the drift and diffusion components balance.

4. Built-In Potential Equation

For an ideal abrupt PN junction at thermal equilibrium, assuming non-degenerate doping and complete dopant ionization:

Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NA × ND / ni²)

Where:

  • Vbi = Built-in potential in volts.
  • k = Boltzmann constant = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K.
  • T = Absolute temperature in kelvin.
  • q = Electronic charge = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • NA = Acceptor concentration in cm⁻³.
  • ND = Donor concentration in cm⁻³.
  • ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration in cm⁻³.

4.1 Thermal Voltage

VT = kT/q

At T = 300 K:
VT ≈ 0.02585 V = 25.85 mV

Therefore, the built-in potential can also be written as:

Vbi = VT ln(NA ND / ni²)

5. Typical Barrier Potential Values

The barrier potential depends on semiconductor material, temperature and doping concentrations. The following values are broad textbook approximations, not fixed operating voltage drops.

Semiconductor Material Common Approximate Barrier Potential Range
Silicon (Si) Approximately 0.6–0.8 V
Germanium (Ge) Approximately 0.2–0.3 V
Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) Depends strongly on junction doping and temperature; often around 1 V or higher
Do not treat these ranges as universal constants. The actual built-in potential is calculated from the material's intrinsic carrier concentration, doping and temperature.

6. Factors Affecting Barrier Potential

6.1 Doping Concentration

From the built-in potential equation, increasing NA or ND generally increases Vbi if the other parameters remain fixed.

Vbi = VT ln(NA ND / ni²)

A higher doping concentration changes the carrier concentration ratio and therefore changes the equilibrium barrier potential.

6.2 Temperature

Temperature affects both thermal voltage and intrinsic carrier concentration.

  • Thermal voltage VT increases approximately linearly with absolute temperature.
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration ni increases strongly with temperature.
  • For fixed, fully ionized dopant concentrations in ordinary silicon, built-in potential generally decreases as temperature rises.

6.3 Semiconductor Material

Different materials have different band gaps and intrinsic carrier concentrations. These differences affect the built-in potential.

7. PN Junction at Thermal Equilibrium

A PN junction without an externally applied voltage is called an unbiased junction.

At thermal equilibrium:

  • The depletion region is established.
  • An internal electric field exists.
  • A built-in potential exists across the depletion region.
  • Diffusion and drift components balance.
  • Net terminal current is zero.
Net current = Diffusion current + Drift current = 0
Equilibrium does not mean that all carrier movement stops. Individual carriers continue to move, but the opposing transport mechanisms balance macroscopically.

8. Energy-Band Behaviour at Equilibrium

At thermal equilibrium, the Fermi level is constant throughout the junction. The conduction-band and valence-band edges bend across the junction because of the built-in electrostatic potential.

Conceptual band diagram. The Fermi level is flat at equilibrium; band bending represents the built-in junction potential.

This is a qualitative diagram, not a quantitative energy-scale drawing. The actual band-edge positions depend on doping and material parameters.

9. Forward-Biased Junction Behaviour

A PN junction is forward biased when the positive terminal of an external supply is connected to the P-side and the negative terminal is connected to the N-side.

9.1 Forward-Bias Connections

Forward bias: supply positive to P and supply negative to N.

9.2 What Happens Under Forward Bias?

  1. The applied voltage opposes the built-in electric field.
  2. The effective potential barrier is reduced.
  3. The depletion region becomes narrower.
  4. Majority carriers can cross the junction more easily.
  5. Significant forward current flows when the applied voltage is sufficient.
Approximate effective barrier under forward bias:
Vbarrier ≈ Vbi − VF
The expression above is a simplified electrostatic description. VF is the externally applied forward junction voltage. The current-voltage relationship is nonlinear and is treated in detail in the diode V-I characteristics lecture.

10. Reverse-Biased Junction Behaviour

A PN junction is reverse biased when the negative terminal of an external supply is connected to the P-side and the positive terminal is connected to the N-side.

10.1 Reverse-Bias Connections

Reverse bias: supply negative to P and supply positive to N.

10.2 What Happens Under Reverse Bias?

  1. The applied voltage reinforces the built-in electric field.
  2. The effective potential barrier increases.
  3. The depletion region becomes wider.
  4. Majority-carrier diffusion across the junction is strongly suppressed.
  5. A small reverse current can flow due to minority carriers and other leakage mechanisms.
Approximate effective barrier under reverse bias:
Vbarrier ≈ Vbi + VR
Reverse bias does not mean absolutely zero current. Real diodes have reverse leakage current. If reverse voltage reaches the breakdown region, current can rise sharply.

11. Comparison: Unbiased, Forward-Biased and Reverse-Biased Junction

Parameter Unbiased Forward Biased Reverse Biased
External voltage None P positive, N negative P negative, N positive
Effective barrier Vbi Reduced Increased
Depletion width Equilibrium width Decreases Increases
Majority-carrier diffusion Balanced by drift Strongly increases Strongly suppressed
Terminal current Zero net current Significant forward current Small leakage before breakdown

12. Effect of Applied Voltage on Depletion Width

For an ideal abrupt junction under the depletion approximation:

W = √[(2εs/q)(1/NA + 1/ND)(Vbi − V)]

Where:

  • W = Total depletion width.
  • εs = Semiconductor permittivity.
  • q = Electronic charge.
  • NA = Acceptor concentration.
  • ND = Donor concentration.
  • Vbi = Built-in potential.
  • V = Applied junction voltage, positive for forward bias and negative for reverse bias.

12.1 Forward Bias

Positive V reduces the quantity (Vbi − V), so depletion width decreases.

12.2 Reverse Bias

Negative V makes (Vbi − V) larger, so depletion width increases.

The depletion-width equation assumes the junction is not driven into conditions where breakdown, strong high-level injection, significant series resistance or other non-ideal effects invalidate the simple depletion approximation.

13. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Calculate Built-In Potential

Given:

  • NA = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • ND = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • T = 300 K
  • VT = 0.02585 V

Find: Vbi.

Vbi = VT ln(NA ND / ni²)

Vbi = 0.02585 ln[(10¹⁶ × 10¹⁶)/(10¹⁰ × 10¹⁰)]

Vbi = 0.02585 ln(10¹²)

Vbi = 0.02585 × 27.631

Vbi ≈ 0.714 V

Answer: Built-in potential ≈ 0.714 V.

Numerical 2: Calculate Barrier Potential for a Different Doping Level

Given:

  • NA = 2 × 10¹⁷ cm⁻³
  • ND = 5 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • VT = 0.02585 V
Vbi = 0.02585 ln[(2 × 10¹⁷ × 5 × 10¹⁵)/(10²⁰)]

Vbi = 0.02585 ln(10¹³)

Vbi = 0.02585 × 29.934

Vbi ≈ 0.774 V

Answer: Built-in potential ≈ 0.774 V.

Numerical 3: Approximate Barrier Under Forward Bias

Given:

  • Built-in potential Vbi = 0.714 V
  • Applied forward junction voltage VF = 0.20 V
Vbarrier ≈ Vbi − VF

Vbarrier ≈ 0.714 − 0.20

Vbarrier ≈ 0.514 V

Answer: Approximate remaining barrier = 0.514 V.

Numerical 4: Approximate Barrier Under Reverse Bias

Given:

  • Built-in potential Vbi = 0.714 V
  • Applied reverse voltage VR = 2 V
Vbarrier ≈ Vbi + VR

Vbarrier ≈ 0.714 + 2

Vbarrier ≈ 2.714 V

Answer: Approximate barrier = 2.714 V.

The last two calculations illustrate the electrostatic barrier change in the depletion approximation. They do not calculate the diode's terminal voltage drop or current.

Numerical 5: Calculate Thermal Voltage at 350 K

Given:

  • k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K
  • T = 350 K
  • q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
VT = kT/q

VT = (1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 350)/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)

VT ≈ 0.03015 V

VT ≈ 30.15 mV

Answer: Thermal voltage ≈ 30.15 mV.

14. Practical Diode Behaviour and Engineering Relevance

14.1 Why Does a Diode Conduct in Forward Bias?

Forward bias reduces the junction barrier and allows majority carriers to cross the junction. This produces significant forward current.

14.2 Why Does a Diode Block Current in Reverse Bias?

Reverse bias increases the barrier and widens the depletion region, suppressing majority-carrier diffusion. A small reverse leakage current remains.

14.3 Why Does a Reverse-Biased Diode Have a Wide Depletion Region?

The applied reverse voltage reinforces the junction electric field and pulls mobile carriers away from the junction.

14.4 What Happens at Breakdown?

At sufficiently high reverse voltage, breakdown mechanisms such as Zener tunnelling or avalanche multiplication can produce a large reverse current.

A reverse-biased diode must be operated within its specified reverse-voltage and power limits. Use current limiting and suitable protective components when testing breakdown behaviour.

15. Common Technical Mistakes

  • Confusing built-in potential with the forward voltage drop of a conducting diode.
  • Assuming that the depletion region is completely empty of all charge.
  • Reversing the direction of the junction electric field.
  • Assuming reverse bias produces exactly zero current.
  • Assuming barrier potential is always a fixed value for a given material.
  • Forgetting that doping and temperature affect built-in potential.
  • Assuming forward bias widens the depletion region.
  • Using the equilibrium built-in potential equation without checking its assumptions.

16. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is barrier potential?
It is the internal potential difference across the depletion region that opposes majority-carrier diffusion.
Q2. Why is barrier potential developed?
Diffusion and recombination expose fixed ionized dopant atoms, which establish an electric field and internal potential.
Q3. What is built-in potential?
It is the equilibrium electrostatic potential difference across a PN junction.
Q4. Is barrier potential the same as forward voltage drop?
No. Barrier potential is an internal equilibrium potential. Forward voltage drop is the terminal voltage under an applied forward current.
Q5. Write the built-in potential equation.
Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NA ND/ni²).
Q6. What is thermal voltage?
Thermal voltage is VT = kT/q. It is approximately 25.85 mV at 300 K.
Q7. What is the typical approximate barrier potential of silicon?
Textbooks commonly use approximately 0.6–0.8 V as a broad illustrative range, but the actual built-in potential depends on doping and temperature.
Q8. What is the approximate barrier potential of germanium?
A broad textbook approximation is around 0.2–0.3 V, depending on junction conditions.
Q9. What happens to the barrier under forward bias?
The effective barrier decreases.
Q10. What happens to the barrier under reverse bias?
The effective barrier increases.
Q11. What happens to depletion width under forward bias?
It decreases.
Q12. What happens to depletion width under reverse bias?
It increases.
Q13. What is the direction of the electric field in a PN junction?
From the N-side towards the P-side.
Q14. What is the net current at thermal equilibrium?
Zero, because the opposing drift and diffusion current components balance.
Q15. What is the effect of increased doping on built-in potential?
Increasing either doping concentration generally increases the built-in potential when other parameters remain fixed.
Q16. How does temperature affect built-in potential?
Temperature changes thermal voltage and intrinsic carrier concentration. In ordinary silicon junctions with fixed doping, built-in potential generally decreases as temperature rises.
Q17. Why does a forward-biased PN junction conduct?
Forward bias lowers the barrier and allows majority carriers to cross the junction.
Q18. Why does a reverse-biased PN junction have low current?
Reverse bias increases the barrier and suppresses majority carrier diffusion. Only a small leakage current generally flows before breakdown.
Q19. What is reverse breakdown?
It is a condition in which reverse current increases sharply after the junction reaches its breakdown voltage.
Q20. Name two important breakdown mechanisms.
Zener breakdown and avalanche breakdown.
Q21. Does a diode stop all reverse current?
No. A real diode has reverse leakage current.
Q22. What is the relationship between built-in potential and doping?
Built-in potential depends logarithmically on the product of acceptor and donor concentrations.
Q23. Why is built-in potential not measured directly using an ordinary voltmeter across an unbiased diode?
The internal junction potential is balanced by contact potentials in the complete measurement circuit, so an ordinary terminal voltage measurement does not reveal the internal built-in potential.
Q24. What is the role of the depletion region?
It contains the space charge and electric field that establish the junction barrier and control carrier transport.
Q25. What is the main difference between an unbiased and forward-biased PN junction?
An unbiased junction is at thermal equilibrium with zero net current. Forward bias lowers the barrier and allows significant current to flow.

  • VT = kT/q.
  • Vbi = VT ln(NA ND/ni²).
  • At equilibrium, net current is zero.
  • Forward bias reduces the effective barrier.
  • Reverse bias increases the effective barrier.
  • Forward bias narrows the depletion region.
  • Reverse bias widens the depletion region.
  • Real diodes have reverse leakage current.
  • Reverse breakdown can produce a large current.
