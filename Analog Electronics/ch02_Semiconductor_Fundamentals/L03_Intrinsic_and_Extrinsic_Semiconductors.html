Lesson 3: Energy Band Theory

Understand energy bands, valence band, conduction band, forbidden energy gap and the electrical classification of conductors, semiconductors and insulators.

1. Introduction to Energy Band Theory

Energy Band Theory explains the electrical behavior of solids by describing the allowed energy ranges available to electrons.

When a large number of atoms combine to form a solid, their individual atomic energy levels interact and split into many closely spaced energy levels. These levels form energy bands.

The arrangement of these energy bands helps determine whether a material behaves as a conductor, semiconductor or insulator.

2. Energy Levels in an Atom

Electrons in an isolated atom can occupy only specific allowed energy states.

  • Each electron has a particular energy state.
  • Electrons cannot occupy arbitrary energy values.
  • Energy is associated with the electron's quantum state.

When atoms come close together in a solid, interactions between their electrons cause atomic energy levels to split into many closely spaced levels.

These closely spaced levels form energy bands.

4. Valence Band

The valence band is the highest energy band that is occupied by electrons in the ground-state description of a material.

Electrons in this band are generally associated with bonding between atoms.

Important Characteristics

  • Contains electrons involved in atomic bonding.
  • Can be completely or partially filled, depending on the material.
  • Electrons may gain energy and move to higher energy states.
  • Holes can be created in the valence band.

5. Conduction Band

The conduction band is an allowed energy band in which electrons can occupy mobile states and contribute to electrical conduction.

Important Characteristics

  • Electrons in available conduction-band states can move through the crystal.
  • These electrons contribute to electric current.
  • Electrons can enter the conduction band by receiving sufficient energy.

Example

When a silicon electron receives sufficient energy, it can move from the valence band into the conduction band, leaving behind a hole in the valence band.

6. Forbidden Energy Gap

The forbidden energy gap is the energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band in which no allowed electronic states exist in the ideal band model.

It is also called the band gap and is represented by Eg.

Eg = Ec - Ev

Ec = Conduction Band Minimum
Ev = Valence Band Maximum

Unit

Band gap energy is commonly measured in electron volts (eV).

One electron volt is the energy gained by an electron when it moves through a potential difference of one volt.

1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules

7. Electron Excitation and Band Gap

An electron in the valence band must receive sufficient energy to reach an available state in the conduction band.

For an ideal semiconductor at zero temperature, the minimum energy required for this transition is related to its band gap.

Minimum excitation energy ≈ Eg

The energy can be supplied through different mechanisms:

  • Thermal energy
  • Light energy (photons)
  • Electrical energy

When an electron enters the conduction band, a hole remains in the valence band. This creates an electron-hole pair.

8. Energy Band Structure of Conductors

Conductors allow electric current to flow easily because they have available electronic states that electrons can occupy with little energy.

Energy Band Characteristics

  • The highest occupied band may be partially filled.
  • Alternatively, the valence and conduction bands may overlap.
  • Electrons can move into nearby available states under an electric field.
  • Electrical conductivity is generally high.

9. Energy Band Structure of Semiconductors

Semiconductors have a relatively small forbidden energy gap between their valence band and conduction band.

At absolute zero in an ideal intrinsic semiconductor, the valence band is full and the conduction band is empty.

At higher temperatures, some electrons can gain sufficient energy to enter the conduction band, leaving holes in the valence band.

10. Energy Band Structure of Insulators

Insulators generally have a large forbidden energy gap. Under ordinary conditions, very few electrons can gain enough energy to enter the conduction band.

Characteristics

  • Large band gap.
  • Very low electrical conductivity under normal conditions.
  • Valence band is generally filled.
  • Conduction band is generally empty.

Placement Point

Silicon is widely used in integrated circuits. SiC and GaN are important in high-voltage, high-temperature and high-frequency power electronics.

13. Direct and Indirect Band Gap

Direct Band Gap Semiconductor

In a direct band gap semiconductor, the conduction band minimum and valence band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum (k).

An electron can transition between these band edges by absorbing or emitting a photon without requiring a change in crystal momentum.

Example: Gallium Arsenide (GaAs).

Indirect Band Gap Semiconductor

In an indirect band gap semiconductor, the conduction band minimum and valence band maximum occur at different crystal momentum values.

A phonon is generally required along with the photon to satisfy momentum conservation during an optical transition.

Example: Silicon (Si).

Property Direct Band Gap Indirect Band Gap
Band Edge Position Same k value Different k values
Photon Transition Can occur without phonon assistance Generally requires phonon assistance
Light Emission Generally efficient Generally less efficient
Example GaAs Silicon

Applications

  • GaAs: LEDs, laser diodes and optoelectronic devices.
  • Silicon: Integrated circuits and microcontrollers.

14. Practical Importance in Electronics

  • Diodes: Semiconductor band structure helps explain carrier transport across a PN junction.
  • Transistors: Semiconductor energy bands are fundamental to controlling current.
  • LEDs: Electron-hole recombination produces light.
  • Solar Cells: Photons generate electron-hole pairs.
  • Power Electronics: SiC and GaN support high-voltage and high-temperature applications.
  • Integrated Circuits: Silicon is widely used to manufacture processors and microcontrollers.

16. Lesson Summary

  • Energy bands are formed from closely spaced allowed energy levels in solids.
  • The valence band contains electrons associated with bonding.
  • The conduction band contains mobile electronic states.
  • The forbidden energy gap separates the valence and conduction band edges.
  • Conductors have available states that allow easy electron movement.
  • Semiconductors have a relatively small band gap.
  • Insulators generally have a large band gap.
  • Silicon has an indirect band gap of approximately 1.12 eV at 300 K.
  • GaAs has a direct band gap of approximately 1.42 eV at 300 K.
  • SiC and GaN are important wide band gap semiconductor materials.
