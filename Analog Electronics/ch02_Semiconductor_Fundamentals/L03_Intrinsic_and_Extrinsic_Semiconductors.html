Lecture 3: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

Learn how pure and impurity-doped semiconductor materials conduct electricity, how doping controls carrier concentration, and why semiconductor doping is essential in practical electronic devices.

1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Define intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors.
  • Explain electron-hole pair generation in pure silicon.
  • Understand semiconductor doping and its purpose.
  • Differentiate donor and acceptor impurities.
  • Explain majority and minority carrier concentrations.
  • Understand intrinsic carrier concentration.
  • Apply the mass-action law to basic numerical problems.
  • Explain the practical importance of doping in electronic devices.

2. Introduction to Semiconductor Classification

Semiconductors can be classified according to whether the material contains only its original semiconductor atoms or has been intentionally modified by adding controlled impurity atoms.

The two main types are:

  • Intrinsic semiconductor: A chemically pure semiconductor material without intentional doping.
  • Extrinsic semiconductor: A semiconductor whose electrical properties have been modified by intentionally introducing impurity atoms.

Classification of semiconductor materials.

3. Intrinsic Semiconductors

An intrinsic semiconductor is a pure semiconductor material in which no impurity atoms have been intentionally added to modify its electrical properties.

Silicon and germanium are commonly studied as intrinsic semiconductor materials.

3.1 Carrier generation

At absolute zero, an ideal intrinsic semiconductor has a full valence band and an empty conduction band. As temperature increases, thermal energy can excite some electrons across the band gap.

This process generates:

  • A free electron in the conduction band.
  • A hole in the valence band.
One generated electron-hole pair:
One conduction-band electron + One valence-band hole

3.2 Electron and hole concentrations

In an intrinsic semiconductor, every thermally generated electron is accompanied by one hole. Therefore, their concentrations are equal.

n = p = ni
n = Electron concentration
p = Hole concentration
ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration

Carrier concentration is normally expressed in cm⁻³ or m⁻³.

3.3 Intrinsic carrier concentration

Intrinsic carrier concentration represents the equilibrium concentration of electrons and holes in a pure semiconductor at a specified temperature.

It depends strongly on temperature and semiconductor material.

For silicon at approximately 300 K, a commonly used textbook value for intrinsic carrier concentration is around 1.0 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Exact values vary with the reference data and model used.

Intrinsic semiconductor diagram

Simplified representation of an intrinsic silicon lattice. All lattice sites are occupied by silicon atoms.

3.4 Conductivity of intrinsic semiconductors

Both electrons and holes contribute to intrinsic semiconductor conductivity.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)
For intrinsic material:
σi = qni(μn + μp)

Where:

  • σ = Electrical conductivity (S/cm when concentrations and mobility units are consistent)
  • q = Magnitude of electron charge (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
  • n = Electron concentration
  • p = Hole concentration
  • μn = Electron mobility
  • μp = Hole mobility

4. Extrinsic Semiconductors

An extrinsic semiconductor is produced by introducing a controlled amount of impurity atoms into a pure semiconductor material. This process is called doping.

Doping increases the concentration of one type of charge carrier, making the semiconductor more useful for practical electronic applications.

Why is doping required?

  • To increase electrical conductivity.
  • To control the number of charge carriers.
  • To produce N-type and P-type semiconductor regions.
  • To manufacture PN junction diodes and transistors.
  • To control the electrical behavior of integrated circuits.

Common dopant elements

Impurity category Examples Valence electrons Role in silicon
Pentavalent Phosphorus, arsenic, antimony 5 Donor impurity
Trivalent Boron, aluminium, gallium 3 Acceptor impurity
Dopants are introduced in carefully controlled concentrations. The final electrical behavior also depends on activation, compensation, temperature and material defects.

5. Doping Process

Doping is the controlled introduction of impurity atoms into a semiconductor crystal. It changes the free-electron and hole concentrations.

Common doping techniques

5.1 Thermal diffusion

In diffusion, impurity atoms are introduced into a semiconductor at elevated temperatures. Dopant atoms move through the crystal and form a doped region.

5.2 Ion implantation

In ion implantation, dopant ions are accelerated and directed into the semiconductor surface. The dose and penetration depth can be controlled through process parameters.

Comparison

Parameter Diffusion Ion implantation
Mechanism Thermal movement of impurity atoms Accelerated dopant ions enter the material
Temperature High processing temperature Implantation can be performed at relatively low substrate temperature
Control Controlled by time, temperature and concentration Precise dose and depth control
Damage Generally no implantation damage Can create crystal damage requiring annealing
Placement point: Ion implantation is widely used in modern semiconductor fabrication because it provides precise control over dopant dose and spatial placement.

6. Donor and Acceptor Impurities

6.1 Donor impurities

A donor impurity is a pentavalent atom introduced into a silicon or germanium crystal. It has five valence electrons.

Four electrons participate in covalent bonding with neighboring semiconductor atoms. The fifth electron is relatively weakly bound and can be thermally excited into the conduction band.

Common donor impurities include phosphorus, arsenic and antimony.

6.2 Acceptor impurities

An acceptor impurity is a trivalent atom introduced into a silicon or germanium crystal. It has three valence electrons.

It can form three covalent bonds with neighboring atoms. The fourth bond is incomplete, creating an acceptor state that can accept an electron from the valence band. This process leaves a hole in the valence band.

Common acceptor impurities include boron, aluminium and gallium.

Feature Donor impurity Acceptor impurity
Valence electrons 5 3
Typical examples Phosphorus, arsenic Boron, gallium
Primary effect Provides electrons Creates holes
Semiconductor type N-type P-type

7. N-Type Semiconductor

An N-type semiconductor is formed by doping pure silicon or germanium with pentavalent donor impurities.

The additional donor electron can become a mobile electron, so electrons are the majority carriers and holes are the minority carriers.

Characteristics

  • Formed using pentavalent impurities.
  • Electrons are majority carriers.
  • Holes are minority carriers.
  • Donor atoms become positively charged ionized donors after donating electrons.
  • The material remains electrically neutral overall.

N-type silicon lattice

Conceptual illustration of a pentavalent donor atom. Four of its valence electrons participate in covalent bonding, while the fifth can become a mobile electron.

The diagram is a simplified bonding illustration. A real dopant atom's electronic states and ionization behavior are determined by the semiconductor's band structure.

8. P-Type Semiconductor

A P-type semiconductor is formed by doping pure silicon or germanium with trivalent acceptor impurities.

The acceptor impurity can accept an electron from a neighboring bond, leaving a hole. Therefore, holes are the majority carriers and electrons are the minority carriers.

Characteristics

  • Formed using trivalent impurities.
  • Holes are majority carriers.
  • Electrons are minority carriers.
  • Ionized acceptor atoms are negatively charged.
  • The material remains electrically neutral overall.

P-type silicon lattice

Conceptual illustration of a trivalent acceptor atom. The incomplete bonding state can accept an electron, leaving a hole in the valence band.

The hole symbol represents a mobile valence-band vacancy, not a hole permanently fixed at the boron atom.

9. Majority and Minority Carriers

In an extrinsic semiconductor, one type of carrier has a much higher concentration than the other at ordinary operating conditions.

Semiconductor type Majority carrier Minority carrier
N-type Electrons Holes
P-type Holes Electrons

Important distinction

N-type does not mean the semiconductor has a net negative charge, and P-type does not mean it has a net positive charge. Both materials are electrically neutral overall when all mobile carriers and ionized dopants are considered.

Interview point: N-type and P-type describe the dominant mobile carrier type, not the overall electrical charge of the semiconductor material.

10. Mass-Action Law

At thermal equilibrium, the product of electron concentration and hole concentration in a semiconductor is equal to the square of its intrinsic carrier concentration.

np = ni²
n = Electron concentration
p = Hole concentration
ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration

For an N-type semiconductor

If donor concentration is sufficiently high compared with intrinsic carrier concentration and donors are fully ionized, electron concentration is approximately equal to donor concentration.

n ≈ ND
p = ni² / n

For a P-type semiconductor

If acceptor concentration is sufficiently high compared with intrinsic carrier concentration and acceptors are fully ionized, hole concentration is approximately equal to acceptor concentration.

p ≈ NA
n = ni² / p
These approximations assume thermal equilibrium, non-degenerate doping and negligible compensation by other dopants. For compensated or heavily doped materials, charge neutrality and more complete semiconductor statistics may be required.

11. Fermi Level in Intrinsic and Extrinsic Semiconductors

The Fermi level is an energy level associated with the electron occupancy probability in a semiconductor at thermal equilibrium.

Intrinsic semiconductor

For an ideal intrinsic semiconductor, the Fermi level lies near the middle of the band gap. In a real semiconductor, it may be slightly displaced from the exact middle because electron and hole effective densities of states can differ.

N-type semiconductor

Donor doping shifts the Fermi level toward the conduction band.

P-type semiconductor

Acceptor doping shifts the Fermi level toward the valence band.

Conceptual equilibrium energy diagrams. Ec is the conduction-band edge, Ev is the valence-band edge, Ei is the intrinsic Fermi level, and EF is the Fermi level. Diagrams are not to scale.

The Fermi level is not necessarily a physical energy state occupied by an electron. It is a reference energy used to describe occupancy probability.

12. Conductivity of Extrinsic Semiconductors

The conductivity of a semiconductor depends on both carrier concentration and carrier mobility.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Where:

  • q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • n = Electron concentration
  • p = Hole concentration
  • μn = Electron mobility
  • μp = Hole mobility

In an N-type semiconductor, the electron contribution is usually dominant. In a P-type semiconductor, the hole contribution is usually dominant.

For N-type material when electrons dominate:
σ ≈ qnμn

For P-type material when holes dominate:
σ ≈ qpμp
Increasing dopant concentration generally increases majority carrier concentration, but very heavy doping can reduce carrier mobility due to increased impurity scattering.

13. Solved Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Minority Carrier Concentration in N-Type Silicon

An N-type silicon sample has an electron concentration of 5 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³. Assume its intrinsic carrier concentration at the specified temperature is 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Calculate its hole concentration at thermal equilibrium.

Given:

  • n = 5 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
np = ni²
p = ni² / n
p = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (5 × 10¹⁵)
p = 1 × 10²⁰ / 5 × 10¹⁵
p = 2 × 10⁴ cm⁻³

Answer: Hole concentration = 2 × 10⁴ cm⁻³.

Problem 2: Minority Electron Concentration in P-Type Silicon

A P-type silicon sample has a hole concentration of 2 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. Assume ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³. Calculate the minority electron concentration.

n = ni² / p
n = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (2 × 10¹⁶)
n = 1 × 10²⁰ / 2 × 10¹⁶
n = 5 × 10³ cm⁻³

Answer: Electron concentration = 5 × 10³ cm⁻³.

Problem 3: Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

An intrinsic semiconductor has an electron concentration of 8 × 10⁹ cm⁻³. Find its hole concentration.

n = p = ni
p = 8 × 10⁹ cm⁻³

Answer: Hole concentration = 8 × 10⁹ cm⁻³.

Problem 4: Conductivity of N-Type Silicon

An N-type silicon sample has:

  • Electron concentration n = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • Electron mobility μn = 1350 cm²/V·s
  • Hole contribution is negligible.

Calculate its approximate conductivity.

σ ≈ qnμn
σ = (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹)(1 × 10¹⁶)(1350)
σ = 2.1627 S/cm

Answer: Approximately 2.16 S/cm.

The calculation assumes the stated mobility remains valid at the given doping level. Actual mobility depends on doping, temperature and material quality.

14. Practical Applications of Doping

1. PN Junction Diodes

A PN junction is formed by creating adjacent P-type and N-type regions in the same semiconductor crystal. Doping controls the carrier concentrations on both sides of the junction.

2. Bipolar Junction Transistors

BJTs use differently doped emitter, base and collector regions. The doping profiles influence current gain, carrier injection and device performance.

3. MOSFETs

Doping is used to create source and drain regions and to adjust the electrical properties of the channel and substrate.

4. Integrated Circuits

Doping allows semiconductor manufacturers to create the different electrically active regions needed for transistors, resistors, diodes and other integrated components.

5. Embedded Electronics

Microcontrollers, ADCs, memory devices and power-management ICs contain semiconductor structures whose electrical characteristics depend on controlled doping.

15. Common Placement Interview Questions

Q1. What is an intrinsic semiconductor?

A pure semiconductor without intentional impurity doping.

Q2. What is an extrinsic semiconductor?

A semiconductor whose electrical properties are modified by introducing controlled impurity atoms.

Q3. What is doping?

The controlled addition of impurity atoms to a semiconductor to modify its carrier concentration and conductivity.

Q4. Why is doping required?

It provides control over carrier concentration and allows the fabrication of useful semiconductor devices.

Q5. What are the two types of extrinsic semiconductors?

N-type and P-type semiconductors.

Q6. What is an N-type semiconductor?

A semiconductor doped with donor impurities in which electrons are the majority carriers.

Q7. What is a P-type semiconductor?

A semiconductor doped with acceptor impurities in which holes are the majority carriers.

Q8. Name two pentavalent donor impurities.

Phosphorus and arsenic.

Q9. Name two trivalent acceptor impurities.

Boron and gallium.

Q10. What are majority carriers in N-type material?

Electrons.

Q11. What are majority carriers in P-type material?

Holes.

Q12. Are N-type and P-type materials electrically charged overall?

No. They are electrically neutral overall under ordinary equilibrium conditions.

Q13. State the mass-action law.

At thermal equilibrium, np = ni².

Q14. Where is the Fermi level in an N-type semiconductor?

It shifts toward the conduction band compared with intrinsic material.

Q15. Where is the Fermi level in a P-type semiconductor?

It shifts toward the valence band compared with intrinsic material.

Q16. What is the main difference between diffusion and ion implantation?

Diffusion uses thermal motion of dopant atoms, whereas ion implantation accelerates dopant ions into the semiconductor.

Q17. Can an intrinsic semiconductor conduct electricity?

Yes. Thermal generation creates electron-hole pairs, and both types of carriers contribute to conduction.

Q18. What happens to minority carrier concentration when majority carrier concentration increases at constant temperature?

It decreases according to the mass-action law, assuming thermal equilibrium.

Q19. What is the main role of donor impurities?

They supply electrons to the semiconductor conduction band.

Q20. What is the main role of acceptor impurities?

They accept electrons from valence-band states, producing holes.

18. Important Revision Points

  • Intrinsic semiconductor means pure semiconductor without intentional doping.
  • Extrinsic semiconductor means intentionally doped semiconductor.
  • Doping modifies carrier concentration.
  • Pentavalent impurities are donors.
  • Trivalent impurities are acceptors.
  • N-type material has electrons as majority carriers.
  • P-type material has holes as majority carriers.
  • Both N-type and P-type materials remain electrically neutral overall.
  • At equilibrium, np = ni².
  • Donor doping moves the Fermi level toward the conduction band.
  • Acceptor doping moves the Fermi level toward the valence band.
  • Diffusion and ion implantation are important doping techniques.
