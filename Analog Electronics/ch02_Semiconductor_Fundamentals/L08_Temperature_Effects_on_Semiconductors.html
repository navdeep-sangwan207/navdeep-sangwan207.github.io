Lecture 8: Temperature Effects on Semiconductors

Analog Electronics | Semiconductor Fundamentals | NS Learning Hub

1. Introduction

Temperature has a significant influence on semiconductor materials because the electrical behavior of a semiconductor depends on the availability and movement of charge carriers.

When temperature changes, several semiconductor properties are affected:

  • Generation of electron-hole pairs.
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration.
  • Electron and hole mobility.
  • Electrical conductivity and resistivity.
  • PN junction characteristics.
  • Leakage current and transistor operating point.
Placement concept: Unlike most metallic conductors, the resistance of a semiconductor generally decreases as temperature increases in its intrinsic operating region. However, the behavior depends on whether the semiconductor is in the freeze-out, extrinsic, or intrinsic region.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the effect of temperature on semiconductor energy bands.
  • Explain intrinsic carrier concentration variation with temperature.
  • Understand temperature-dependent mobility and conductivity.
  • Compare freeze-out, extrinsic, and intrinsic regions.
  • Analyze temperature effects on diodes and BJTs.
  • Solve numerical problems involving carrier concentration and conductivity.
  • Identify temperature-related faults in electronic circuits.

2. Temperature and Electron-Hole Pair Generation

At very low temperatures, most valence electrons remain bound in their covalent bonds. As temperature increases, thermal energy can excite some electrons from the valence band into the conduction band.

When an electron moves into the conduction band, it leaves behind a hole in the valence band. This creates an electron-hole pair.

Conceptual illustration of thermal generation of an electron-hole pair.

Important Observations

  • Increasing temperature increases thermal energy.
  • More electrons can cross the bandgap.
  • The number of free electrons and holes increases.
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration rises strongly with temperature.
Thermal generation: Valence-band electron → Conduction-band electron + Hole
An electron-hole pair is generated together. Therefore, in an intrinsic semiconductor at thermal equilibrium, electron and hole concentrations remain equal.

3. Effect of Temperature on Energy Bandgap

The energy bandgap is the energy difference between the conduction-band edge and the valence-band edge.

For most common semiconductors, the bandgap decreases as temperature increases. The semiconductor crystal lattice expands and electron-phonon interactions change the electronic energy levels.

Eg = EC − EV

Varshni Equation

The temperature dependence of the bandgap can be approximated using the Varshni equation:

Eg(T) = Eg(0) − [αT² / (T + β)]

Where:

  • Eg(T): Bandgap at temperature T.
  • Eg(0): Extrapolated bandgap at 0 K.
  • α and β: Material-dependent constants.
  • T: Absolute temperature in kelvin.
Conceptual trend only; the exact curve depends on the semiconductor material.
Interview point: The bandgap of silicon decreases as temperature increases. This contributes to the increase in intrinsic carrier concentration.

4. Temperature Dependence of Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

Intrinsic carrier concentration (ni) is the equilibrium concentration of electrons and holes in a pure semiconductor.

For a non-degenerate semiconductor, it is expressed as:

ni = √(NCNV) exp[−Eg/(2kT)]

Where:

  • NC: Effective density of states in the conduction band.
  • NV: Effective density of states in the valence band.
  • Eg: Semiconductor bandgap in joules or electron-volts, consistent with k.
  • k: Boltzmann constant.
  • T: Absolute temperature in kelvin.

Since the effective density of states varies approximately as T3/2, a commonly used temperature-dependent form is:

ni(T) ∝ T3/2 exp[−Eg(T)/(2kT)]
The exponential term makes intrinsic carrier concentration highly temperature-sensitive. The bandgap itself also changes with temperature.

Mass-Action Law

For a semiconductor at thermal equilibrium:

np = ni2

For an intrinsic semiconductor:

n = p = ni

As temperature increases, ni increases. In an intrinsic semiconductor, both electron and hole concentrations increase together.

Approximate Silicon Reference

Property Approximate value at 300 K
Temperature 27°C
Intrinsic carrier concentration About 1.0 × 1010 cm−3
Bandgap About 1.12 eV
Thermal voltage (kT/q) About 25.85 mV
These are commonly used approximate silicon values. Actual values depend on the material, temperature, material parameters, and reference data.

5. Three Temperature Regions of a Doped Semiconductor

A doped semiconductor does not behave identically at every temperature. Its behavior is commonly divided into three regions.

5.1 Freeze-Out Region

  • Occurs at sufficiently low temperatures.
  • Many dopant atoms are not thermally ionized.
  • Only a small number of free carriers are available.
  • Carrier concentration is strongly dependent on temperature.
  • Conductivity is generally low.

5.2 Extrinsic Region

  • Dopant atoms are substantially ionized.
  • Majority carrier concentration is approximately controlled by doping.
  • For uncompensated N-type material, n is approximately ND.
  • For uncompensated P-type material, p is approximately NA.
  • Mobility generally decreases with increasing temperature in this region because of increased lattice scattering.

5.3 Intrinsic Region

  • At sufficiently high temperatures, thermally generated carriers become dominant.
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration becomes comparable to or larger than the dopant-controlled majority carrier concentration.
  • Both electron and hole concentrations increase significantly.
  • Conductivity generally rises strongly with temperature.
  • The semiconductor begins to lose its strongly dopant-controlled behavior.
Conceptual illustration of the transition between temperature regions; boundaries depend on doping and material.
Region Carrier behavior Dominant effect
Freeze-out Incomplete dopant ionization Carrier concentration increases with temperature
Extrinsic Dopants control majority carriers Mobility generally decreases with temperature
Intrinsic Thermal generation dominates Carrier concentration rises strongly
Important: Do not state that semiconductor resistance always decreases with temperature without specifying the operating region. The freeze-out, extrinsic, and intrinsic regions have different dominant mechanisms.

6. Effect of Temperature on Carrier Mobility

Mobility describes how easily electrons or holes move through a semiconductor under an applied electric field.

μ = vd/E

Where:

  • μ: Carrier mobility.
  • vd: Drift velocity.
  • E: Applied electric field.

Scattering Mechanisms

Carrier mobility is influenced by several scattering mechanisms:

  1. Lattice or phonon scattering: Increased lattice vibration at higher temperature causes more frequent carrier scattering.
  2. Ionized impurity scattering: Charged dopant atoms deflect moving carriers. This can be particularly important at lower temperatures.

Temperature Trend

Condition Typical mobility behavior
Increasing temperature in a phonon-scattering-dominated range Mobility decreases
Low-temperature impurity-scattering-dominated range Mobility may increase as temperature rises
High-temperature operation Phonon scattering generally becomes important
A simplified engineering approximation in a phonon-scattering-dominated range is μ ∝ T−m, where m is a positive, material- and condition-dependent exponent. It is not a universal law across all temperature ranges.

7. Effect of Temperature on Conductivity and Resistivity

Semiconductor conductivity depends on both the concentration and mobility of electrons and holes.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Where:

  • σ: Electrical conductivity in S/cm when concentrations are in cm−3 and mobility is in cm²/(V·s).
  • q: Magnitude of electron charge = 1.602 × 10−19 C.
  • n: Electron concentration.
  • p: Hole concentration.
  • μn: Electron mobility.
  • μp: Hole mobility.
ρ = 1/σ

The overall temperature effect depends on which factor dominates: carrier concentration or carrier mobility.

Intrinsic Semiconductor

As temperature increases, intrinsic carrier concentration increases strongly. This effect generally dominates the reduction in mobility. Therefore:

Intrinsic semiconductor: Conductivity generally increases and resistivity generally decreases as temperature rises.

Extrinsic Semiconductor

In the normal extrinsic region, the majority carrier concentration is approximately fixed by the dopant concentration. As temperature rises, mobility generally decreases due to lattice scattering.

Extrinsic semiconductor: Conductivity can decrease and resistivity can increase with temperature in a phonon-scattering-dominated range. This trend is not universal across freeze-out or intrinsic regions.

8. Semiconductor vs Metallic Conductor

Parameter Metallic conductor Semiconductor in intrinsic region
Temperature increases More lattice vibrations More thermal carrier generation
Carrier concentration Approximately constant Increases strongly
Mobility Generally decreases Generally decreases at high temperature
Overall conductivity Generally decreases Generally increases
Overall resistance Generally increases Generally decreases
The comparison refers to the intrinsic semiconductor region. A doped semiconductor in its extrinsic region can show a different temperature coefficient.

9. Temperature Effects on a PN Junction Diode

Temperature changes the electrical characteristics of a PN junction diode. Important effects include forward voltage, reverse leakage current, carrier generation, and reverse breakdown behavior.

9.1 Forward Voltage

For a silicon diode operating at a specified forward current, the forward voltage typically decreases as temperature increases.

Approximate silicon diode temperature coefficient: −2 mV/°C

This is a common engineering rule of thumb near ordinary operating conditions. The actual coefficient depends on diode type, current, temperature, and operating conditions.

Example

A silicon diode has a forward voltage of 0.70 V at 25°C. Estimate its forward voltage at 75°C using a temperature coefficient of −2 mV/°C.

ΔT = 75 − 25 = 50°C
ΔVF = −2 × 50 = −100 mV = −0.10 V
Estimated forward voltage = 0.70 − 0.10 = 0.60 V

9.2 Reverse Leakage Current

Reverse leakage current generally increases significantly with temperature because thermally generated minority carriers become more numerous.

A commonly used rough approximation for some silicon diodes is that reverse leakage current may approximately double for every 10°C rise. This is only a rule of thumb, not a universal diode specification.

Reverse leakage current must be checked against the diode datasheet. It can vary considerably between device types and operating conditions.

9.3 Reverse Breakdown

Temperature also influences reverse breakdown voltage:

  • Zener-dominated breakdown: Often has a negative temperature coefficient.
  • Avalanche-dominated breakdown: Often has a positive temperature coefficient.
  • Near the transition region: The coefficient may be small or change with operating current.
Diode characteristic Typical temperature effect
Forward voltage at fixed current Decreases
Reverse leakage current Increases
Zener-dominated breakdown voltage Often decreases
Avalanche-dominated breakdown voltage Often increases

10. Temperature Effects on a Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)

A BJT is sensitive to temperature because its base-emitter junction, collector current, leakage current, and current gain can change with temperature.

10.1 Base-Emitter Voltage (VBE)

For a silicon BJT operating at approximately constant collector current, VBE generally decreases as junction temperature increases.

Typical VBE temperature coefficient: approximately −2 mV/°C

10.2 Collector Current

Temperature can increase collector current through several mechanisms:

  • Base-emitter voltage decreases at a fixed collector current.
  • Leakage currents increase.
  • Current gain β may change with temperature.
  • The collector current can rise if the bias network does not provide sufficient stabilization.

10.3 Thermal Runaway

Thermal runaway is a condition in which an increase in transistor temperature causes an increase in collector current, which produces additional power dissipation and further increases temperature.

Simplified positive-feedback cycle associated with thermal runaway.

10.4 Methods to Reduce Thermal Runaway

  • Use emitter resistance for negative feedback.
  • Use a properly designed voltage-divider bias network.
  • Provide adequate heat sinking.
  • Maintain the transistor within its safe operating area.
  • Use thermal compensation where required.
  • Ensure proper PCB copper area and thermal design.
Placement concept: Emitter resistance improves thermal stability because an increase in emitter current increases the voltage drop across the emitter resistor, reducing the effective base-emitter voltage.

11. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

A pure silicon semiconductor has an intrinsic carrier concentration of 1 × 1010 cm−3 at 300 K. Find the electron and hole concentrations.

Solution:

For an intrinsic semiconductor:

n = p = ni
n = 1 × 1010 cm−3
p = 1 × 1010 cm−3

Numerical 2: Minority Carrier Concentration in N-Type Silicon

An N-type silicon semiconductor has:

  • Donor concentration ND = 1 × 1016 cm−3
  • Intrinsic concentration ni = 1 × 1010 cm−3

Assuming complete ionization and an uncompensated semiconductor, calculate the minority hole concentration.

Solution:
n ≈ ND = 1 × 1016 cm−3
Using the mass-action law:
p = ni2/n
p = (1 × 1010)² / (1 × 1016)
p = 1 × 104 cm−3

Numerical 3: Conductivity of Intrinsic Silicon

Given:

  • n = p = 1 × 1010 cm−3
  • μn = 1350 cm²/(V·s)
  • μp = 480 cm²/(V·s)
  • q = 1.6 × 10−19 C

Calculate conductivity.

Solution:
σ = q(nμn + pμp)
Substituting:
σ = 1.6 × 10−19 [(1 × 1010 × 1350) + (1 × 1010 × 480)]
σ = 1.6 × 10−19 × 1.83 × 1013
σ = 2.928 × 10−6 S/cm

Numerical 4: Resistivity from Conductivity

If semiconductor conductivity is 2.928 × 10−6 S/cm, calculate its resistivity.

Solution:
ρ = 1/σ
ρ = 1/(2.928 × 10−6)
ρ ≈ 3.415 × 105 Ω·cm

Numerical 5: Temperature Change of Diode Forward Voltage

A silicon diode has a forward voltage of 0.72 V at 25°C. Estimate its forward voltage at 65°C using −2 mV/°C.

Solution:
ΔT = 65 − 25 = 40°C
ΔVF = −2 × 40 = −80 mV
VF,new = 0.72 − 0.08 = 0.64 V

Numerical 6: Thermal Voltage

Calculate the thermal voltage at 350 K.

Given:

  • k = 1.38 × 10−23 J/K
  • T = 350 K
  • q = 1.602 × 10−19 C
Solution:
VT = kT/q
VT = (1.38 × 10−23 × 350) / (1.602 × 10−19)
VT ≈ 0.03015 V = 30.15 mV

12. Practical Applications and Troubleshooting

12.1 Temperature Effects in Electronic Equipment

  • Power supplies: Semiconductor junction temperatures affect diode drops, leakage, and regulator behavior.
  • Amplifiers: BJT bias points can shift with temperature.
  • Switching circuits: Increased junction temperature can affect leakage and switching performance.
  • Sensor circuits: Temperature-dependent semiconductor properties can influence measurement accuracy.
  • Automotive electronics: Semiconductor devices must operate over specified temperature ranges.

12.2 Common Fault Symptoms

Observed symptom Possible temperature-related cause Inspection
Transistor current rises after warming Insufficient bias stabilization Measure bias voltage and collector current
Diode reverse leakage increases Higher junction temperature Check reverse current against datasheet limits
Amplifier output drifts Bias or device parameter variation Monitor DC operating point during warm-up
Device becomes excessively hot Excessive power dissipation or inadequate cooling Check supply, load, heat sink and operating area
Intermittent operation after heating Thermal stress or temperature-sensitive component fault Inspect thermal behavior and solder connections

12.3 Practical Testing Procedure

  1. Identify the semiconductor device and obtain its datasheet.
  2. Record the ambient temperature and supply voltage.
  3. Measure the initial DC operating point.
  4. Operate the circuit within its rated limits.
  5. Monitor device temperature using a suitable temperature sensor or thermocouple.
  6. Record changes in diode forward voltage or transistor bias voltage.
  7. Compare readings with datasheet specifications.
  8. Switch off the circuit if temperature, current, or power exceeds safe limits.
Do not heat a semiconductor directly with a flame or uncontrolled heat source. Use a controlled, low-temperature method and remain within the device's rated operating range. Never touch an energized circuit to estimate its temperature.

12.4 Suggested Laboratory Activity

Objective: Observe how temperature affects the forward voltage of a silicon diode.

Required components:

  • Silicon diode such as 1N4148, subject to its datasheet ratings.
  • Current-limited DC supply.
  • Series current-limiting resistor.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Temperature sensor or thermocouple.

Procedure:

  1. Connect the diode in forward bias with a series resistor.
  2. Set a safe, fixed forward current.
  3. Record the diode forward voltage at room temperature.
  4. Allow the diode temperature to change gradually using a controlled method.
  5. Keep the current approximately constant and record the forward voltage at each temperature.
  6. Plot forward voltage against temperature.

Expected observation:

  • Forward voltage generally decreases as junction temperature increases at fixed current.
  • The measured temperature coefficient may differ from the approximate −2 mV/°C rule.
For meaningful results, measure the actual diode temperature rather than assuming it equals the surrounding air temperature. Keep the current constant during comparison.

13. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What happens to a semiconductor when temperature increases?
Thermal energy generates more electron-hole pairs. Intrinsic carrier concentration increases, while carrier mobility generally decreases in a phonon-scattering-dominated range.
Q2. Why does intrinsic carrier concentration increase with temperature?
More electrons gain sufficient thermal energy to move from the valence band to the conduction band, generating electron-hole pairs.
Q3. What is the approximate intrinsic carrier concentration of silicon at 300 K?
A commonly used approximate value is 1 × 1010 cm−3.
Q4. What is the effect of temperature on the semiconductor bandgap?
The bandgap of most common semiconductors decreases as temperature increases.
Q5. What is the freeze-out region?
It is the low-temperature region in which many dopant atoms are not ionized, resulting in a low free-carrier concentration.
Q6. What is the extrinsic region?
It is the temperature range in which dopant atoms largely control the majority carrier concentration.
Q7. What is the intrinsic region?
It is the high-temperature region in which thermally generated carriers become dominant over dopant-controlled carriers.
Q8. How does carrier mobility change with temperature?
Mobility generally decreases with increasing temperature when lattice or phonon scattering dominates. At low temperatures, impurity scattering can produce a different trend.
Q9. Why does semiconductor resistance generally decrease at high temperature?
In the intrinsic region, the increase in carrier concentration generally dominates the reduction in mobility, increasing conductivity.
Q10. Does semiconductor resistance always decrease with temperature?
No. In the extrinsic region, mobility reduction can dominate and resistivity may increase. Behavior also differs in the freeze-out region.
Q11. State the conductivity equation of a semiconductor.
σ = q(nμn + pμp).
Q12. What is the mass-action law?
At thermal equilibrium, np = ni2.
Q13. What happens to the forward voltage of a silicon diode when temperature increases?
At a fixed forward current, its forward voltage generally decreases. A common approximate coefficient is −2 mV/°C.
Q14. What happens to diode reverse leakage current with temperature?
It generally increases due to increased thermal generation of carriers.
Q15. What is the typical temperature coefficient of silicon BJT VBE?
Approximately −2 mV/°C at a specified collector current, as an engineering rule of thumb.
Q16. What is thermal runaway?
It is a positive-feedback condition in which rising temperature increases current and power dissipation, causing further heating.
Q17. How can thermal runaway be reduced in a BJT circuit?
Use emitter resistance, a stable bias network, appropriate heat sinking, and thermal compensation where necessary.
Q18. What is the thermal voltage at room temperature?
Approximately 25.85 mV at 300 K.
Q19. What is the difference between thermal generation and recombination?
Thermal generation creates electron-hole pairs by exciting electrons into the conduction band. Recombination occurs when a conduction-band electron fills a hole.
Q20. Why is temperature compensation important in analog circuits?
It reduces changes in bias current, operating point, gain, and output caused by temperature-dependent semiconductor parameters.
Q21. Why can a transistor draw more current after warming up?
Changes in VBE, leakage current, and current gain can increase collector current if the bias circuit does not provide adequate stabilization.
Q22. Which region has the highest influence of intrinsic carriers?
The intrinsic region at sufficiently high temperature.
Q23. What is the relationship between conductivity and resistivity?
They are reciprocals: ρ = 1/σ.
Q24. What is the effect of temperature on the bandgap of silicon?
It decreases as temperature rises, contributing to increased intrinsic carrier concentration.
Q25. Why must semiconductor datasheets specify operating temperature?
Because electrical parameters, leakage, power dissipation, reliability, and safe operating limits depend on junction temperature.

15. Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Assuming that semiconductor resistance always decreases with temperature.
  • Ignoring the difference between freeze-out, extrinsic, and intrinsic regions.
  • Assuming carrier mobility always increases with temperature.
  • Confusing intrinsic carrier concentration with dopant concentration.
  • Using Celsius directly in equations requiring absolute temperature.
  • Applying the approximate −2 mV/°C diode rule as an exact value for every diode and current.
  • Assuming every reverse breakdown mechanism has the same temperature coefficient.
  • Ignoring thermal runaway and maximum junction temperature in transistor design.
