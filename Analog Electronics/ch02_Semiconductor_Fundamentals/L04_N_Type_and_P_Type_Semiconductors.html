2. Introduction to Doped Semiconductors

A pure semiconductor such as silicon has relatively low electrical conductivity at room temperature. Its conductivity can be increased and controlled by intentionally adding a small quantity of impurity atoms.

This controlled process is called doping.

Doping produces two important categories of extrinsic semiconductors:

  • N-type semiconductor: Formed by adding donor impurities.
  • P-type semiconductor: Formed by adding acceptor impurities.
Doping does not normally make the semiconductor positively or negatively charged overall. The material remains electrically neutral because the mobile carriers are balanced by fixed ionized impurity atoms.

3. N-Type Semiconductor

3.1 Formation of N-Type Material

An N-type semiconductor is formed by doping pure silicon or germanium with a pentavalent impurity. A pentavalent atom has five valence electrons.

Common pentavalent donor impurities include:

  • Phosphorus (P)
  • Arsenic (As)
  • Antimony (Sb)

Silicon has four valence electrons. When a pentavalent atom replaces a silicon atom in the crystal lattice, four of its electrons form covalent bonds with neighbouring silicon atoms. Its fifth electron is weakly bound and can become a free conduction electron.

Simplified lattice illustration: four bonding electrons are shown around the donor site; the fifth electron is available for conduction.

3.2 Why Is It Called N-Type?

The fifth electron supplied by the donor atom can become a free electron. Therefore, electrons are the majority charge carriers. The letter N refers to the negative charge of the dominant mobile carriers, not to a net negative charge of the entire crystal.

3.3 Charge Carriers in N-Type Material

Carrier Role
Electrons Majority carriers
Holes Minority carriers

3.4 Donor Energy Level

Donor impurities introduce an energy level slightly below the conduction band. At room temperature, many donor electrons can acquire enough thermal energy to enter the conduction band.

Conceptual energy-band diagram. The donor level lies close to the conduction band; the exact Fermi-level position depends on doping and temperature.

4. P-Type Semiconductor

4.1 Formation of P-Type Material

A P-type semiconductor is formed by doping pure silicon or germanium with a trivalent impurity. A trivalent atom has three valence electrons.

Common acceptor impurities include:

  • Boron (B)
  • Aluminium (Al)
  • Gallium (Ga)
  • Indium (In)

When a trivalent atom replaces a silicon atom, it forms three covalent bonds with neighbouring silicon atoms. The fourth bond lacks one electron. This absence of an electron is represented as a hole.

Simplified lattice illustration showing a missing electron in one of the covalent bonds.

4.2 Why Is It Called P-Type?

The missing electron in a covalent bond behaves as a positive charge carrier called a hole. Holes are the majority carriers in P-type material.

4.3 Charge Carriers in P-Type Material

Carrier Role
Holes Majority carriers
Electrons Minority carriers

4.4 Acceptor Energy Level

Acceptor impurities introduce an energy level slightly above the valence band. Electrons from the valence band can occupy these acceptor levels, leaving holes in the valence band.

Conceptual energy-band diagram. The acceptor level lies close to the valence band; the Fermi-level position depends on doping and temperature.

Interview point: Donor atoms donate electrons to the conduction band. Acceptor atoms accept electrons from the valence band, creating holes.

6. Majority and Minority Carriers

In a doped semiconductor, one type of charge carrier has a much higher concentration than the other.

  • Majority carriers: The charge carriers present in higher concentration.
  • Minority carriers: The charge carriers present in lower concentration.
Semiconductor Majority Carrier Minority Carrier
N-Type Electrons Holes
P-Type Holes Electrons

Minority carriers are important in semiconductor devices. For example, the reverse leakage current of a PN junction is strongly associated with minority carriers.

7. Carrier Concentration and Mass-Action Law

At thermal equilibrium, the product of electron concentration and hole concentration is related to the intrinsic carrier concentration.

n × p = ni²

Where:

  • n: Electron concentration in cm⁻³
  • p: Hole concentration in cm⁻³
  • ni: Intrinsic carrier concentration in cm⁻³

For silicon at approximately 300 K, a commonly used approximate value is:

ni ≈ 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

7.1 N-Type Approximation

For a non-compensated N-type semiconductor with sufficiently strong donor doping and nearly complete donor ionization:

n ≈ ND
p = ni² / n

7.2 P-Type Approximation

For a non-compensated P-type semiconductor with sufficiently strong acceptor doping and nearly complete acceptor ionization:

p ≈ NA
n = ni² / p
These approximations assume thermal equilibrium, negligible compensation, and a doping and temperature range where the dopants are substantially ionized. They are not universal equations for every doping or temperature condition.

8. Conductivity of N-Type and P-Type Semiconductors

Semiconductor conductivity depends on the concentration and mobility of electrons and holes.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Where:

  • σ = Electrical conductivity in S/cm when concentrations are in cm⁻³ and mobility in cm²/(V·s).
  • q = Electronic charge = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • n = Electron concentration
  • p = Hole concentration
  • μn = Electron mobility
  • μp = Hole mobility

In N-type material, the electron contribution is generally dominant. In P-type material, the hole contribution is generally dominant.

Resistivity: ρ = 1 / σ

Conductivity and resistivity are reciprocal quantities. Higher conductivity means lower resistivity.

9. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Minority Carrier Concentration in N-Type Silicon

Given:

  • Electron concentration, n = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • Intrinsic concentration, ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Find: Hole concentration p.

np = ni²

p = ni² / n

p = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (1 × 10¹⁶)

p = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³

Answer: Minority hole concentration = 10⁴ cm⁻³.

Numerical 2: Minority Carrier Concentration in P-Type Silicon

Given:

  • Hole concentration, p = 5 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
  • Intrinsic concentration, ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Find: Electron concentration n.

n = ni² / p

n = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (5 × 10¹⁵)

n = 2 × 10⁴ cm⁻³

Answer: Minority electron concentration = 2 × 10⁴ cm⁻³.

Numerical 3: Conductivity of N-Type Silicon

Given:

  • n = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • p = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³
  • μn = 1350 cm²/(V·s)
  • μp = 480 cm²/(V·s)
  • q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Find: Conductivity σ.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

σ = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ [(1 × 10¹⁶ × 1350) + (1 × 10⁴ × 480)]

σ ≈ 2.16 S/cm

Answer: Conductivity ≈ 2.16 S/cm.

Numerical 4: Resistivity from Conductivity

Given: Conductivity σ = 2.16 S/cm.

Find: Resistivity ρ.

ρ = 1 / σ

ρ = 1 / 2.16

ρ ≈ 0.463 Ω·cm

Answer: Resistivity ≈ 0.463 Ω·cm.

10. Effect of Temperature on Doped Semiconductors

The carrier concentrations and conductivity of doped semiconductors depend on temperature.

Important Temperature Regions

  1. Freeze-out region: At very low temperatures, many dopant atoms are not ionized. The free-carrier concentration is relatively low.
  2. Extrinsic region: At moderate temperatures, most dopants are ionized. The carrier concentration is mainly determined by the doping level.
  3. Intrinsic region: At sufficiently high temperatures, thermally generated electron-hole pairs become significant and intrinsic carriers can dominate.
Temperature affects both carrier concentration and carrier mobility. Therefore, conductivity does not depend on temperature through carrier concentration alone.

11. Practical Applications

Application Role of Doped Semiconductor
PN Junction Diode Uses adjoining P-type and N-type regions.
BJT Uses differently doped emitter, base and collector regions.
MOSFET Uses doped source, drain and body regions.
Photodiode Uses semiconductor junctions to convert light into electrical current.
Solar Cell Uses a junction to separate light-generated charge carriers.
Integrated Circuits Use controlled doping to create device regions and isolation structures.

12. Practical Troubleshooting and Placement Relevance

12.1 Why Does Doping Increase Conductivity?

Doping increases the number of available mobile charge carriers. A higher carrier concentration generally increases conductivity, although mobility can change with impurity concentration.

12.2 Why Is a Semiconductor Crystal Electrically Neutral?

When donor atoms release electrons, they become positively charged donor ions. The negative electrons balance these positive ions. Similarly, acceptor ions are negatively charged and are balanced by positive holes.

12.3 What Happens if Doping Concentration Increases?

  • The majority-carrier concentration generally increases.
  • Conductivity generally increases.
  • Resistivity generally decreases.
  • Carrier mobility may decrease because of increased impurity scattering.

12.4 Laboratory and Device-Level Relevance

  • Understand why a diode requires P-type and N-type regions.
  • Relate doping concentration to junction behaviour.
  • Understand why reverse leakage current exists.
  • Recognize how semiconductor doping affects transistor operation.

13. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is an N-type semiconductor?
An extrinsic semiconductor formed by adding pentavalent donor impurities to a pure semiconductor. Electrons are its majority carriers.
Q2. What is a P-type semiconductor?
An extrinsic semiconductor formed by adding trivalent acceptor impurities. Holes are its majority carriers.
Q3. Name three donor impurities.
Phosphorus, arsenic and antimony.
Q4. Name three acceptor impurities.
Boron, aluminium and gallium.
Q5. Why is phosphorus used as a donor impurity in silicon?
Phosphorus has five valence electrons. Four participate in covalent bonding, while the fifth can become a conduction electron.
Q6. Why does boron create a hole in silicon?
Boron has three valence electrons, so one of the four covalent bonds around the substituted silicon site lacks an electron.
Q7. What are majority carriers in N-type material?
Electrons.
Q8. What are majority carriers in P-type material?
Holes.
Q9. What are minority carriers?
Charge carriers present in a lower concentration than the majority carriers.
Q10. Is an N-type semiconductor negatively charged?
No. It is electrically neutral overall. Its majority mobile carriers are negatively charged electrons.
Q11. Is a P-type semiconductor positively charged?
No. It is electrically neutral overall. Its majority mobile carriers are positively charged holes.
Q12. What is a donor impurity?
An impurity that supplies electrons to the semiconductor's conduction band.
Q13. What is an acceptor impurity?
An impurity that accepts electrons from the valence band, creating holes.
Q14. Where is the donor energy level located?
Slightly below the conduction band.
Q15. Where is the acceptor energy level located?
Slightly above the valence band.
Q16. What is the mass-action law?
At thermal equilibrium, the product of electron and hole concentrations is equal to the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration: np = ni².
Q17. What happens to minority-carrier concentration when doping increases?
Under the mass-action law at fixed temperature and equilibrium, increasing majority-carrier concentration reduces the minority-carrier concentration.
Q18. Which carrier generally has higher mobility in silicon?
Electrons generally have higher mobility than holes in silicon.
Q19. What is the effect of increased doping on resistivity?
Resistivity generally decreases because carrier concentration increases, although mobility also changes.
Q20. What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors?
An intrinsic semiconductor is ideally pure, whereas an extrinsic semiconductor has intentionally added donor or acceptor impurities.
Q21. Why are minority carriers important in a PN junction?
Minority carriers contribute significantly to reverse leakage current and influence junction switching and transient behaviour.
Q22. What is the Fermi level in an N-type semiconductor?
It is generally closer to the conduction band than in an intrinsic semiconductor.
Q23. What is the Fermi level in a P-type semiconductor?
It is generally closer to the valence band than in an intrinsic semiconductor.
Q24. Can a semiconductor be doped with both donor and acceptor impurities?
Yes. Such material is called a compensated semiconductor. Its net doping depends on the relative donor and acceptor concentrations.
Q25. What is the difference between a donor and a donor ion?
A donor atom is the impurity before donating an electron. After donating an electron, it becomes a positively charged ionized donor.

15. Practical Learning Activities

  1. Draw a silicon lattice containing one phosphorus donor atom. Identify the four covalent bonds and the fifth electron.
  2. Draw a silicon lattice containing one boron acceptor atom. Mark the incomplete bond as a hole.

These activities are primarily diagram-based and calculation-based. Directly measuring doping concentration requires specialized semiconductor characterization equipment; it cannot be determined reliably using an ordinary digital multimeter.
