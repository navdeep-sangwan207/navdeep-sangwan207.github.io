Electrons, Holes and Intrinsic Semiconductors

Understand charge carriers, electron-hole pair generation, intrinsic carrier concentration and semiconductor conductivity.

1. Introduction to Charge Carriers

Electric current in a semiconductor is produced by the movement of charge carriers. Unlike metals, where conduction is mainly due to free electrons, semiconductor conduction involves two types of charge carriers.

  • Electrons
  • Holes

Electrons carry negative electric charge, whereas holes behave as positively charged carriers.

Definition: A charge carrier is a particle or an effective charge state responsible for carrying electric current through a material.

Charge Carriers in Different Materials

Material Main Charge Carriers
Metallic Conductors Electrons
Intrinsic Semiconductors Electrons and Holes
Insulators Very few mobile charge carriers under ordinary conditions

2. Free Electrons in Semiconductors

A free electron is an electron occupying a mobile conduction-band state in a semiconductor.

In an intrinsic semiconductor, an electron can gain sufficient thermal energy to move from the valence band into the conduction band.

Characteristics of Free Electrons

  • They have negative electric charge.
  • Their charge is approximately -1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb.
  • They occupy conduction-band states.
  • They contribute to electrical conduction.
  • They move under the influence of an electric field.
Electron Charge = -1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
Important: In semiconductor physics, the term free electron generally refers to an electron that can move through the crystal in an available conduction-band state.

3. Holes in Semiconductors

A hole is an unoccupied electronic state in the valence band. It is created when an electron leaves an occupied valence-band state.

A hole behaves as a positively charged carrier because the absence of a negatively charged electron is represented as an effective positive charge.

Characteristics of Holes

  • Holes behave as positively charged carriers.
  • Their effective charge is +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulomb.
  • They are associated with unoccupied valence-band states.
  • They contribute to semiconductor electrical conduction.
  • Hole movement is described through successive electron transitions into neighboring vacancies.
Effective Hole Charge = +1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
Important Note: A hole is not a separate physical particle like an electron. It is a useful model representing an unoccupied electron state in the valence band.

4. Electron-Hole Pair Generation

In an intrinsic semiconductor, thermal energy can excite an electron from the valence band into the conduction band.

When this happens, two charge carriers are produced:

  1. A free electron in the conduction band.
  2. A hole in the valence band.

These two carriers are called an electron-hole pair.

Generation Process:

Thermal Energy → Electron Excitation → Free Electron + Hole

Important Characteristics

  • One electron-hole pair is generated by each excitation event.
  • The electron carries negative charge.
  • The hole behaves as a positive charge carrier.
  • The total electric charge is conserved.
  • Both carriers can contribute to electrical conduction.
Placement Point: In an intrinsic semiconductor, electrons and holes are generated in equal numbers.

5. Intrinsic Semiconductor

An intrinsic semiconductor is a chemically pure semiconductor material without intentional impurity doping.

Silicon and germanium are common examples of intrinsic semiconductor materials.

Characteristics

  • It is ideally a pure semiconductor crystal.
  • Conduction occurs through electrons and holes.
  • Electron and hole concentrations are equal.
  • Carrier concentration depends strongly on temperature.
  • At absolute zero, an ideal intrinsic semiconductor has no thermally generated free carriers.

Examples of Intrinsic Semiconductors

Material Symbol Band Gap at Approximately 300 K
Silicon Si 1.12 eV
Germanium Ge 0.66 eV
Note: Intrinsic means that the semiconductor is not intentionally doped. Real semiconductor materials may still contain trace impurities and defects.

6. Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

Intrinsic carrier concentration is the number of thermally generated electrons or holes per unit volume in an intrinsic semiconductor at thermal equilibrium.

It is represented by the symbol ni.

ni = n = p

Where:

  • ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration.
  • n = Electron concentration.
  • p = Hole concentration.

Units

Carrier concentration is commonly expressed in:

  • cm⁻³
  • m⁻³
Example:

Suppose an intrinsic semiconductor has an electron concentration of 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at a particular temperature.

Because it is intrinsic and at thermal equilibrium:

n = p = ni

p = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Therefore, the hole concentration is also 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

7. Electron and Hole Concentration

The electron concentration represents the number of mobile conduction-band electrons per unit volume.

The hole concentration represents the number of holes in the valence band per unit volume.

Intrinsic Semiconductor

n = p = ni

Comparison of Electrons and Holes

Property Electron Hole
Effective Charge Negative Positive
Band Location Conduction Band Valence Band
Symbol for Concentration n p
Movement Electron movement through the crystal Effective movement of an unoccupied state
Intrinsic Concentration ni ni
Remember: Equal electron and hole concentrations are a defining property of an intrinsic semiconductor at thermal equilibrium.

8. Thermal Generation of Electron-Hole Pairs

Thermal generation occurs when electrons receive energy from heat and move from the valence band into the conduction band.

Process

  1. The semiconductor receives thermal energy.
  2. Some valence electrons gain sufficient energy.
  3. These electrons enter conduction-band states.
  4. Holes are left behind in the valence band.
  5. Electron-hole pairs are generated.
Important Relationship:

Temperature Increase → More Thermal Excitation → Higher Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

Thermal generation is one of the important reasons semiconductor conductivity increases as temperature increases.

9. Recombination of Electron-Hole Pairs

Recombination occurs when a conduction-band electron occupies an available hole state in the valence band.

As a result, the free electron and the hole cease to exist as separate mobile charge carriers.

Recombination Process

  1. A free electron exists in the conduction band.
  2. An available hole exists in the valence band.
  3. The electron loses energy and occupies the hole state.
  4. The electron-hole pair is eliminated.
  5. Energy is released to the surroundings or another excitation, depending on the recombination mechanism.
Electron + Hole → Recombination

Common Recombination Mechanisms

Mechanism Description
Radiative Recombination Energy is released as a photon.
Shockley-Read-Hall Recombination Recombination occurs through defect or impurity energy levels.
Auger Recombination Energy is transferred to another charge carrier.
Placement Point: Recombination reduces the number of mobile electron-hole pairs.

10. Thermal Equilibrium in Intrinsic Semiconductors

Thermal equilibrium is the condition in which a semiconductor has a stable temperature and its carrier concentrations remain constant with time.

At thermal equilibrium, the rate of electron-hole pair generation equals the rate of recombination.

Generation Rate = Recombination Rate

Important Characteristics

  • Carrier concentrations remain constant with time.
  • Electron and hole concentrations are equal in an intrinsic semiconductor.
  • Generation and recombination processes continue to occur.
  • There is no net change in the carrier population due to these processes.
Important: Thermal equilibrium does not mean that generation and recombination stop. Both processes continue at equal average rates.

11. Law of Mass Action

For a semiconductor at thermal equilibrium, the product of electron concentration and hole concentration is equal to the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration.

n × p = ni²

Where:

  • n = Electron concentration.
  • p = Hole concentration.
  • ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration.

For an Intrinsic Semiconductor

Since n = p = ni:

ni × ni = ni²

Numerical Example

Consider a semiconductor at thermal equilibrium with:

ni = 2 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Electron concentration:

n = 4 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Using the law of mass action:

np = ni²

p = ni² / n

p = (2 × 10¹⁰)² / (4 × 10¹⁰)

p = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³

Therefore, the hole concentration is 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

Important: The law of mass action applies to a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium under the usual non-degenerate semiconductor conditions.

12. Effect of Temperature on Intrinsic Semiconductors

Temperature has a significant effect on the electrical behavior of intrinsic semiconductors.

As temperature increases, more electrons gain sufficient thermal energy to enter the conduction band.

At Absolute Zero (0 K)

  • An ideal intrinsic semiconductor has a full valence band.
  • The conduction band is empty.
  • There are no thermally generated free electron-hole pairs.
  • Its intrinsic conductivity due to thermal carriers is zero in the ideal model.

At Room Temperature

  • Thermal energy generates electron-hole pairs.
  • Both electrons and holes contribute to current.
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration is greater than zero.
  • Conductivity is higher than at absolute zero.

At Higher Temperature

  • More electron-hole pairs are generated.
  • Intrinsic carrier concentration increases rapidly.
  • Electrical conductivity generally increases.
  • Electrical resistivity generally decreases.
Placement Point: A semiconductor generally has a negative temperature coefficient of resistance over the usual intrinsic conduction range.

13. Intrinsic Conductivity

Electrical conductivity describes how easily a material allows electric current to flow.

In an intrinsic semiconductor, both electrons and holes contribute to conductivity.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Where:

  • σ = Electrical conductivity (S/m).
  • q = Magnitude of electronic charge.
  • n = Electron concentration.
  • p = Hole concentration.
  • μn = Electron mobility.
  • μp = Hole mobility.

For an Intrinsic Semiconductor

Since n = p = ni:

σi = qni(μn + μp)

This equation shows that intrinsic conductivity depends on carrier concentration and carrier mobility.

Resistivity

ρ = 1 / σ

Where ρ represents electrical resistivity, measured in ohm-metre (Ω·m).

Numerical Example:

Given:

  • q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁶ m⁻³
  • μn = 0.14 m²/V·s
  • μp = 0.05 m²/V·s

Calculate intrinsic conductivity.

σi = qni(μn + μp)

σi = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹) (1 × 10¹⁶)(0.14 + 0.05)

σi = 3.04 × 10⁻⁴ S/m

Therefore, intrinsic conductivity is 3.04 × 10⁻⁴ S/m.

14. Intrinsic Fermi Level

The Fermi level is an important energy reference used to describe the probability of electron occupation of available energy states.

In an intrinsic semiconductor, the Fermi level is called the intrinsic Fermi level and is represented by Ei.

Important Characteristics

  • It lies approximately near the middle of the band gap.
  • For an intrinsic semiconductor, electron and hole concentrations are equal.
  • Its exact position can differ slightly from the exact middle due to differences in electron and hole effective density of states.
Intrinsic Semiconductor: n = p
Remember: The intrinsic Fermi level is approximately at the center of the band gap, not necessarily exactly at the center.

15. Applications and Practical Importance

Understanding electrons, holes and intrinsic semiconductor behavior is essential for studying practical semiconductor devices.

Device Importance
PN Junction Diode Electron and hole movement determines junction behavior.
BJT Both electrons and holes participate in transistor operation.
MOSFET Carrier movement in semiconductor channels controls current.
Solar Cell Light generates electron-hole pairs.
Photodiode Incident light generates carriers that produce photocurrent.
Integrated Circuits Carrier behavior forms the basis of semiconductor switching devices.
Practical Connection: The behavior of electrons and holes in intrinsic materials provides the foundation for understanding extrinsic semiconductors, PN junctions and transistors.

16. Important Terms to Remember

Term Meaning
Charge Carrier Particle or effective charge state responsible for carrying current.
Free Electron Electron occupying a mobile conduction-band state.
Hole Unoccupied electron state in the valence band.
Electron-Hole Pair A conduction-band electron and its corresponding valence-band hole.
Intrinsic Semiconductor A semiconductor without intentional impurity doping.
Intrinsic Carrier Concentration Equilibrium electron or hole concentration in an intrinsic semiconductor.
Generation Creation of electron-hole pairs.
Recombination Elimination of an electron-hole pair when an electron fills a hole state.
Thermal Equilibrium Condition in which generation and recombination balance each other.
Mobility Measure of how easily a charge carrier moves under an electric field.
