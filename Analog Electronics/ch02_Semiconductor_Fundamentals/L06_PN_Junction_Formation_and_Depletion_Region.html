2. What Is a PN Junction?

A PN junction is formed when P-type and N-type regions exist adjacent to each other within a semiconductor crystal. It is the basic structure of an ordinary semiconductor diode.

  • P-type region: Contains holes as majority carriers.
  • N-type region: Contains electrons as majority carriers.
  • Junction: The boundary region between P-type and N-type material.
A practical PN junction is normally fabricated by selectively doping different regions of a semiconductor crystal. It is not generally made by simply placing two separate semiconductor pieces together.

3. Before Junction Formation

Consider separate P-type and N-type regions before they are brought into electrical contact within the same crystal.

P-Type Region N-Type Region
Holes are majority carriers. Electrons are majority carriers.
Electrons are minority carriers. Holes are minority carriers.
Contains negatively charged ionized acceptors. Contains positively charged ionized donors.

Conceptual representation of majority carriers in the two regions.

4. Diffusion of Charge Carriers

When a PN junction is formed, there is a large difference in carrier concentration across the junction.

Electrons are highly concentrated on the N-side, while holes are highly concentrated on the P-side.

Due to this concentration gradient:

  • Electrons diffuse from the N-region towards the P-region.
  • Holes diffuse from the P-region towards the N-region.

This movement of carriers due to a concentration gradient is called diffusion.

The arrows show diffusion direction: electrons move N to P; holes move P to N.

Diffusion is caused by a concentration gradient. It does not require an externally applied voltage.

5. Recombination Near the Junction

When electrons diffuse from the N-side into the P-side, they encounter holes. Some electrons recombine with holes.

Similarly, holes diffusing into the N-side can recombine with electrons.

As a result, mobile carriers are removed from a region close to the junction.

  • Mobile electrons near the junction are reduced.
  • Mobile holes near the junction are reduced.
  • Fixed ionized dopant atoms remain in the crystal.

6. Formation of the Depletion Region

The region around the junction that is depleted of most mobile electrons and holes is called the depletion region or depletion layer.

When mobile carriers diffuse and recombine, the fixed ionized dopant atoms are exposed:

  • Ionized acceptor atoms on the P-side carry negative charge.
  • Ionized donor atoms on the N-side carry positive charge.

Simplified cross-section. Negative acceptor ions are on the P-side; positive donor ions are on the N-side.

The depletion region is not completely empty of every charge carrier. It is a region in which mobile carrier concentration is greatly reduced compared with the neutral regions.

7. Space-Charge Region

The fixed positive and negative ions in the depletion region create a region of net electric charge called the space-charge region.

Side Fixed Ion Charge
P-side depletion region Ionized acceptor Negative
N-side depletion region Ionized donor Positive

The space-charge region is responsible for the electric field that opposes further majority-carrier diffusion.

8. Electric Field Across the Depletion Region

The positive donor ions on the N-side and negative acceptor ions on the P-side establish an electric field.

The electric field direction is from the positive donor ions towards the negative acceptor ions:

Electric field direction: N-side → P-side

The electric field points from positive donor ions to negative acceptor ions.

8.1 Effect of the Electric Field

  • It opposes further diffusion of majority carriers.
  • It drives electrons towards the N-side when they are in the depletion region.
  • It drives holes towards the P-side when they are in the depletion region.

This field produces a drift component of carrier movement.

9. Drift and Diffusion at Equilibrium

Two carrier transport mechanisms operate in a PN junction:

Diffusion Current

Caused by a carrier concentration gradient. Majority carriers diffuse across the junction.

Drift Current

Caused by the electric field in the depletion region. The field drives carriers in directions that oppose majority-carrier diffusion.

Diffusion Drift
Driven by concentration gradient. Driven by electric field.
Important for majority-carrier movement during junction formation. Important for carrier movement in the depletion-region field.
Occurs even without an external voltage. Occurs when an electric field is present.
At thermal equilibrium:

Net current = Drift current + Diffusion current = 0
At equilibrium, drift and diffusion components balance for the junction as a whole. This does not mean that individual carriers stop moving.

10. Built-In Potential (Barrier Potential)

The electric field in the depletion region creates an internal potential difference across the junction. This is called the built-in potential or barrier potential.

It opposes the further diffusion of majority carriers.

10.1 Built-In Potential Equation

For an ideal, abrupt, non-degenerate PN junction at thermal equilibrium:

Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NA ND / ni²)

Where:

  • Vbi = Built-in potential in volts
  • k = Boltzmann constant = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K
  • T = Absolute temperature in kelvin
  • q = Electronic charge = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
  • NA = Acceptor concentration in cm⁻³
  • ND = Donor concentration in cm⁻³
  • ni = Intrinsic carrier concentration in cm⁻³

10.2 Thermal Voltage

VT = kT/q

At approximately 300 K:
VT ≈ 25.85 mV
The built-in potential is an internal equilibrium potential. It is not the same as the diode's forward voltage drop measured during operation. A practical diode's forward voltage depends on current, temperature, device material and construction.

11. Solved Numerical Problems

Numerical 1: Calculate Built-In Potential

Given:

  • NA = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • ND = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • T = 300 K
  • VT = 0.02585 V

Find: Built-in potential.

Vbi = VT ln(NA ND / ni²)

Vbi = 0.02585 ln[(10¹⁶ × 10¹⁶)/(10¹⁰)²]

Vbi = 0.02585 ln(10¹²)

Vbi = 0.02585 × 27.631

Vbi ≈ 0.714 V

Answer: Built-in potential ≈ 0.714 V.

Numerical 2: Built-In Potential for Different Doping

Given:

  • NA = 2 × 10¹⁷ cm⁻³
  • ND = 5 × 10¹⁵ cm⁻³
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • VT = 0.02585 V
Vbi = 0.02585 ln[(2 × 10¹⁷ × 5 × 10¹⁵)/(10²⁰)]

Vbi = 0.02585 ln(10¹³)

Vbi = 0.02585 × 29.934

Vbi ≈ 0.774 V

Answer: Built-in potential ≈ 0.774 V.

Numerical 3: Depletion Width at Zero Bias

For an ideal abrupt silicon junction, the total depletion width under the depletion approximation is:

W = √[(2εs/q)(1/NA + 1/ND)Vbi]

Given:

  • NA = ND = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
  • Vbi = 0.714 V
  • εs = 11.7ε0
  • ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹⁴ F/cm
  • q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C

Step 1: Calculate semiconductor permittivity.

εs = 11.7 × 8.854 × 10⁻¹⁴
εs ≈ 1.036 × 10⁻¹² F/cm

Step 2: Substitute values.

W = √[(2 × 1.036 × 10⁻¹² / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) × (2 × 10⁻¹⁶) × 0.714]

W ≈ 4.30 × 10⁻⁵ cm

W ≈ 0.43 μm

Answer: Total depletion width ≈ 0.43 μm.

Numerical 4: Depletion Width Distribution

For the symmetric junction in Numerical 3, NA = ND. The depletion region extends equally into both sides.

xp = xn = W/2

xp = xn = 0.43/2

xp = xn ≈ 0.215 μm

Answer: Depletion width on each side ≈ 0.215 μm.

12. Depletion Width and Doping Concentration

The depletion region does not necessarily extend equally into the P-type and N-type regions.

Charge neutrality requires the total uncovered negative charge on the P-side to balance the total uncovered positive charge on the N-side.

NA xp = ND xn

Where:

  • xp = Depletion width extending into the P-side
  • xn = Depletion width extending into the N-side

12.1 Effect of Unequal Doping

  • If NA = ND, depletion extends equally into both regions.
  • If NA is much greater than ND, depletion extends farther into the N-side.
  • If ND is much greater than NA, depletion extends farther into the P-side.

Conceptual illustration of an asymmetrically doped junction. The lightly doped side accommodates most of the depletion width.

The depletion width is inversely related to doping concentration on each side. The more lightly doped side generally takes a greater share of the depletion region.

13. Depletion Width Equations

For an abrupt junction under the depletion approximation, the total depletion width at applied junction voltage V is:

W = √[(2εs/q)(1/NA + 1/ND)(Vbi − V)]

Here, V is positive for forward bias and negative for reverse bias, using the conventional voltage across the junction.

13.1 Width on P-Side

xp = [ND/(NA + ND)]W

13.2 Width on N-Side

xn = [NA/(NA + ND)]W

13.3 Total Width

W = xp + xn
These equations assume an abrupt junction, uniform doping on each side, and the depletion approximation. Real devices may require more detailed models.

14. Practical Applications of PN Junction Formation

Application Role of PN Junction
Rectifier Diode Allows current to flow preferentially in one direction.
Zener Diode Uses reverse-bias breakdown for voltage regulation.
LED Electron-hole recombination produces light.
Photodiode Converts incident light into electrical current.
Solar Cell Uses a junction field to help separate photogenerated carriers.
BJT Uses two PN junctions to control carrier injection and collection.

15. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a PN junction?
A PN junction is the boundary between P-type and N-type semiconductor regions in a semiconductor crystal.
Q2. How is a practical PN junction fabricated?
It is typically fabricated by introducing different dopants into selected regions of a semiconductor wafer.
Q3. What is diffusion?
Diffusion is carrier movement caused by a concentration gradient.
Q4. In which direction do electrons diffuse during PN junction formation?
From the N-region towards the P-region.
Q5. In which direction do holes diffuse?
From the P-region towards the N-region.
Q6. What is recombination?
Recombination occurs when an electron occupies a hole state, removing a mobile electron-hole pair.
Q7. What is the depletion region?
It is the region around a PN junction where mobile carrier concentration is greatly reduced.
Q8. Why is it called a depletion region?
Because it is depleted of most mobile electrons and holes.
Q9. What charges are present in the depletion region?
Fixed negative acceptor ions on the P-side and fixed positive donor ions on the N-side.
Q10. What is a space-charge region?
It is the region containing the uncovered fixed ionized dopant charges.
Q11. What is the direction of the electric field in a PN junction?
From the N-side towards the P-side.
Q12. What is built-in potential?
It is the internal potential difference established across the depletion region at thermal equilibrium.
Q13. Write the built-in potential equation.
Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NA ND/ni²).
Q14. What is thermal voltage at room temperature?
Approximately 25.85 mV at 300 K.
Q15. Is built-in potential the same as forward voltage drop?
No. Built-in potential is an internal equilibrium potential. Forward voltage drop is measured under an applied forward current and depends on operating conditions.
Q16. What is the relationship between depletion width and doping?
The depletion region extends farther into the more lightly doped side.
Q17. What happens when both sides have equal doping?
The depletion region extends equally into both sides of an ideal abrupt junction.
Q18. What is drift current?
It is current produced by carrier movement under an electric field.
Q19. What is diffusion current?
It is current produced by carrier movement due to a concentration gradient.
Q20. What is the net current in an unbiased PN junction at thermal equilibrium?
Zero. Drift and diffusion current components balance.
Q21. Does a depletion region contain no carriers at all?
No. Its mobile carrier concentration is greatly reduced, but it is not necessarily zero.
Q22. What happens to the depletion width under reverse bias?
It increases.
Q23. What happens to the depletion width under forward bias?
It decreases.
Q24. What is the purpose of the depletion-region electric field?
It opposes majority-carrier diffusion and helps establish equilibrium in the unbiased junction.
Q25. Why is the PN junction important in electronics?
It is the fundamental structure used in diodes, LEDs, photodiodes, solar cells, Zener diodes and bipolar transistors.

18. Quick Revision

  • A PN junction consists of adjacent P-type and N-type regions.
  • Electrons diffuse from N to P during junction formation.
  • Holes diffuse from P to N.
  • Recombination removes mobile carriers near the junction.
  • The depletion region contains fixed ionized dopant atoms.
  • P-side depletion charge is negative.
  • N-side depletion charge is positive.
  • Electric field direction is N to P.
  • Built-in potential opposes majority-carrier diffusion.
  • Drift and diffusion balance at thermal equilibrium.
  • Depletion extends farther into the lightly doped side.
  • Reverse bias increases depletion width.
  • Forward bias decreases depletion width.
