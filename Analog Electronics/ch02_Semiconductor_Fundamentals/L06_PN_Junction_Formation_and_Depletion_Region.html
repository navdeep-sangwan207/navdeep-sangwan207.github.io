1. Introduction to P-Type and N-Type Semiconductors

Extrinsic semiconductors are classified into two main categories depending on the type of impurity atoms introduced into the semiconductor crystal.

N-Type Semiconductor

An N-type semiconductor is produced by adding pentavalent donor impurities to pure silicon or germanium.

Electrons are the majority charge carriers, while holes are the minority charge carriers.

P-Type Semiconductor

A P-type semiconductor is produced by adding trivalent acceptor impurities to pure silicon or germanium.

Holes are the majority charge carriers, while electrons are the minority charge carriers.

2. Formation of N-Type Semiconductor

An N-type semiconductor is formed by introducing a pentavalent impurity into a pure semiconductor crystal.

Example: Silicon Doped with Phosphorus

Silicon has four valence electrons, whereas phosphorus has five valence electrons.

  1. Four phosphorus electrons form covalent bonds with neighboring silicon atoms.
  2. The fifth electron is weakly bound to the phosphorus atom.
  3. Thermal energy can excite this electron into the conduction band.
  4. The phosphorus atom becomes a positively charged donor ion.
  5. The free electron contributes to electrical conduction.

3. Charge Carriers in N-Type Semiconductor

In an N-type semiconductor, the electron concentration is significantly higher than the hole concentration under normal extrinsic operating conditions.

Majority Carriers: Electrons

Free electrons move through the conduction band and contribute to electrical current.

Minority Carriers: Holes

A small number of holes are also present due to thermal generation of electron-hole pairs.

n > p

Where:

  • n = Electron concentration.
  • p = Hole concentration.
Remember: N-type does not mean that the semiconductor has a net negative charge. The material remains electrically neutral overall.

5. Fermi Level in N-Type Semiconductor

The Fermi level is an energy reference used to describe the probability that available electronic states are occupied.

When a semiconductor is doped with donor impurities, its Fermi level shifts toward the conduction band.

Important Points

  • The Fermi level moves upward compared with an intrinsic semiconductor.
  • It moves closer to the conduction band.
  • Higher donor concentration generally moves it closer to the conduction band.
  • At very high doping, the semiconductor may become degenerate.

6. Formation of P-Type Semiconductor

A P-type semiconductor is formed by introducing trivalent impurity atoms into a pure silicon or germanium crystal.

Example: Silicon Doped with Boron

Silicon has four valence electrons, whereas boron has three valence electrons.

  1. Three boron electrons form covalent bonds with neighboring silicon atoms.
  2. One bonding position remains incomplete.
  3. An electron from a neighboring bond can occupy the acceptor state.
  4. This electron transfer leaves a hole in the valence band.
  5. The boron atom becomes a negatively charged acceptor ion.

7. Charge Carriers in P-Type Semiconductor

In a P-type semiconductor, the hole concentration is significantly higher than the electron concentration under normal extrinsic conditions.

Majority Carriers: Holes

Holes move through the valence band when neighboring electrons fill vacant bonding positions.

Minority Carriers: Electrons

A small number of electrons are present due to thermal generation of electron-hole pairs.

p > n

Where:

  • p = Hole concentration.
  • n = Electron concentration.
Remember: P-type does not mean that the semiconductor has a net positive charge. The material remains electrically neutral overall.

10. Movement of Electrons and Holes

Charge carriers move when an electric field is applied across a semiconductor.

Electron Movement

Electrons have negative charge. Their drift direction is opposite to the applied electric field.

Hole Movement

Holes behave as positively charged mobile carriers. Their drift direction is along the applied electric field.

11. Drift Current in P-Type and N-Type Semiconductors

When an electric field is applied, mobile electrons and holes produce drift current.

Electron Drift Current Density

Jn = q × n × μn × E

Hole Drift Current Density

Jp = q × p × μp × E

Where:

  • J = Current density (A/cm²).
  • q = Magnitude of electronic charge (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C).
  • n = Electron concentration (cm⁻³).
  • p = Hole concentration (cm⁻³).
  • μn = Electron mobility (cm²/V·s).
  • μp = Hole mobility (cm²/V·s).
  • E = Electric field (V/cm).

Total Drift Current Density

J = q(nμn + pμp)E
Both electrons and holes contribute to the total conventional current. Their individual current contributions are in the same conventional-current direction under an applied electric field.

12. Electrical Conductivity of Doped Semiconductors

Electrical conductivity describes how easily a material allows electric current to flow.

In a semiconductor, conductivity depends on carrier concentration and carrier mobility.

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Where:

  • σ = Electrical conductivity (S/cm).
  • q = Electronic charge.
  • n = Electron concentration.
  • p = Hole concentration.
  • μn = Electron mobility.
  • μp = Hole mobility.

Conductivity in N-Type Material

Electrons are the majority carriers, so electron conduction usually dominates.

Conductivity in P-Type Material

Holes are the majority carriers, so hole conduction usually dominates.

Important: Electron mobility is generally higher than hole mobility in silicon under comparable conditions. However, total conductivity depends on both mobility and carrier concentration.

13. Carrier Concentration and Law of Mass Action

At thermal equilibrium in a non-degenerate semiconductor:

np = ni²

N-Type Semiconductor

n ≈ ND

p = ni² / n

P-Type Semiconductor

p ≈ NA

n = ni² / p

These approximations assume substantial dopant ionization and negligible compensation.

Example: An N-type semiconductor has:
  • ni = 1 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³
  • n = 1 × 10¹⁶ cm⁻³
Calculate minority carrier concentration.
p = ni² / n

p = (1 × 10¹⁰)² / (1 × 10¹⁶)

p = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³
Answer: Minority carrier concentration = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻³.

14. Effect of Temperature on P-Type and N-Type Semiconductors

Temperature affects the carrier concentration and conductivity of doped semiconductors.

Low-Temperature Region: Freeze-Out

At sufficiently low temperatures, some dopant atoms are not ionized. Fewer free carriers are available.

Moderate-Temperature Region: Extrinsic Region

Most shallow dopants become ionized. Majority carrier concentration is largely controlled by the dopant concentration.

High-Temperature Region: Intrinsic Region

Thermal generation of electron-hole pairs becomes increasingly important. Intrinsic carrier concentration can become comparable to or greater than the doping concentration.

16. Practical Applications

1. PN Junction Diodes

Joining P-type and N-type semiconductor regions creates a PN junction diode used for rectification and switching.

2. Bipolar Junction Transistors

BJTs use alternating P-type and N-type regions to control current amplification and switching.

3. MOSFETs

MOSFETs use doped semiconductor regions to form source, drain and body structures.

4. Integrated Circuits

Different doping regions are used to manufacture transistors, diodes and other semiconductor devices.

5. Solar Cells

P-type and N-type regions form a junction that helps separate photo-generated charge carriers.

6. LED and Photodiode Devices

Doped semiconductor junctions are used in light-emitting and light-detecting devices.
