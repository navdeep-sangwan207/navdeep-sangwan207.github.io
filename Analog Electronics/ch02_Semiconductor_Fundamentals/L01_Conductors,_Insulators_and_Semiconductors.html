Lecture 1: Conductors, Insulators and Semiconductors

Understand how the atomic structure and energy-band arrangement of a material determine its ability to conduct electric current. This is a fundamental concept behind diodes, transistors, integrated circuits and electronic devices.

1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lecture, you should be able to:

  • Differentiate conductors, insulators and semiconductors.
  • Explain valence electrons and free electrons.
  • Understand valence band, conduction band and forbidden energy gap.
  • Compare the energy-band diagrams of different materials.
  • Explain the effect of temperature on conductivity.
  • Identify practical applications of semiconductor materials.
  • Answer common electronics placement interview questions.

2. Introduction to Electrical Materials

Materials are classified according to how easily they allow electric charge to flow through them. Their electrical behavior depends on the availability of charge carriers and the energy required for electrons to move into conducting states.

The three important categories are:

  • Conductors: Materials that allow electric current to flow easily.
  • Insulators: Materials that strongly resist electric current.
  • Semiconductors: Materials whose conductivity lies between that of conductors and insulators and can be controlled.

Examples of materials

Category Examples Common application
Conductors Copper, aluminium, silver Wires, PCB tracks, electrical connections
Insulators Glass, rubber, mica, plastic Cable insulation, electrical isolation
Semiconductors Silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide Diodes, transistors, ICs, sensors

3. Atomic Structure and Electrical Conduction

An atom consists of a positively charged nucleus containing protons and neutrons, surrounded by negatively charged electrons. Electrons occupy different energy levels around the nucleus.

Valence electrons

The electrons in the outermost occupied shell are called valence electrons. They play an important role in chemical bonding and electrical conduction.

Free electrons

Electrons that gain sufficient energy to move away from their bonded state and participate in electrical conduction are called free electrons.

When an electric field is applied, mobile charge carriers acquire a net drift motion, producing electric current.

Conventional current direction is opposite to electron drift direction in a metallic conductor.
Important: In semiconductor materials, both electrons and holes contribute to electrical conduction. Holes represent the absence of electrons in otherwise occupied bonding states.

4. Energy Band Theory

In an isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. When a large number of atoms form a solid, interactions between their electrons cause these energy levels to split into closely spaced levels, forming energy bands.

4.1 Valence Band

The valence band is the highest energy band that is normally occupied by valence electrons at absolute zero temperature.

4.2 Conduction Band

The conduction band is the energy band in which electrons can move through the material and contribute to electrical conduction.

4.3 Forbidden Energy Gap

The forbidden energy gap, also called the band gap, is the energy range between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band in which no allowed electron energy states exist in an ideal periodic crystal.

Band gap: Eg = Ec - Ev
Ec = Conduction-band edge energy
Ev = Valence-band edge energy

5. Conductors

Conductors have a large number of mobile charge carriers. In metals, the valence and conduction bands may overlap, or a band may be partially filled. Electrons can therefore move into nearby available energy states with very little additional energy.

Characteristics

  • High electrical conductivity.
  • Low electrical resistivity.
  • Large number of mobile electrons.
  • Generally low resistance for a given length and cross-sectional area.
  • Used for current-carrying paths and electrical connections.

Practical example: Copper wire

Copper is commonly used in electrical wiring and PCB tracks because it has high conductivity, good mechanical properties and is relatively economical.

R = ρL/A
R = Resistance (Ω)
ρ = Resistivity (Ω·m)
L = Length (m)
A = Cross-sectional area (m²)

For the same material and length, increasing the cross-sectional area reduces resistance.

6. Insulators

Insulators have a filled valence band and an empty conduction band at absolute zero, separated by a large forbidden energy gap. Ordinary thermal energy is generally insufficient to move a significant number of electrons into the conduction band.

Characteristics

  • Very low electrical conductivity under normal conditions.
  • High electrical resistivity.
  • Large forbidden energy gap.
  • Used to prevent unwanted current flow.
  • Can break down electrically if the applied electric field becomes excessive.

Practical applications

  • Rubber insulation around electrical wires.
  • Plastic insulation in electronic cables.
  • Glass and ceramic electrical insulators.
  • Insulating layers in electronic devices.
Engineering point: An insulator is not an absolutely perfect barrier to current. At sufficiently high electric fields, electrical breakdown can occur.

7. Semiconductors

Semiconductors have electrical conductivity between that of conductors and insulators. Their conductivity can be controlled by temperature, light, electric fields and the addition of controlled impurities called dopants.

Silicon is the most widely used semiconductor material in modern electronic devices.

Characteristics

  • Moderate band gap compared with typical insulators.
  • Electrical conductivity is controllable.
  • Both electrons and holes can act as charge carriers.
  • Conductivity generally increases as temperature increases.
  • Doping can significantly increase carrier concentration.

Common semiconductor materials

Material Approximate band gap at 300 K Application
Silicon (Si) 1.12 eV Diodes, transistors, microprocessors, ICs
Germanium (Ge) 0.66 eV Specialized semiconductor devices and detectors
Gallium Arsenide (GaAs) 1.42 eV High-frequency and optoelectronic devices
The band-gap values are approximate and refer to intrinsic materials near room temperature. Actual device behavior also depends on doping, defects, structure and operating conditions.

9. Temperature Effect on Electrical Conductivity

9.1 Conductors

In ordinary metallic conductors, increasing temperature increases lattice vibrations. These vibrations scatter conducting electrons more frequently, generally increasing resistance and reducing conductivity.

9.2 Semiconductors

In an intrinsic semiconductor, increasing temperature generates more electron-hole pairs. The increased carrier concentration generally causes conductivity to increase and resistivity to decrease.

9.3 Insulators

Insulators generally remain poor conductors under normal operating conditions. At sufficiently high temperatures, some insulators can develop increased conductivity because more charge carriers become available.

Placement concept: Metals generally have a positive temperature coefficient of resistance over their normal operating range. Intrinsic semiconductors generally have a negative temperature coefficient of resistance.

10. Why Silicon Is Widely Used

Silicon is widely used in electronics because of the following properties:

  • Suitable band gap: Its band gap is approximately 1.12 eV at room temperature.
  • Abundance: Silicon is abundant in the Earth's crust.
  • Thermal stability: It is suitable for many practical operating conditions.
  • Oxide formation: Silicon forms a stable silicon dioxide layer (SiO₂), useful in MOS technology.
  • Doping control: Its electrical properties can be accurately controlled by adding dopants.
  • Manufacturing maturity: Silicon processing technology is highly developed.

Applications

  • Microcontrollers and microprocessors.
  • Memory chips and integrated circuits.
  • Rectifier and switching diodes.
  • BJT and MOSFET transistors.
  • Solar cells and semiconductor sensors.

11. Solved Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Energy in Electron Volts

An electron gains energy equal to 1.12 eV. Express this energy in joules.

Given:

  • Energy = 1.12 eV
  • 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
E = 1.12 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹
E = 1.794 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Answer: Approximately 1.79 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Problem 2: Resistance of a Copper Wire

A copper wire has a length of 10 m and a cross-sectional area of 1 mm². Calculate its resistance. Assume copper resistivity is 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m at the specified temperature.

Given:

  • L = 10 m
  • A = 1 mm² = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m²
  • ρ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m
R = ρL/A
R = (1.68 × 10⁻⁸ × 10)/(1 × 10⁻⁶)
R = 0.168 Ω

Answer: 0.168 Ω.

Problem 3: Band Gap Energy Conversion

Calculate the energy in joules corresponding to the silicon band gap of 1.12 eV.

E = 1.12 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹
E = 1.794 × 10⁻¹⁹ J

Answer: 1.794 × 10⁻¹⁹ J approximately.

12. Practical Electronics Applications

Application 1: PCB Tracks

Copper tracks on a printed circuit board provide low-resistance connections between electronic components. Their width and thickness affect current-carrying capability and voltage drop.

Application 2: Cable Insulation

Plastic or rubber insulation prevents accidental contact with conductors and reduces the risk of short circuits and electric shock.

Application 3: Semiconductor Switching

Silicon-based transistors are used as electronic switches in microcontrollers, embedded systems, power supplies and digital circuits.

Application 4: Temperature Sensors

Semiconductor devices such as thermistors and integrated temperature sensors use temperature-dependent electrical properties to measure temperature.

13. Common Placement Interview Questions

Q1. What is a semiconductor?

A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator and can be controlled through temperature, doping, light or electric fields.

Q2. Give two examples of semiconductors.

Silicon and germanium.

Q3. What is the approximate band gap of silicon?

Approximately 1.12 eV at 300 K.

Q4. What is the forbidden energy gap?

It is the energy range between the valence-band edge and conduction-band edge where no allowed electron states exist in an ideal crystal.

Q5. Why does copper conduct electricity?

Copper contains a large number of mobile electrons that can drift under an applied electric field.

Q6. Why is rubber used as wire insulation?

Rubber has high electrical resistivity and helps prevent unwanted current flow and accidental contact.

Q7. What happens to semiconductor conductivity when temperature increases?

For an intrinsic semiconductor, conductivity generally increases because thermal energy generates more electron-hole pairs.

Q8. What is the difference between valence and conduction bands?

The valence band contains bonding electrons, while the conduction band contains electrons that can contribute to electrical conduction.

Q9. Why is silicon preferred over germanium in many applications?

Silicon has a larger band gap, better high-temperature performance for many applications, abundant availability and a useful stable oxide.

Q10. What is the unit of band-gap energy?

Electron volt (eV) is commonly used.

Q11. Can an insulator conduct electricity?

Yes. Under sufficiently high electric fields or other extreme conditions, an insulator can undergo electrical breakdown.

Q12. What is resistivity?

Resistivity is an intrinsic material property that indicates how strongly a material opposes electric current. Its SI unit is Ω·m.

Q13. What is the difference between resistance and resistivity?

Resistance depends on the material and its dimensions, whereas resistivity is a material property at a specified temperature and physical condition.

Q14. What is the charge carrier in a metal?

Mobile electrons are the principal charge carriers in ordinary metals.

Q15. Why are semiconductors important in embedded systems?

Semiconductor devices form the transistors, memory, microcontrollers, sensors and interface circuits used in embedded systems.

15. Practical Learning and Observation

This activity demonstrates the difference between a conductive path and an insulating path using a low-voltage circuit.

Activity: Testing Material Conductivity

Objective: Observe whether different materials allow a small test current to flow.

Required components:

  • Low-voltage battery or current-limited DC supply.
  • LED.
  • Suitable series current-limiting resistor.
  • Connecting wires.
  • Test materials such as copper wire, plastic and rubber.

Procedure:

  1. Construct a low-voltage LED test circuit with a suitable series resistor.
  2. Leave a small gap in the test path.
  3. Insert a test material across the gap.
  4. Observe whether the LED turns on.
  5. Repeat with different materials and record observations.
Safety: Use only a low-voltage, current-limited supply. Never use household mains voltage for this activity. Disconnect power before changing test materials.

Expected observations

Material Expected observation Reason
Copper wire LED may glow High electrical conductivity
Plastic LED remains off Very low conductivity
Rubber LED remains off Very high resistivity

The LED test is qualitative. A semiconductor sample may not produce a visible LED response in this simple setup because its behavior depends on its type, doping, geometry and electrical connections.

16. Important Revision Points

  • Conductors have many mobile charge carriers.
  • Insulators have a large forbidden energy gap.
  • Semiconductors have a relatively small band gap.
  • Silicon band gap is approximately 1.12 eV at 300 K.
  • Germanium band gap is approximately 0.66 eV at 300 K.
  • Valence electrons participate in bonding.
  • Conduction-band electrons can contribute to current.
  • Intrinsic semiconductor conductivity generally increases with temperature.
  • Silicon is widely used because of its electrical, thermal and manufacturing properties.
