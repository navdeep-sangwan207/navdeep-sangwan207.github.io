Lecture 9: Semiconductor Parameters and Interview Questions

Analog Electronics | Semiconductor Fundamentals | NS Learning Hub

1. Introduction

Semiconductor parameters describe the electrical and physical properties that determine how semiconductor materials and devices behave in electronic circuits.

An electronics or embedded systems engineer should understand these parameters when selecting components, reading datasheets, designing circuits, testing devices, and troubleshooting hardware.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the important electrical parameters of semiconductors.
  • Differentiate intrinsic and extrinsic material properties.
  • Understand carrier concentration, mobility, conductivity, and resistivity.
  • Interpret important silicon, germanium, and gallium arsenide properties.
  • Understand junction capacitance, leakage current, and breakdown voltage.
  • Read important semiconductor datasheet specifications.
  • Solve placement-oriented numerical problems.
  • Prepare for semiconductor fundamentals interview questions.
Placement focus: Semiconductor theory becomes useful in industry when you can connect material parameters to diode operation, transistor performance, leakage, switching, temperature behavior, and component selection.

2. Important Semiconductor Parameters

2.1 Energy Bandgap (Eg)

Energy bandgap is the energy difference between the bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band.

Eg = EC − EV
  • It determines how much energy is needed to excite an electron into the conduction band.
  • It influences intrinsic carrier concentration.
  • It affects optical absorption and emission.
  • It varies with temperature.

2.2 Intrinsic Carrier Concentration (ni)

Intrinsic carrier concentration is the equilibrium concentration of electrons and holes in a pure semiconductor.

n = p = ni

For silicon at approximately 300 K, a commonly used reference value is:

ni ≈ 1 × 1010 cm−3

2.3 Carrier Concentration

Carrier concentration represents the number of mobile charge carriers per unit volume.

Parameter Meaning
n Electron concentration
p Hole concentration
ND Donor concentration
NA Acceptor concentration
ni Intrinsic carrier concentration

For an uncompensated N-type semiconductor with complete donor ionization and negligible intrinsic contribution:

n ≈ ND

For an uncompensated P-type semiconductor with complete acceptor ionization and negligible intrinsic contribution:

p ≈ NA

2.4 Mass-Action Law

np = ni2

This relationship applies to a semiconductor in thermal equilibrium. It is useful for calculating minority-carrier concentration.

2.5 Carrier Mobility (μ)

Mobility indicates how easily charge carriers move through a semiconductor under an applied electric field.

μ = vd/E

Common units:

  • cm²/(V·s)
  • m²/(V·s)

2.6 Conductivity (σ)

σ = q(nμn + pμp)

Conductivity measures the ability of a semiconductor to conduct electric current.

2.7 Resistivity (ρ)

ρ = 1/σ

Resistivity is the opposition offered by a material to current flow. Its SI unit is Ω·m; a commonly used semiconductor unit is Ω·cm.

2.8 Diffusion Coefficient (D)

The diffusion coefficient describes how rapidly carriers spread due to a concentration gradient.

Einstein relation: D/μ = kT/q

For electrons and holes at thermal equilibrium:

Dn/μn = Dp/μp = VT

Where VT = kT/q is the thermal voltage.

2.9 Carrier Lifetime (τ)

Carrier lifetime is the average time an excess minority carrier exists before recombination, under the conditions relevant to the lifetime measurement or model.

  • It influences charge storage.
  • It affects switching behavior.
  • It is important in photodiodes and solar cells.
  • It influences diffusion length.

2.10 Diffusion Length (L)

L = √(Dτ)

Diffusion length represents the characteristic distance a carrier diffuses before recombination.

3. Comparison of Common Semiconductor Materials

Silicon, germanium, and gallium arsenide are important semiconductor materials. Their properties influence device selection and application.

Property at approximately 300 K Silicon (Si) Germanium (Ge) Gallium Arsenide (GaAs)
Bandgap 1.12 eV 0.66 eV 1.42 eV
Bandgap type Indirect Indirect Direct
Electron mobility About 1350 cm²/V·s About 3900 cm²/V·s About 8500 cm²/V·s
Hole mobility About 480 cm²/V·s About 1900 cm²/V·s About 400 cm²/V·s
Typical applications ICs, power devices, sensors Specialized detectors and devices RF, microwave and optoelectronics
The mobility values are representative approximate bulk-material values, not guaranteed values for every device. Actual mobility depends on doping, temperature, crystal quality, electric field, and processing.

3.1 Why Silicon Is Widely Used

  • Abundant raw material.
  • Well-developed manufacturing technology.
  • High-quality silicon dioxide can be formed.
  • Suitable for large-scale integrated circuits.
  • Good thermal and electrical characteristics for many applications.

3.2 Why GaAs Is Used in RF and Optoelectronics

  • High electron mobility.
  • Direct bandgap.
  • Useful for high-frequency and high-speed devices.
  • Suitable for many light-emitting and photonic applications.

3.3 Why Germanium Is Used in Specialized Applications

  • Relatively small bandgap.
  • High carrier mobility.
  • Useful in some infrared detectors and specialized devices.
  • Higher intrinsic carrier concentration at a given temperature than silicon.

4. Direct and Indirect Bandgap

Semiconductors are classified as direct-bandgap or indirect-bandgap materials based on the relative positions of the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum in crystal momentum space.

4.1 Direct Bandgap

In a direct-bandgap semiconductor, the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum. An electron can recombine with a hole while emitting a photon without requiring a phonon to conserve crystal momentum.

4.2 Indirect Bandgap

In an indirect-bandgap semiconductor, the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at different crystal momenta. A phonon is normally involved in band-to-band optical transitions to conserve momentum.

Simplified conceptual energy-versus-momentum comparison. The curves are illustrative, not quantitative band structures.
Feature Direct bandgap Indirect bandgap
Band extrema Same momentum Different momentum
Photon emission Efficient band-to-band emission is possible Requires phonon assistance for momentum conservation
Examples GaAs Si, Ge
Applications LEDs, laser diodes, optoelectronics Integrated circuits and many electronic devices

5. Important PN Junction Parameters

5.1 Barrier Potential

The built-in potential is the electrostatic potential difference established across a PN junction at thermal equilibrium.

Vbi = (kT/q) ln(NAND/ni2)

This ideal abrupt-junction expression assumes thermal equilibrium, non-degenerate doping, and the applicable semiconductor approximations.

5.2 Depletion Width

The depletion region is the region near the PN junction where mobile carriers are depleted and fixed ionized dopants remain.

W = √[(2εs/q) (1/NA + 1/ND) (Vbi − VA)]

For this ideal abrupt-junction expression, VA is positive for forward bias and negative for reverse bias. The expression applies within the depletion approximation and before breakdown.

5.3 Junction Capacitance

A reverse-biased PN junction has depletion-region capacitance because the separated charge regions behave approximately like the plates of a capacitor.

Cj = εsA/W

Where:

  • εs = Semiconductor permittivity.
  • A = Junction area.
  • W = Depletion width.

As reverse bias increases, depletion width generally increases and junction capacitance decreases.

5.4 Diffusion Capacitance

Under forward bias, stored injected minority carriers contribute to diffusion capacitance.

For a simple diode model:

Cd = dQ/dV

Where Q is stored charge and V is the diode voltage.

5.5 Reverse Saturation Current

Reverse saturation current is the small reverse current associated with minority-carrier transport in an ideal diode model. It is strongly temperature-dependent.

5.6 Breakdown Voltage

Breakdown voltage is the reverse voltage at which the diode current increases sharply due to a breakdown mechanism.

Parameter Meaning
Vbi Built-in potential
W Depletion width
Cj Junction capacitance
Cd Diffusion capacitance
IR Reverse current
VBR Reverse breakdown voltage

6. Important Semiconductor Diode Parameters

Parameter Meaning Engineering relevance
Forward voltage (VF) Voltage across a conducting diode at a specified current Power loss and voltage drop
Forward current (IF) Specified forward conduction current Current rating and thermal design
Reverse voltage (VR) Reverse voltage applied across the diode Reverse operating limits
Peak inverse voltage (PIV) Maximum reverse voltage encountered in a specified rectifier circuit Rectifier diode selection
Reverse leakage (IR) Small current under reverse bias before breakdown Leakage and power consumption
Reverse recovery time (trr) Time associated with recovery from forward conduction to reverse blocking Switching performance
Junction capacitance (Cj) Capacitance associated with depletion region High-frequency response
Power dissipation (PD) Power converted into heat Thermal design

6.1 Diode Power Dissipation

PD ≈ VFIF

For a diode carrying 100 mA with a forward voltage of 0.7 V:

PD = 0.7 × 0.1 = 0.07 W
Power dissipation = 70 mW
For switching and transient operation, calculate or estimate power using the actual voltage and current waveforms rather than relying only on the DC approximation.

7. Important BJT Parameters

7.1 Current Gain (β)

β = IC/IB

β is the common-emitter DC current gain, under the stated operating conditions.

7.2 Common-Base Current Gain (α)

α = IC/IE

7.3 Relationship Between α and β

β = α/(1 − α)
α = β/(β + 1)

7.4 Collector-Emitter Saturation Voltage

VCE(sat) is the collector-emitter voltage when a BJT is driven into saturation under specified test conditions.

7.5 Maximum Collector Current

The maximum collector current is a device rating. It must not be exceeded, and it does not independently guarantee safe operation.

7.6 Maximum Power Dissipation

P ≈ VCEIC

This is a simplified DC estimate for a BJT in a particular operating condition. Base-drive and other power contributions may matter in detailed thermal calculations.

7.7 Transition Frequency (fT)

Transition frequency is the frequency at which the magnitude of the small-signal common-emitter short-circuit current gain becomes unity, under specified test conditions.

7.8 Junction Temperature

Junction temperature is the temperature of the semiconductor die inside the device.

Tj ≈ Ta + PDθJA

Where:

  • Tj = Junction temperature.
  • Ta = Ambient temperature.
  • PD = Device power dissipation.
  • θJA = Junction-to-ambient thermal resistance.
This thermal equation is a steady-state approximation. Actual junction temperature depends on the thermal path, mounting, airflow, PCB design, heat sink, and transient thermal impedance.

8. Important MOSFET Parameters

MOSFETs are voltage-controlled field-effect devices. Their datasheets contain several parameters that are important for circuit design.

Parameter Meaning
VGS(th) Gate-source threshold voltage measured under specified test conditions
RDS(on) Drain-source on-state resistance
ID Drain current rating under specified thermal conditions
VDS(max) Maximum rated drain-source voltage
VGS(max) Maximum permitted gate-source voltage
Qg Gate charge
Ciss Input capacitance
Coss Output capacitance
Crss Reverse transfer capacitance
Ptot Maximum power dissipation under specified conditions
Important: VGS(th) is not the recommended gate-drive voltage for fully turning on a power MOSFET. Check RDS(on) at the actual gate-source voltage available in the circuit.

8.1 MOSFET Conduction Loss

Pconduction = ID2RDS(on)

Example:

A MOSFET carries 5 A and has an on-resistance of 0.04 Ω at the actual gate-drive voltage and operating temperature.

P = 5² × 0.04 = 1 W
Conduction loss = 1 W

In a practical design, RDS(on) must be evaluated at the relevant junction temperature and gate voltage. Switching losses must also be considered.

9. Important Parameter Relationships

Parameter Relationship Use
Bandgap Eg = EC − EV Energy band analysis
Mass-action law np = ni² Minority-carrier calculation
Mobility μ = vd/E Carrier transport
Conductivity σ = q(nμn + pμp) Electrical conduction
Resistivity ρ = 1/σ Material resistance
Einstein relation D/μ = kT/q Drift-diffusion analysis
Diffusion length L = √(Dτ) Carrier transport
Thermal voltage VT = kT/q Diode and carrier equations
Diode current I = IS[exp(V/(ηVT)) − 1] Diode I-V analysis
Junction capacitance Cj = εsA/W Junction behavior
BJT current gain β = IC/IB Transistor analysis
MOSFET conduction loss P = ID²RDS(on) Power design
In the diode equation, η is the ideality factor, IS is the reverse saturation-current parameter, and VT is thermal voltage. The equation is an idealized model and does not describe every high-current, breakdown, or parasitic effect.

10. Solved Placement-Oriented Numericals

Numerical 1: Minority Carrier Concentration

An N-type silicon semiconductor has donor concentration ND = 2 × 1016 cm−3. The intrinsic carrier concentration is ni = 1 × 1010 cm−3. Calculate minority hole concentration.

Solution:

Assuming complete ionization, no compensation, and n ≈ ND:

p = ni²/n
p = (1 × 1010)² / (2 × 1016)
p = 5 × 103 cm−3

Numerical 2: Conductivity of an N-Type Semiconductor

Given:

  • n = 5 × 1015 cm−3
  • p = 2 × 104 cm−3
  • μn = 1200 cm²/(V·s)
  • μp = 400 cm²/(V·s)
  • q = 1.6 × 10−19 C
Solution:
σ = q(nμn + pμp)
σ = 1.6 × 10−19 [(5 × 1015 × 1200) + (2 × 104 × 400)]
σ ≈ 1.6 × 10−19 × 6 × 1018
σ ≈ 0.96 S/cm

Numerical 3: Resistivity

If conductivity is 0.96 S/cm, calculate resistivity.

ρ = 1/σ = 1/0.96
ρ ≈ 1.042 Ω·cm

Numerical 4: Diffusion Length

Given:

  • D = 25 cm²/s
  • τ = 4 μs = 4 × 10−6 s
L = √(Dτ)
L = √(25 × 4 × 10−6)
L = √(100 × 10−6)
L = 0.01 cm = 100 μm

Numerical 5: BJT Current Gain

A BJT has a base current of 40 μA and collector current of 4 mA. Calculate β.

β = IC/IB
β = 4 mA / 40 μA = 4000/40
β = 100

Numerical 6: BJT Base Current

A transistor has β = 120 and required collector current is 24 mA. Calculate the approximate base current in forward-active operation.

IB = IC/β
IB = 24 mA/120
IB = 0.2 mA = 200 μA

Numerical 7: Diode Power Dissipation

A diode has a forward voltage of 0.8 V and carries 250 mA. Calculate approximate DC power dissipation.

P = VF × IF
P = 0.8 × 0.25
P = 0.2 W

Numerical 8: MOSFET Conduction Loss

A MOSFET carries 4 A and has RDS(on) = 0.05 Ω under the specified operating conditions.

P = ID²RDS(on)
P = 4² × 0.05 = 16 × 0.05
P = 0.8 W

Numerical 9: Junction Temperature

Given:

  • Ambient temperature Ta = 40°C
  • Power dissipation PD = 1.5 W
  • Thermal resistance θJA = 30°C/W
Tj = Ta + PDθJA
Tj = 40 + (1.5 × 30)
Tj = 85°C

Numerical 10: Thermal Voltage

Calculate thermal voltage at 300 K using k = 1.38 × 10−23 J/K and q = 1.602 × 10−19 C.

VT = kT/q
VT = (1.38 × 10−23 × 300) / (1.602 × 10−19)
VT ≈ 25.84 mV

11. Semiconductor Datasheet Interpretation

A datasheet is the primary reference for selecting and operating a semiconductor device. Engineers should not select a component based only on its part number or a typical voltage value.

11.1 Important Datasheet Sections

Datasheet section What to check
Absolute maximum ratings Voltage, current, power, temperature limits
Electrical characteristics Forward voltage, leakage, gain, on-resistance
Thermal characteristics Junction temperature and thermal resistance
Switching characteristics Turn-on, turn-off and recovery times
Test conditions Voltage, current, temperature and measurement setup
Package information Pin configuration, dimensions and mounting details

11.2 Absolute Maximum Ratings vs Recommended Operating Conditions

Absolute maximum ratings specify stress limits that must not be exceeded. They do not necessarily define normal operating conditions.

Recommended operating conditions specify the intended range for normal device operation, where provided by the manufacturer.

Do not design a circuit to operate continuously at absolute maximum ratings. Provide suitable electrical and thermal margins and verify the actual datasheet conditions.

11.3 Example: Selecting a Rectifier Diode

Suppose a rectifier circuit requires a diode that can handle:

  • Maximum repetitive reverse voltage of 100 V.
  • Average forward current of 1 A.
  • Operating temperature up to the expected ambient and junction limits.

Before selecting a diode, verify:

  1. Repetitive peak reverse voltage rating.
  2. Average forward current rating.
  3. Surge current rating if startup or charging surges occur.
  4. Forward voltage and power dissipation.
  5. Reverse leakage at the expected temperature.
  6. Thermal resistance and mounting conditions.
  7. Package and pin configuration.
The required voltage and current ratings must include circuit transients, tolerance, load conditions, and suitable design margins.

12. Practical Semiconductor Testing

12.1 Testing a Diode Using a Digital Multimeter

  1. Switch off the circuit supply.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely.
  3. Isolate at least one diode terminal when in-circuit components could affect the reading.
  4. Select diode-test mode.
  5. Connect red probe to the anode and black probe to the cathode.
  6. Record the forward voltage reading.
  7. Reverse the probes and observe the reverse reading.
Observation Possible interpretation
Forward drop and reverse OL Typical healthy diode indication
Near-zero reading in both directions Possible shorted diode
OL in both directions Possible open diode or poor connection
Unexpected reverse reading Possible leakage, parallel circuit path, or device-specific behavior

12.2 Testing a BJT

  • Identify the base, collector, and emitter using the datasheet.
  • Use diode-test mode to examine the base-emitter and base-collector junctions.
  • Check for an unexpected short between collector and emitter.
  • Compare results with the expected junction polarity for NPN or PNP devices.
  • Use a suitable transistor tester or controlled circuit for gain measurements.

12.3 Testing a MOSFET

  • Identify gate, drain, and source from the datasheet.
  • Check for an obvious gate-to-source or gate-to-drain short.
  • Check the intrinsic body-diode path using diode-test mode.
  • Use a controlled gate-drive test to verify switching behavior.
  • Never exceed the maximum gate-source voltage rating.
Multimeter tests are preliminary checks, not complete proof of device health. In-circuit readings may be affected by parallel components, and power devices require appropriate controlled tests.

13. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Semiconductor Fundamentals

Q1. What is a semiconductor?
A semiconductor is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator and can be controlled through doping, temperature, electric fields, and illumination.
Q2. What is the energy bandgap?
It is the energy difference between the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum.
Q3. What is the bandgap of silicon at room temperature?
Approximately 1.12 eV at 300 K.
Q4. What is the difference between intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors?
An intrinsic semiconductor is ideally pure, while an extrinsic semiconductor has intentional impurity doping to control carrier concentration.
Q5. What is doping?
Doping is the controlled introduction of impurity atoms into a semiconductor to modify its electrical properties.
Q6. What is a donor impurity?
A donor impurity supplies electrons to the conduction band and produces N-type material.
Q7. What is an acceptor impurity?
An acceptor impurity accepts an electron from a neighboring bond, creating a hole and producing P-type material.
Q8. What are majority and minority carriers?
Majority carriers are the more numerous mobile carriers in a doped semiconductor; minority carriers are the less numerous carriers.
Q9. State the mass-action law.
At thermal equilibrium, np = ni².
Q10. What is intrinsic carrier concentration?
It is the equilibrium electron or hole concentration in an intrinsic semiconductor, where n = p = ni.

Carrier Transport and Material Parameters

Q11. What is carrier mobility?
Mobility is the drift velocity produced per unit applied electric field.
Q12. What is the unit of mobility?
Commonly cm²/(V·s) or m²/(V·s).
Q13. What is conductivity?
Conductivity measures how easily a material conducts electric current. For a semiconductor, σ = q(nμn + pμp).
Q14. What is resistivity?
Resistivity measures the opposition of a material to current flow and is the reciprocal of conductivity.
Q15. What is the Einstein relation?
It relates carrier diffusion coefficient and mobility: D/μ = kT/q under the applicable non-degenerate equilibrium conditions.
Q16. What is carrier lifetime?
It is the average time an excess carrier exists before recombination under specified conditions.
Q17. What is diffusion length?
It is the characteristic distance a carrier diffuses before recombination, given by L = √(Dτ) in the simple model.
Q18. What is the difference between drift and diffusion current?
Drift current is caused by an electric field, while diffusion current is caused by a carrier concentration gradient.

Material and Temperature

Q19. Why is silicon widely used in integrated circuits?
It offers mature manufacturing technology, suitable electrical properties, abundant raw material, and high-quality silicon dioxide formation.
Q20. Why is GaAs useful in optoelectronics?
GaAs has a direct bandgap, making radiative optical transitions comparatively efficient.
Q21. What is the difference between direct and indirect bandgap materials?
In direct materials, the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same momentum; in indirect materials, they occur at different momenta.
Q22. What happens to intrinsic carrier concentration when temperature increases?
It increases strongly because thermal energy generates more electron-hole pairs.
Q23. What happens to semiconductor mobility when temperature increases?
Mobility generally decreases in a phonon-scattering-dominated range. At low temperatures, impurity scattering may produce a different trend.
Q24. What is the freeze-out region?
It is the low-temperature region in which many dopants are not ionized and free-carrier concentration is low.
Q25. What is thermal voltage at room temperature?
Approximately 25.85 mV at 300 K.

PN Junction and Diodes

Q26. What is depletion width?
It is the width of the region around a PN junction that is depleted of mobile carriers and contains fixed ionized dopants.
Q27. What is junction capacitance?
It is capacitance associated with charge separation across the depletion region.
Q28. What happens to junction capacitance when reverse bias increases?
The depletion width generally increases, so junction capacitance decreases.
Q29. What is reverse recovery time?
It describes the time associated with a diode recovering its reverse-blocking capability after forward conduction.
Q30. What is reverse breakdown voltage?
It is the reverse voltage at which current increases sharply due to a breakdown mechanism.

Transistors and Practical Engineering

Q31. Define BJT current gain β.
β = IC/IB in common-emitter operation under specified conditions.
Q32. What is transition frequency fT?
It is the frequency at which the magnitude of small-signal common-emitter short-circuit current gain becomes unity.
Q33. What is thermal runaway?
It is a positive-feedback condition in which rising temperature increases current and power dissipation, causing further heating.
Q34. What is MOSFET RDS(on)?
It is the drain-source on-state resistance under specified gate voltage, drain current, and temperature conditions.
Q35. Why is VGS(th) not sufficient to select a MOSFET gate-drive voltage?
Threshold voltage is measured at a small specified drain current. It does not guarantee low RDS(on) or full enhancement.
Q36. What is junction temperature?
It is the temperature of the semiconductor die inside a device package.
Q37. What is thermal resistance?
Thermal resistance describes the temperature rise per unit power transferred through a specified thermal path, usually expressed in °C/W or K/W.
Q38. What is the difference between absolute maximum ratings and operating conditions?
Absolute maximum ratings are stress limits; recommended operating conditions describe the intended normal operating range.
Q39. How do you test a diode using a multimeter?
Use diode-test mode, measure forward drop, reverse the probes, and compare the results with expected device behavior.
Q40. Why should a datasheet be checked before replacing a semiconductor?
To verify voltage, current, power, thermal, switching, package, and pin-configuration compatibility.

Conceptual Questions

  1. Explain the relationship between carrier concentration and conductivity.
  2. Compare silicon, germanium, and GaAs.
  3. Explain direct and indirect bandgap with examples.
  4. Differentiate between drift and diffusion current.
  5. Explain how junction capacitance changes with reverse bias.
  6. Differentiate between diffusion capacitance and junction capacitance.
  7. Explain reverse recovery in a PN junction diode.
  8. Explain the importance of junction temperature in power semiconductor selection.
  9. Differentiate between BJT current gain α and β.
  10. Explain why MOSFET gate threshold voltage is not the same as full turn-on voltage.

Numerical Practice

  1. A P-type silicon semiconductor has NA = 4 × 1016 cm−3 and ni = 1 × 1010 cm−3. Calculate minority electron concentration, assuming complete ionization and no compensation.
  2. A semiconductor has n = 3 × 1015 cm−3, p = 2 × 104 cm−3, μn = 1000 cm²/(V·s), and μp = 400 cm²/(V·s). Calculate conductivity using q = 1.6 × 10−19 C.
  3. A diode carries 0.5 A at a forward drop of 0.75 V. Calculate its approximate power dissipation.
  4. A BJT has IC = 12 mA and IB = 100 μA. Calculate β.
  5. A MOSFET carries 6 A with RDS(on) = 0.025 Ω. Calculate conduction loss.
  6. A device dissipates 2 W, has ambient temperature 35°C, and thermal resistance 25°C/W. Estimate junction temperature using the steady-state approximation.
  7. Calculate thermal voltage at 350 K.
  8. A carrier has diffusion coefficient D = 20 cm²/s and lifetime τ = 9 μs. Calculate diffusion length.

Practical Interview Scenarios

  1. A diode becomes hot during operation. What parameters would you inspect?
  2. A transistor collector current increases after the circuit warms up. What could be the cause?
  3. A MOSFET is not switching fully with a microcontroller output. Which datasheet parameters would you check?
  4. A replacement diode has a higher current rating but a lower reverse voltage rating. Is it a suitable replacement?
  5. A semiconductor device fails after operating for several minutes. How would you investigate the thermal cause?

15. Common Mistakes in Semiconductor Analysis

  • Confusing electron concentration with electron mobility.
  • Mixing cm−3 and m−3 without unit conversion.
  • Using Celsius instead of kelvin in thermal equations.
  • Applying the mass-action law to a non-equilibrium condition without an appropriate model.
  • Assuming dopant concentration always equals majority carrier concentration.
  • Ignoring compensation and incomplete ionization where relevant.
  • Assuming typical datasheet values are guaranteed limits.
  • Confusing absolute maximum ratings with recommended operating conditions.
  • Selecting a MOSFET based only on threshold voltage.
  • Ignoring junction temperature and thermal resistance.
  • Assuming all diode breakdown mechanisms have identical temperature behavior.
