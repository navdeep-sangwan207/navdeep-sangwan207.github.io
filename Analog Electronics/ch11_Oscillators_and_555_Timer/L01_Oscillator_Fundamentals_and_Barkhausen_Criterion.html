2. What Is an Oscillator?

An electronic oscillator is a circuit that generates a repetitive electrical waveform without requiring a repetitive external input signal.

It converts DC power from a supply into a periodic AC signal. Depending on the design, its output can be sinusoidal, square, triangular or another periodic waveform.

Sinusoidal Oscillator

Generates a sine wave. Examples include RC phase-shift, Wien bridge and LC oscillators.

Relaxation Oscillator

Uses charging and discharging processes to produce switching waveforms, such as a 555 astable output.

Crystal Oscillator

Uses mechanical resonance of a piezoelectric crystal for accurate frequency generation.

3. Why Are Oscillators Required?

  • Clock generation in digital systems.
  • Carrier generation in communication equipment.
  • Signal generation in electronic testing instruments.
  • Frequency generation in radio transmitters and receivers.
  • Timing signals in microcontrollers and embedded systems.
  • Audio tone generation and alarm circuits.
  • Reference-frequency generation in measurement systems.
Embedded systems connection: A microcontroller needs a clock source to execute instructions and maintain timing. Depending on the design, this can be an internal oscillator, external crystal or resonator, or another clock source.

5. Basic Principle of an Oscillator

A feedback oscillator consists of an amplifier and a frequency-selective feedback network. A portion of the output signal is fed back to the input in the correct phase to reinforce the oscillation.

How it works

  1. A small initial disturbance is present due to noise, switching or power-up transients.
  2. The amplifier increases the signal amplitude.
  3. The feedback network returns a portion of the output to the input.
  4. If the returned signal reinforces the original signal at the selected frequency, oscillations build up.
  5. Nonlinearities or an amplitude-control mechanism limit the growth and establish a stable amplitude.

6. Positive Feedback

Positive feedback occurs when the feedback signal reinforces the input signal.

Vf = β × Vout

Here β is the feedback factor, representing the fraction of output returned to the input.

For an amplifier with positive feedback, the effective gain is:

Af = A / (1 − Aβ)

As the loop gain Aβ approaches unity at the relevant phase condition, the ideal closed-loop gain expression becomes very large. This is the basis of the oscillation condition.

Positive feedback does not automatically guarantee a useful oscillator. The frequency-selective network, phase shift, loop gain, start-up condition and amplitude stabilization must all be considered.

7. Loop Gain

Loop gain is the product of the amplifier gain and feedback factor.

Loop Gain = Aβ

Where:

  • A = amplifier voltage gain.
  • β = feedback network transfer factor.
  • Aβ = total gain around the feedback loop.

For sustained sinusoidal oscillation in the ideal Barkhausen model, the loop gain must satisfy both magnitude and phase conditions at the oscillation frequency.

8. Barkhausen Criterion

The Barkhausen criterion gives the ideal conditions for sustained sinusoidal oscillations in a linear feedback oscillator.

|Aβ| = 1
∠Aβ = 0° or 360° × n

Where n is an integer. Equivalently, the total phase shift around the feedback loop must be an integer multiple of 360°.

Magnitude Condition

The magnitude of the loop gain must be unity at the oscillation frequency.

|Aβ| = 1

Phase Condition

The total phase shift around the loop must be zero degrees modulo 360 degrees.

∠Aβ = 0° (mod 360°)
For start-up, the small-signal loop gain is generally designed to be slightly greater than unity. As amplitude grows, nonlinear effects or an amplitude-control circuit reduce the effective gain to approximately unity.

9. Understanding the Phase Condition

Suppose the amplifier introduces 180° phase shift and the feedback network introduces another 180°.

Total Phase Shift = 180° + 180° = 360°

A 360° phase shift is equivalent to 0° for a periodic sinusoidal signal. The feedback signal therefore reinforces the original signal.

10. Magnitude Condition and Start-Up

Loop-gain magnitudeExpected behaviour
|Aβ| less than 1Oscillations decay and cannot be sustained.
|Aβ| approximately 1Ideal sustained oscillation condition.
|Aβ| greater than 1Oscillation amplitude initially grows; limiting action is needed.

In practical circuits, the amplifier cannot provide unlimited output amplitude. Device nonlinearity, supply rails, clipping, automatic gain control or other amplitude-stabilization mechanisms limit the signal.

A circuit with loop gain greater than one does not remain indefinitely in a linear growing state. Without suitable amplitude control, distortion and clipping can occur.

13. Oscillator Frequency

An oscillator produces a periodic signal at a frequency determined by its frequency-selective network and active-device characteristics.

f = 1 / T

Where:

  • f = frequency in hertz (Hz).
  • T = time period in seconds.

The frequency-selection components depend on the oscillator family.

OscillatorFrequency-determining elements
RC phase-shiftResistors and capacitors
Wien bridgeResistors and capacitors
ColpittsInductor and capacitive divider
HartleyInductive divider and capacitor
CrystalPiezoelectric crystal resonance and associated circuit

14. Types of Electronic Oscillators

RC Oscillators

Use resistors and capacitors to select the oscillation frequency. Commonly used at low and audio frequencies.

LC Oscillators

Use an inductor and capacitor as a resonant network. Commonly used in RF circuits.

Crystal Oscillators

Use a quartz crystal to obtain high frequency stability.

Relaxation Oscillators

Use threshold switching and capacitor charging/discharging to produce periodic waveforms.

15. Practical Oscillator Design Considerations

  • Choose the required frequency and waveform.
  • Select an appropriate oscillator topology.
  • Calculate frequency-selective components.
  • Verify amplifier gain and phase conditions.
  • Ensure sufficient start-up margin.
  • Include amplitude stabilization where necessary.
  • Check supply voltage, output swing and device bandwidth.
  • Use proper decoupling and PCB layout.
  • Check component tolerance and temperature drift.
  • Measure the output using an oscilloscope or frequency counter.

16. Oscillator Measurement Using an Oscilloscope

  1. Connect the oscilloscope ground correctly to the circuit ground.
  2. Connect the probe to the oscillator output.
  3. Choose a suitable voltage scale and time base.
  4. Observe the waveform shape and amplitude.
  5. Measure the period using the horizontal scale.
  6. Calculate frequency using f=1/T.
  7. Check for clipping, distortion, unwanted oscillation or unstable amplitude.
Use a suitable probe and avoid accidentally shorting a circuit node to ground through the oscilloscope probe ground.

18. Worked Examples

Example 1: Frequency from Time Period

An oscillator has a time period of 0.5 ms. Find its frequency.

T = 0.5 ms = 0.0005 s
f = 1 / 0.0005 = 2000 Hz = 2 kHz

Example 2: Time Period from Frequency

An oscillator operates at 10 kHz.

T = 1 / 10000 = 0.0001 s = 100 µs

Example 3: Barkhausen Magnitude Condition

An amplifier has gain A=50 and feedback factor β=0.02.

Aβ = 50 × 0.02 = 1

The magnitude condition is satisfied. The phase condition must also be satisfied at the same frequency.

19. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is an electronic oscillator?

A circuit that generates a periodic electrical waveform using DC power without requiring a continuous periodic input.

2. What is positive feedback?

Feedback in which the returned signal reinforces the original input signal.

3. What is loop gain?

The product of amplifier gain and feedback factor, Aβ.

4. State the Barkhausen criterion.

The loop-gain magnitude is unity and total loop phase shift is zero degrees modulo 360 degrees at the oscillation frequency.

5. Why is 360° phase shift equivalent to 0°?

Because a sinusoidal signal shifted by one complete cycle returns to the same phase.

6. What happens if loop gain is less than unity?

Oscillations decay rather than being sustained.

7. Why is loop gain initially designed above unity?

To provide start-up margin so that small initial disturbances can grow into oscillations.

8. Why is amplitude stabilization required?

To prevent unlimited growth and reduce clipping and distortion.

9. What is the difference between an amplifier and an oscillator?

An amplifier amplifies an applied signal, while an oscillator generates a periodic signal.

10. What determines the frequency of an oscillator?

The frequency-selective network and the active circuit's operating characteristics.

11. What is an RC oscillator?

An oscillator that uses resistors and capacitors to establish its frequency and feedback phase.

12. What is an LC oscillator?

An oscillator that uses an inductor-capacitor resonant network.

13. Why are crystal oscillators frequency stable?

Quartz crystals have a high-Q mechanical resonance and a well-defined resonant frequency.

14. What is the role of an oscillator in a microcontroller?

It provides the clock reference used for instruction execution and timing.

15. How can oscillator frequency be measured?

Measure the waveform period using an oscilloscope or use a frequency counter.

16. Does satisfying the Barkhausen criterion alone guarantee start-up?

No. The criterion describes the ideal sustained oscillation condition. Start-up margin, noise, nonlinearities and circuit dynamics also matter.

  5. An amplifier provides 180° phase shift and its feedback network provides 180°. Find the total phase shift.
  6. Explain why loop gain slightly greater than unity is useful during oscillator start-up.
  7. Explain the effect of temperature variation on an RC oscillator's frequency.
Show Answers

  2. T=1/500000=2 µs.
  3. Aβ=100×0.01=1.
  4. β=1/40=0.025.

  7. Resistance and capacitance can change with temperature, changing the frequency.

21. Lecture Summary

  • An oscillator generates a periodic waveform from a DC supply.
  • Positive feedback reinforces the signal around the feedback loop.
  • Loop gain is the product Aβ.
  • The Barkhausen criterion has magnitude and phase conditions.
  • Start-up generally requires loop gain slightly above unity.
  • Amplitude stabilization establishes a practical steady-state waveform.
  • RC, LC, crystal and relaxation oscillators use different frequency-generating mechanisms.
