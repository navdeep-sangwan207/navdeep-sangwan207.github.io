2. What is an RC Phase-Shift Oscillator?

An RC phase-shift oscillator is a sinusoidal oscillator that uses a resistor-capacitor (RC) feedback network and an inverting amplifier to generate a sinusoidal output.

It is commonly used for generating low and audio-frequency signals. The RC network determines the oscillation frequency.

Important: The RC network provides approximately 180 degrees of phase shift, while the inverting amplifier provides another 180 degrees. The total phase shift is 360 degrees.

3. Circuit Configuration

A typical RC phase-shift oscillator consists of:

  • An inverting amplifier, commonly built using a transistor or op-amp.
  • Three cascaded RC sections forming the feedback network.
  • A DC power supply for the amplifier.

4. Working Principle

  1. When power is switched ON, a small initial signal is generated due to noise and circuit transients.
  2. The inverting amplifier amplifies the signal and introduces a phase shift of 180 degrees.
  3. The three-section RC feedback network introduces another 180-degree phase shift at the oscillation frequency.
  4. The total phase shift becomes 360 degrees, so the feedback signal reinforces the input signal.
  5. The oscillation amplitude grows initially and is eventually limited by the amplifier's nonlinear behavior or an amplitude-control mechanism.

5. Phase Shift in RC Network

Each RC section contributes part of the total phase shift. For a commonly used three-section equal-component network, the network provides 180 degrees of phase shift at the oscillation frequency.

Part Phase Shift
RC feedback network 180 degrees
Inverting amplifier 180 degrees
Total 360 degrees

The feedback network also attenuates the signal. Therefore, the amplifier must provide sufficient gain to compensate for this attenuation.

6. Frequency of Oscillation

For a conventional three-section RC phase-shift oscillator with equal resistors and capacitors, the oscillation frequency is approximately:

Frequency Formula:

f = 1 / (2πRC√6)

Where:
f = Frequency of oscillation in Hz
R = Resistance in ohms
C = Capacitance in farads

This formula assumes a standard three-section network with equal R and C values and the usual idealized amplifier model. Loading and practical amplifier characteristics can affect the actual frequency.

7. Minimum Amplifier Gain

The RC feedback network attenuates the output signal. In the conventional equal-component three-section design, its feedback factor magnitude at the oscillation frequency is approximately 1/29.

|β| = 1/29

Barkhausen magnitude condition:
|Aβ| = 1

A × 1/29 = 1

A = 29

Therefore, the ideal amplifier gain must be at least 29. In practice, slightly higher startup gain is generally needed, followed by amplitude stabilization.

Placement point: Remember the two important values for the conventional three-section RC phase-shift oscillator: phase shift of 180 degrees from the RC network and minimum ideal amplifier gain of 29.

8. Numerical Example

Question: Calculate the frequency of oscillation if each resistor is 10 kΩ and each capacitor is 0.01 µF.

Given:

  • R = 10 kΩ = 10,000 Ω
  • C = 0.01 µF = 0.01 × 10-6 F
f = 1 / (2πRC√6)

f = 1 / (2π × 10,000 × 0.01 × 10-6 × √6)

f ≈ 649.7 Hz

Answer: Approximately 650 Hz

9. Advantages

  • Simple circuit design.
  • Does not require inductors.
  • Suitable for low and audio-frequency signal generation.
  • Relatively inexpensive components.
  • Useful for laboratory signal generation.

10. Disadvantages

  • Requires relatively high amplifier gain.
  • Frequency stability is generally lower than that of crystal oscillators.
  • Frequency is affected by component tolerances and temperature.
  • Not generally preferred for very high-frequency applications.
  • Amplitude stabilization may require additional circuitry.

11. Applications

  • Audio-frequency signal generators.
  • Low-frequency laboratory oscillators.
  • Electronic testing equipment.
  • Educational oscillator circuits.
  • Signal generation in analog electronic systems.
