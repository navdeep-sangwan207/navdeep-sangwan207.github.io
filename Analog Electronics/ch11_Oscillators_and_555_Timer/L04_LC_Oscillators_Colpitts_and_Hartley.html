2. LC Oscillator Fundamentals

An LC oscillator generates a periodic electrical signal using an inductor (L) and capacitor (C) as its frequency-determining network. The inductor and capacitor form a resonant tank circuit.

Capacitor energy

A capacitor stores energy in its electric field.

EC = ½ CV²

Energy is maximum when capacitor voltage is at its maximum magnitude.

Inductor energy

An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field.

EL = ½ LI²

Energy is maximum when inductor current is at its maximum magnitude.

Energy exchange in an ideal LC tank

Initially charged capacitor energy drives current through the inductor. The capacitor discharges, magnetic energy builds in the inductor, and the inductor current continues charging the capacitor with opposite polarity. This exchange repeats periodically.

f0 = 1 / (2π√LC)

Here, L is in henries, C is in farads and the resonant frequency is in hertz.

Important: An ideal LC tank produces a natural resonant response, but a practical oscillator needs an amplifier and a positive-feedback path to compensate for losses and sustain oscillations.

3. Resonance and Practical LC Oscillators

At resonance, the inductive and capacitive reactances have equal magnitudes:

XL = 2πfL    and    XC = 1/(2πfC)

Equating them gives the resonant-frequency equation:

f0 = 1/(2π√LC)

What happens in a practical tank?

  • Winding resistance in the inductor causes copper losses.
  • Capacitor equivalent series resistance (ESR) causes additional losses.
  • Parasitic capacitance and inductance shift the resonant frequency.
  • Connected amplifier stages and measurement probes load the tank.
  • The amplifier and feedback network must provide enough energy to replace the losses.

Quality factor (Q)

The quality factor describes the sharpness of resonance and the ratio of stored energy to energy dissipated per cycle, using the appropriate resonator definition.

Q = 2π × (Energy stored / Energy dissipated per cycle)

A higher Q generally means lower losses and a narrower resonant bandwidth. For a simple series RLC circuit at resonance:

Q = ω0L/R = 1/(ω0CR)
The series-RLC Q equation must not be applied blindly to every oscillator topology. The effective resistance and loading depend on the actual circuit.

4. Colpitts Oscillator

The Colpitts oscillator uses a capacitive voltage divider to provide feedback. Two capacitors, C1 and C2, are connected in series as the capacitive portion of the resonant tank, with an inductor L forming the resonant network.

Equivalent capacitance

Since C1 and C2 are in series, their equivalent capacitance is:

Ceq = (C1 × C2)/(C1 + C2)

The approximate resonant frequency is:

f0 = 1/(2π√(L × Ceq))

Feedback principle

  • The two capacitors form a capacitive divider.
  • The divider tap provides a fraction of the tank voltage as feedback.
  • An amplifier, commonly a transistor stage, supplies the energy needed to compensate for tank losses.
  • The amplifier and feedback network must produce the correct overall phase for positive feedback.
Design caution: The exact feedback fraction depends on the amplifier connection, capacitor orientation and loading. Do not assume a universal feedback ratio without analyzing the specific circuit.

Colpitts applications

  • RF signal generation and local oscillators.
  • Radio-frequency communication circuits.
  • Frequency sources in electronic instruments.
  • RF oscillator stages in transmitters and receivers.

6. Hartley Oscillator

The Hartley oscillator uses an inductive divider for feedback. Its resonant tank contains a capacitor and two inductive sections, which may be separate coupled coils or a tapped inductor.

Equivalent inductance

For two series-aiding coupled inductors:

Leq = L1 + L2 + 2M

For ideal uncoupled series inductors, mutual inductance M is zero:

Leq = L1 + L2

The approximate resonant frequency is:

f0 = 1/(2π√(LeqC))

Mutual inductance and dot convention

When two inductors are magnetically coupled, the mutual inductance term depends on winding polarity and the direction of current reference.

  • Series aiding: The mutual fluxes reinforce each other, so the equivalent inductance includes +2M.
  • Series opposing: The mutual fluxes oppose each other, so the equivalent inductance includes −2M.
  • Uncoupled coils: M is approximately zero, giving L1 + L2 for series-connected inductors.
The +2M equation applies to the series-aiding reference convention. Check the dot markings and winding connections before using it in a real circuit.

Feedback principle

The tapped inductor provides a fraction of the tank voltage to the amplifier input. The amplifier restores energy lost in the tank. The actual feedback polarity and magnitude depend on the transistor configuration, winding polarity and connection of the tap.

Hartley applications

  • RF oscillators and tuning circuits.
  • Radio receivers and local oscillator stages.
  • Variable-frequency signal generators.
  • Communication and instrumentation circuits.

Calculation method

Mutual inductance:

M = k√(L1L2)

Series-aiding equivalent inductance:

Leq = L1 + L2 + 2M

Resonant frequency:

f0 = 1/(2π√(LeqC))

For uncoupled coils, set k = 0. For series-opposing coupling, use the minus sign before 2M instead.

10. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Colpitts oscillator

Given L = 10 µH, C1 = 100 pF and C2 = 100 pF, calculate the ideal resonant frequency.

  1. Equivalent capacitance = (100 × 100)/(100 + 100) = 50 pF.
  2. Convert values: L = 10 × 10−6 H and C = 50 × 10−12 F.
  3. Apply f = 1/(2π√LC).
Answer: Approximately 7.12 MHz.

Problem 2: Colpitts with unequal capacitors

Given C1 = 150 pF, C2 = 300 pF and L = 20 µH:

  • Ceq = (150 × 300)/(150 + 300) = 100 pF.
  • f = 1/(2π√(20 × 10−6 × 100 × 10−12)).
Answer: Approximately 3.56 MHz.

Problem 3: Hartley oscillator without mutual coupling

Given L1 = 20 µH, L2 = 30 µH and C = 100 pF. Assume uncoupled, series-connected inductors.

  • Leq = L1 + L2 = 50 µH.
  • f = 1/(2π√(50 × 10−6 × 100 × 10−12)).
Answer: Approximately 2.25 MHz.

Problem 4: Hartley oscillator with mutual coupling

Given L1 = 20 µH, L2 = 30 µH, k = 0.5 and C = 100 pF. Assume series-aiding coupling.

  • M = k√(L1L2) = 0.5√(20 × 30) ≈ 12.25 µH.
  • Leq = 20 + 30 + 2(12.25) = 74.5 µH approximately.
  • f = 1/(2π√(74.5 × 10−6 × 100 × 10−12)).
Answer: Approximately 1.84 MHz.

11. Practical Design Considerations

Component tolerance

Inductor and capacitor tolerances cause frequency variation. Select stable components for frequency-sensitive circuits.

Parasitic capacitance

Transistor junction capacitance, PCB tracks and wiring add capacitance to the tank and can lower its resonant frequency.

Tank loading

Connecting a low-impedance load or test probe may reduce Q and shift the oscillation frequency.

Temperature drift

Inductance and capacitance can vary with temperature, causing oscillator frequency drift.

Amplifier gain

The active stage must provide sufficient loop gain to start and sustain oscillation without excessive distortion.

PCB layout

Use short RF connections, a suitable ground return and careful component placement around the resonant tank.

Frequency sensitivity

For an ideal LC resonator, small changes in L and C approximately produce:

Δf/f ≈ −½(ΔL/L + ΔC/C)

For example, if L increases by 2% and C stays constant, the ideal frequency decreases by approximately 1%.

12. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Study the frequency-determining tank of Colpitts and Hartley oscillators and compare calculated and measured resonant frequencies.

Equipment and components

  • Oscilloscope with suitable bandwidth and probe.
  • Function generator or suitable oscillator test circuit.
  • Inductors and stable capacitors of known values.
  • Transistor oscillator circuit or a verified oscillator module.
  • Digital multimeter and component measurement equipment.
  • Current-limited DC power supply.

Procedure

  1. Identify the inductor and capacitor values from component markings or measurements.
  2. For the Colpitts tank, calculate C1 and C2 series-equivalent capacitance.
  3. Calculate the theoretical Colpitts resonant frequency.
  4. For the Hartley tank, identify the inductor connection and determine whether mutual coupling is significant.
  5. Calculate the Hartley equivalent inductance using the appropriate coupling assumption.
  6. Calculate the theoretical Hartley resonant frequency.
  7. Use a verified oscillator circuit and apply its specified supply voltage with current limiting.
  8. Observe the output waveform with a suitable oscilloscope probe.
  9. Measure frequency and compare it with the calculated value.
  10. Change one tank component at a time and record the frequency shift.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is an LC oscillator?

An oscillator that uses an inductor-capacitor resonant network to determine its oscillation frequency.

Q2. What is the resonant-frequency equation?

f0 = 1/(2π√LC), for an ideal LC tank.

Q3. What is the function of the LC tank?

It determines the preferred oscillation frequency through resonance and exchanges energy between electric and magnetic fields.

Q4. Why is an amplifier required?

To compensate for energy lost in the practical resonant circuit and sustain oscillations.

Q5. What is the main difference between Colpitts and Hartley?

Colpitts uses a capacitive divider for feedback, whereas Hartley uses an inductive divider.

Q6. How is equivalent capacitance calculated in Colpitts?

For two series capacitors, Ceq = C1C2/(C1+C2).

Q7. How is equivalent inductance calculated in Hartley?

For series-aiding coupled coils, Leq = L1+L2+2M. For uncoupled series coils, Leq = L1+L2.

Q8. What is mutual inductance?

It is the magnetic coupling effect by which changing current in one coil induces voltage in another coil.

Q9. What is the coupling coefficient?

It describes the degree of magnetic coupling between two coils. Its magnitude is between zero and one for passive coupled inductors.

Q10. What is Q factor?

Q indicates resonator quality and relates stored energy to energy dissipated per cycle.

Q11. What happens if tank capacitance increases?

The ideal resonant frequency decreases, assuming inductance remains constant.

Q12. What happens if tank inductance increases?

The ideal resonant frequency decreases, assuming capacitance remains constant.

Q13. Why can a measured frequency differ from the calculated frequency?

Because of component tolerances, parasitic elements, loading, mutual coupling and measurement effects.

Q14. Why is a high-Q tank useful?

It generally has lower resonator losses and a sharper resonance, which can improve frequency selectivity.

Q15. What is the effect of an oscilloscope probe on an LC oscillator?

Probe capacitance and loading can change the effective tank values, shift frequency or even stop oscillation.

Q16. Can an LC tank alone sustain oscillations indefinitely?

An ideal lossless tank can exchange energy indefinitely in theory. A practical tank loses energy, so an active amplifier and feedback path are required to sustain oscillation.

Q17. What is the role of positive feedback?

It returns a correctly phased portion of the output to the input so that the amplifier can compensate for tank losses.

Q18. Which oscillator uses two capacitors as a feedback divider?

The Colpitts oscillator.

Q19. Which oscillator uses a tapped inductor or two inductive sections?

The Hartley oscillator.

Q20. What is the difference between series-aiding and series-opposing coupling?

Series-aiding coupling increases equivalent inductance through +2M; series-opposing coupling reduces it through −2M.

15. Numerical Practice

1. A Colpitts oscillator has C1 = 200 pF and C2 = 200 pF. Find Ceq.
Ceq = (200 × 200)/(200 + 200) = 100 pF.
2. An LC tank has L = 25 µH and C = 100 pF. Find its ideal resonant frequency.
f0 = 1/(2π√(25 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 × 10⁻¹²)) ≈ 3.18 MHz.
3. A Hartley oscillator has uncoupled series inductors of 10 µH and 40 µH. Find Leq.
Leq = 10 + 40 = 50 µH.
4. If capacitance increases while inductance remains constant, what happens to resonant frequency?
The resonant frequency decreases because f0 is inversely proportional to the square root of capacitance.
5. Two coupled coils have L1 = 16 µH, L2 = 25 µH and k = 0.5. Find M.
M = k√(L1L2) = 0.5√(16 × 25) = 10 µH.

16. Key Takeaways

  • An LC tank exchanges energy between its capacitor and inductor.
  • The ideal resonant frequency is f0 = 1/(2π√LC).
  • Colpitts uses a capacitive feedback divider.
  • Hartley uses an inductive feedback divider.
  • For Colpitts, Ceq = C1C2/(C1+C2).
  • For Hartley series-aiding coupling, Leq = L1+L2+2M.
  • A practical oscillator requires an amplifier and correctly phased feedback.
  • Q factor, parasitics, component tolerance and loading affect practical performance.
Placement focus: Be able to derive both equivalent tank values, calculate resonant frequency, explain the feedback mechanism and diagnose why a practical oscillator may fail to start or drift.
