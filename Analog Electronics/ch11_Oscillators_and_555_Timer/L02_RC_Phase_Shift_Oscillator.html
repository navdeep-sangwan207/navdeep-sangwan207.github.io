2. Introduction

An RC phase shift oscillator is a sinusoidal oscillator that uses a resistor-capacitor feedback network and an inverting amplifier to generate a continuous sinusoidal waveform.

It is commonly used in low-frequency and audio-frequency signal generation. Unlike LC oscillators, it does not require an inductor.

Frequency Network

Three RC sections provide the required frequency-dependent phase shift.

Amplifier

A common-emitter transistor amplifier provides approximately 180° phase inversion.

Feedback

The combined phase shift around the loop becomes 360° or 0° modulo 360°.

5. Phase Shift in the RC Network

A resistor and capacitor together form a frequency-dependent network. The phase relationship between input and output changes with frequency.

For the conventional three-section phase-shift network, the total feedback-network phase shift is 180° at the oscillation frequency.

SectionNominal phase contribution
RC section 1Approximately 60°
RC section 2Approximately 60°
RC section 3Approximately 60°
TotalApproximately 180°
φRC ≈ 60° + 60° + 60° = 180°

The common-emitter amplifier provides another 180° phase inversion.

Total loop phase = 180° + 180° = 360°

6. Why Does a Common-Emitter Amplifier Provide 180°?

In a common-emitter transistor amplifier, an increase in base voltage generally increases collector current. The increased collector current produces a larger voltage drop across the collector resistor, reducing collector voltage.

Therefore, the collector output voltage changes in the opposite direction to the base input voltage.

7. Oscillation Frequency Derivation

For the conventional three-section RC phase-shift oscillator with equal resistor values and equal capacitor values, the ideal oscillation frequency is:

f = 1 / (2πRC√6)

Where:

  • f = oscillation frequency in hertz.
  • R = resistance of each equal resistor in ohms.
  • C = capacitance of each equal capacitor in farads.

The formula assumes the standard equal-component three-section network and its associated loading conditions. A different network topology can produce a different transfer function and frequency equation.

Important observations

  • Increasing R decreases oscillation frequency.
  • Increasing C decreases oscillation frequency.
  • Reducing R or C increases oscillation frequency.
  • Component tolerances cause frequency variation.

8. Amplifier Gain Requirement

The three-section RC network attenuates the feedback signal. The amplifier must provide sufficient gain to compensate for this attenuation.

For the ideal conventional equal-R, equal-C three-section phase-shift network, the feedback attenuation at the oscillation frequency is approximately 1/29.

β ≈ 1/29
|Aβ| = 1
A ≈ 29

Therefore, the ideal minimum amplifier voltage-gain magnitude is approximately 29.

A practical design normally provides some start-up gain margin above the ideal minimum. Excessive gain can increase distortion, so amplitude stabilization is important.

9. Practical Transistor Circuit Concept

A common-emitter transistor amplifier can be used as the inverting amplifier stage. A practical circuit also needs a correctly designed bias network, collector load, emitter stabilization and a properly connected feedback network.

15. Laboratory Experiment

Objective

To study the working principle and frequency response of an RC phase shift oscillator.

Equipment

  • Low-voltage DC power supply.
  • Transistor or suitable amplifier circuit.
  • Resistors and capacitors.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Oscilloscope.

Procedure

  1. Verify the transistor pin configuration using its datasheet.
  2. Construct the complete oscillator circuit using a verified schematic.
  3. Check the transistor bias and supply voltage before enabling operation.
  4. Observe the output using an oscilloscope.
  5. Measure the time period and calculate frequency.
  6. Compare the measured frequency with the theoretical value.
  7. Change an RC component and observe the frequency change.
  8. Record the output amplitude and waveform distortion.

17. Placement-Oriented Interview Questions

1. What is an RC phase shift oscillator?

A sinusoidal oscillator that uses an RC feedback network and an inverting amplifier.

2. Why are three RC sections used?

They provide the required approximately 180° phase shift in the conventional topology.

3. What is the phase shift of a common-emitter amplifier?

Approximately 180° between input and output in its normal inverting operating region.

4. What is the total loop phase shift?

Approximately 360°, equivalent to 0° modulo 360°.

5. State the frequency formula.

For the standard equal-R, equal-C three-section network: f=1/(2πRC√6).

6. What is the minimum amplifier gain?

Approximately 29 in the ideal conventional equal-component network.

7. Why is an inductor not required?

The frequency-selective network uses resistors and capacitors rather than an LC resonant tank.

8. Why can excessive gain cause distortion?

It can drive the amplifier into nonlinear operation or output clipping.

9. What happens when R increases?

The ideal oscillation frequency decreases.

10. What happens when C increases?

The ideal oscillation frequency decreases.

11. What is the role of amplitude stabilization?

It controls output amplitude and helps reduce distortion.

12. Where is an RC phase shift oscillator used?

Low-frequency and audio-frequency signal generation and laboratory test equipment.

13. What is the main limitation of an RC phase shift oscillator?

Its frequency stability and distortion performance are generally not as good as suitable crystal or high-quality Wien bridge designs.

14. Why is a buffer stage sometimes required?

To prevent the output load from disturbing the oscillator feedback network.

15. Does the ideal formula apply to every three-RC network?

No. It applies to the specified conventional equal-component topology and assumptions.
