2. Introduction to Crystal Oscillators

A crystal oscillator is an electronic oscillator that uses the mechanical resonance of a piezoelectric crystal, commonly quartz, to generate a highly stable periodic electrical signal.

Quartz crystals are used because they can provide high Q factor, good frequency stability and relatively low frequency drift compared with many ordinary LC resonators.

Common applications

  • Microcontroller clock generation.
  • Microprocessor and digital system timing.
  • Communication equipment and RF reference oscillators.
  • Real-time clocks and frequency references.
  • Digital instruments, counters and measurement systems.
  • Embedded systems and computer peripherals.
Embedded systems connection: A microcontroller clock may be generated using an external quartz crystal connected to oscillator pins. The crystal frequency helps establish the processor's timing reference, although internal clock circuits and PLLs may modify the final operating frequency.

3. Piezoelectric Effect

The piezoelectric effect is the property of certain materials to generate an electrical charge when mechanical stress is applied. The reverse piezoelectric effect occurs when an applied electric field produces mechanical deformation.

Direct piezoelectric effect

Mechanical pressure or deformation produces an electrical charge across the material.

Inverse piezoelectric effect

An applied electrical voltage causes mechanical deformation or vibration.

How quartz works in an oscillator

  1. An alternating electrical signal is applied to the electrodes of the quartz crystal.
  2. The inverse piezoelectric effect causes mechanical vibration.
  3. The crystal responds strongly near its natural mechanical resonance.
  4. Mechanical vibration produces an electrical response through the direct piezoelectric effect.
  5. The oscillator circuit uses this response to establish a stable oscillation frequency.
The animation and drawing are conceptual. A real quartz resonator has specific vibration modes, electrode geometry, mounting and package characteristics.

4. Quartz Crystal Equivalent Circuit

A quartz crystal can be represented electrically by a motional branch in parallel with a shunt capacitance.

The equivalent circuit consists of:

ComponentMeaningElectrical interpretation
RmMotional resistanceRepresents mechanical and electrical losses.
LmMotional inductanceRepresents the effective vibrating mass of the crystal system.
CmMotional capacitanceRepresents the elasticity or compliance of the resonator.
C0Shunt capacitanceRepresents capacitance between crystal electrodes and associated package effects.
The motional RLC branch is in series. The complete motional branch is in parallel with C0. This equivalent model explains the crystal's electrical resonance behavior.

5. Series Resonance

At series resonance, the reactances of Lm and Cm cancel each other. The motional branch impedance becomes approximately equal to Rm, which is its minimum impedance in the idealized model.

fs = 1/(2π√(LmCm))

At this frequency:

  • The motional inductive reactance equals the motional capacitive reactance.
  • The motional branch impedance is approximately minimum.
  • Current through the motional branch is maximum for a given applied voltage.
  • The crystal behaves approximately resistively at series resonance.

6. Parallel Resonance

Above the series-resonant frequency, the motional branch becomes inductive. Its interaction with the shunt capacitance C0 produces a parallel-resonant frequency.

fp ≈ fs√(1 + Cm/C0)

This is an approximate expression for the unloaded equivalent circuit when motional resistance is small and Cm is much smaller than C0.

  • Parallel resonance occurs slightly above series resonance.
  • The crystal impedance becomes high near parallel resonance.
  • The exact operating frequency depends on the oscillator circuit and external load capacitance.
Important distinction: A crystal may be specified for series-resonant operation or for a particular parallel load capacitance. Selecting the wrong type or load condition can cause a frequency offset.

8. Pierce Crystal Oscillator

The Pierce oscillator is widely used in microcontrollers and digital electronic systems. It typically uses an inverting amplifier, a quartz crystal in the feedback path and two capacitors connected around the crystal.

How it works

  1. The inverting amplifier provides gain and phase inversion.
  2. The quartz crystal provides a highly selective frequency-dependent feedback path.
  3. The load capacitors help establish the crystal's operating load capacitance.
  4. The circuit starts oscillating when the loop has sufficient gain and the correct phase.
  5. The crystal's resonance strongly influences the steady-state oscillation frequency.
This is a functional block-level representation, not a pin-accurate circuit for a particular microcontroller. Follow the oscillator circuit recommended in the exact device datasheet.

9. Crystal Load Capacitance

Many parallel-resonant crystals are specified for a particular load capacitance, CL. In a common Pierce arrangement, the effective external load capacitance is approximately:

CL ≈ (C1 × C2)/(C1 + C2) + Cstray

Cstray represents relevant PCB, pin, package and other parasitic capacitances. The actual load-capacitance relationship depends on the oscillator topology and the crystal manufacturer's specified measurement conditions.

Example

For C1 = 22 pF, C2 = 22 pF and estimated stray capacitance of 3 pF:

Series combination = (22 × 22)/(22 + 22) = 11 pF.

Estimated load capacitance = 11 + 3 = 14 pF.

Estimated CL = 14 pF.
If a crystal is specified for CL = 12.5 pF, the external capacitor values should be selected after accounting for the board's parasitic capacitance and the oscillator manufacturer's design guidance.

Why crystal oscillators are stable

  • Quartz has a well-defined mechanical resonance.
  • Its resonant behavior is highly selective.
  • Its high Q provides a narrow resonance response.
  • Proper crystal selection and oscillator design reduce unwanted frequency variation.

Frequency error in ppm

Frequency error (ppm) = (Measured frequency − Nominal frequency)/Nominal frequency × 106

For example, a 10 MHz crystal operating at 10.000050 MHz has a frequency error of +5 ppm.

13. Crystal Oscillator Selection and Design

Step-by-step selection process

  1. Identify the required nominal frequency.
  2. Check whether the oscillator circuit expects a series-resonant or parallel-resonant crystal.
  3. Check the specified load capacitance.
  4. Verify the crystal ESR and oscillator start-up capability.
  5. Check frequency tolerance and temperature stability requirements.
  6. Confirm the crystal's maximum drive level.
  7. Follow the microcontroller or oscillator IC manufacturer's recommended circuit.
  8. Keep crystal connections short and minimize parasitic capacitance.
  9. Verify start-up time and frequency using suitable measurement equipment.

PCB layout recommendations

  • Place the crystal close to the oscillator pins.
  • Keep oscillator traces short and avoid unnecessary vias.
  • Place load capacitors close to the crystal and use an appropriate ground return.
  • Avoid routing high-speed switching signals near sensitive oscillator connections.
  • Follow the manufacturer's recommendations for ground planes and guard rings.
  • Avoid excessive probe loading during measurement.
Do not assume every microcontroller uses the same crystal capacitor values. Check the device datasheet, oscillator mode, crystal specifications and reference layout.

14. Practical Laboratory Exercise

Objective: Study a quartz crystal oscillator, observe its output frequency and understand the effect of load capacitance and measurement loading.

Required equipment

  • Microcontroller development board or crystal oscillator test circuit.
  • Quartz crystal matching the circuit specification.
  • Oscilloscope with suitable bandwidth and probe capacitance.
  • Frequency counter, if available.
  • Digital multimeter and component datasheets.
  • Computer with the relevant development environment.

Procedure

  1. Read the crystal datasheet and note nominal frequency, load capacitance, ESR and drive-level limit.
  2. Identify the oscillator pins in the microcontroller or oscillator IC datasheet.
  3. Verify the recommended crystal and capacitor connections.
  4. Power the circuit using its specified supply voltage.
  5. Allow the oscillator to start and stabilize.
  6. Measure the clock frequency using a suitable method.
  7. Compare the measured frequency with the nominal frequency.
  8. Calculate the frequency error in ppm.
  9. Observe whether measurement loading changes the oscillator behavior.
  10. Record the results and compare them with the crystal specifications.

16. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a crystal oscillator?

An oscillator that uses the mechanical resonance of a piezoelectric crystal to establish a stable electrical oscillation frequency.

Q2. What is the piezoelectric effect?

The generation of electrical charge due to mechanical stress in certain materials.

Q3. What is the inverse piezoelectric effect?

Mechanical deformation produced when an electric field is applied to a piezoelectric material.

Q4. Why is quartz used in oscillators?

Quartz offers a stable and selective mechanical resonance with a high Q factor.

Q5. What is the crystal equivalent circuit?

A motional series RLC branch in parallel with shunt capacitance C0.

Q6. What does Rm represent?

It represents losses in the crystal's motional equivalent circuit.

Q7. What does Lm represent?

It represents the effective vibrating mass in the equivalent model.

Q8. What does Cm represent?

It represents the mechanical compliance or elasticity in the equivalent model.

Q9. What is C0?

The shunt capacitance associated with the crystal electrodes and package.

Q10. What is series resonance?

The frequency at which the motional inductive and capacitive reactances cancel, leaving approximately Rm in the motional branch.

Q11. What is parallel resonance?

The resonance produced by interaction between the motional branch and shunt capacitance C0.

Q12. Which frequency is generally higher, series or parallel resonance?

Parallel resonance is generally slightly higher than series resonance in the usual crystal equivalent model.

Q13. What is a Pierce oscillator?

A common crystal oscillator topology using an inverting amplifier, a crystal feedback path and two load capacitors.

Q14. What is load capacitance?

The effective capacitance presented to the crystal by the oscillator circuit and its parasitic elements.

Q15. Why are two capacitors used in a Pierce oscillator?

They establish the effective crystal load capacitance and participate in the oscillator feedback network.

Q16. What is ESR?

Equivalent series resistance. It represents losses and affects oscillator start-up requirements.

Q17. What is crystal drive level?

The power dissipated in the crystal during operation. It must remain within the manufacturer's specified limit.

Q18. Why does a crystal oscillator provide better frequency stability than many LC oscillators?

The quartz resonator has a high Q factor and a well-defined mechanical resonance.

Q19. Why can an oscilloscope probe stop a crystal oscillator?

The probe adds capacitance and loading to a sensitive oscillator node, potentially preventing the circuit from meeting its oscillation conditions.

Q20. What does ppm mean in frequency specifications?

Parts per million. It expresses a frequency deviation relative to the nominal frequency.

Q21. What happens if load capacitance is increased?

For common parallel-resonant crystal operation, increasing load capacitance generally pulls the operating frequency downward, within the crystal's specified pulling range.

Q22. Where are crystal oscillators used in embedded systems?

They provide clock references for microcontrollers, processors, communication interfaces and timing circuits.

17. Numerical Practice

1. A crystal has Lm = 10 mH and Cm = 0.02 pF. Calculate its series-resonant frequency.
fs = 1/(2π√(10 × 10⁻³ × 0.02 × 10⁻¹²)) ≈ 11.25 MHz.
2. If fs = 10 MHz, Cm = 0.02 pF and C0 = 3 pF, estimate fp.
fp ≈ 10 MHz × √(1 + 0.02/3) ≈ 10.0333 MHz.
3. A 16 MHz crystal operates at 16.000080 MHz. Calculate its frequency error.
Error = (80/16,000,000) × 10⁶ = +5 ppm.
4. A Pierce circuit has C1 = 18 pF, C2 = 18 pF and estimated stray capacitance = 2 pF. Estimate CL.
CL ≈ (18 × 18)/(18 + 18) + 2 = 9 + 2 = 11 pF.
5. What is the effect of excessive crystal drive level?
It can cause excessive heating, frequency shift, accelerated aging and possible crystal damage.

18. Key Takeaways

  • Quartz crystals use the piezoelectric effect to convert electrical excitation into mechanical vibration.
  • The equivalent circuit contains Rm, Lm and Cm in series, in parallel with C0.
  • Series resonance occurs when the motional inductive and capacitive reactances cancel.
  • Parallel resonance occurs due to interaction between the motional branch and C0.
  • The Pierce oscillator is widely used in microcontroller clock circuits.
  • Load capacitance, ESR and drive level are important crystal specifications.
  • PCB layout and probe loading can affect oscillator operation.
  • Frequency error is commonly expressed in ppm.
Placement focus: Be able to draw the crystal equivalent circuit, derive series resonance, explain parallel resonance, calculate load capacitance and diagnose crystal oscillator start-up problems.
