2. What is a Hartley Oscillator?

A Hartley oscillator is an LC sinusoidal oscillator that uses two inductive sections and one capacitor to form its frequency-determining tank circuit.

It uses inductive feedback to sustain oscillations and is commonly used in radio-frequency (RF) signal generation.

Important: The main identifying feature of a Hartley oscillator is its inductive divider, which consists of two series-connected inductances or a tapped inductor.

3. LC Tank Circuit

The LC tank circuit consists of inductance and capacitance. Energy is exchanged between the magnetic field of the inductor and the electric field of the capacitor.

  • The capacitor stores energy in its electric field.
  • The inductor stores energy in its magnetic field.
  • Energy transfers between these two components.
  • The circuit resonates at a frequency determined by L and C.

4. Circuit Configuration

A typical Hartley oscillator contains:

  • An amplifier, commonly a transistor or an op-amp.
  • Two inductive sections L1 and L2.
  • A capacitor C forming the resonant circuit.
  • An inductive feedback connection.
  • A DC power supply for the amplifier.

5. Working Principle

  1. When the power supply is switched ON, a small initial signal appears due to circuit noise and transients.
  2. The amplifier increases the amplitude of this signal.
  3. The LC tank circuit selects the resonant frequency.
  4. A portion of the output signal is returned through the inductive feedback network.
  5. The feedback signal has the correct phase to reinforce the input signal at the oscillation frequency.
  6. The oscillations build up and are limited to a stable amplitude by practical circuit behavior or an amplitude control mechanism.

6. Frequency of Oscillation

The resonant frequency of an ideal LC tank circuit is:

General LC Frequency Formula:

f = 1 / (2π√(LC))

For a Hartley oscillator, when the two inductors are magnetically coupled:

Ltotal = L1 + L2 + 2M

f = 1 / [2π√(C(L1 + L2 + 2M))]

Where:
L1 and L2 = Inductance values
M = Mutual inductance
C = Tank capacitance

If mutual coupling is negligible:

Ltotal ≈ L1 + L2

f ≈ 1 / [2π√(C(L1 + L2))]
The sign of the mutual-inductance term depends on the winding connection. The positive sign shown applies to the series-aiding inductance arrangement commonly used in the Hartley tank circuit.

7. Role of Mutual Inductance

Mutual inductance is the property by which a changing current in one coil induces a voltage in another magnetically coupled coil.

M = k√(L1L2)

Where:
M = Mutual inductance
k = Coefficient of coupling (0 to 1)
L1 and L2 = Individual inductances

Mutual inductance affects the effective inductance of the tank circuit and therefore changes the oscillation frequency.

8. Numerical Example 1: Frequency Calculation

Question: A Hartley oscillator has L1 = 100 µH, L2 = 200 µH and C = 0.01 µF. Assume mutual inductance is negligible. Calculate the oscillation frequency.

Given:

  • L1 = 100 × 10-6 H
  • L2 = 200 × 10-6 H
  • C = 0.01 × 10-6 F
Ltotal = L1 + L2

Ltotal = 100 µH + 200 µH

Ltotal = 300 µH

f = 1 / (2π√(LC))

f = 1 / (2π√(300 × 10-6 × 0.01 × 10-6))

f ≈ 91.9 kHz

Answer: Approximately 91.9 kHz

9. Advantages

  • Simple LC oscillator configuration.
  • Suitable for radio-frequency signal generation.
  • Frequency can be adjusted using inductance or capacitance.
  • Provides a relatively wide tuning range.
  • Uses inductive feedback without requiring a separate transformer in every implementation.

10. Disadvantages

  • Frequency stability is generally lower than that of crystal oscillators.
  • Inductors can be bulky compared with capacitors and resistors.
  • Stray capacitance and inductance affect frequency.
  • Performance can be affected by coil losses and component tolerances.
  • Not ideal where extremely accurate frequency control is required.

11. Applications

  • RF signal generators.
  • Radio transmitters and receivers.
  • Local oscillator circuits in communication equipment.
  • Frequency-tunable electronic circuits.
  • Laboratory RF testing equipment.
