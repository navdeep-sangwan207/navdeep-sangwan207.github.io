Lesson 3: NPN and PNP Transistors

Understand transistor types, symbols, biasing polarity, carrier movement and practical circuit applications.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the construction of NPN and PNP transistors.
  • Identify NPN and PNP circuit symbols.
  • Understand the emitter arrow direction.
  • Compare voltage polarity and current direction.
  • Understand active-region biasing conditions.
  • Identify practical applications of both transistor types.
  • Understand how to identify transistor terminals.

2. Types of Bipolar Junction Transistors

A Bipolar Junction Transistor is classified into two types according to the arrangement of its semiconductor regions:

  1. NPN transistor.
  2. PNP transistor.

Both transistors contain three regions and two PN junctions. Their main difference is the arrangement of P-type and N-type semiconductor materials.

Conceptual semiconductor arrangements of NPN and PNP transistors.

3. NPN Transistor

An NPN transistor consists of:

  • N-type emitter.
  • P-type base.
  • N-type collector.

Electrons are the majority carriers in the emitter and collector regions.

3.1 NPN Circuit Symbol

Conceptual NPN symbol. The emitter arrow points outward.

Remember: In an NPN transistor, the emitter arrow points out. Conventional emitter current flows out of the emitter.

3.2 NPN Active-Region Biasing

For an NPN transistor operating in the forward-active region:

  • Base voltage is higher than emitter voltage.
  • Collector voltage is higher than base voltage.
  • Emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • Collector-base junction is reverse biased.
VC > VB > VE

For a silicon transistor, the base-emitter voltage is commonly around 0.6–0.7 V under typical forward-active operating conditions. The exact value depends on current, temperature and device characteristics.

3.3 Current Direction in NPN

  • Conventional base current enters the base.
  • Conventional collector current enters the collector.
  • Conventional emitter current leaves the emitter.
  • Electrons move mainly from emitter toward collector.

4. PNP Transistor

A PNP transistor consists of:

  • P-type emitter.
  • N-type base.
  • P-type collector.

Holes are the majority carriers in the emitter and collector regions.

4.1 PNP Circuit Symbol

Conceptual PNP symbol. The emitter arrow points inward.

Remember: In a PNP transistor, the emitter arrow points in. Conventional emitter current flows into the emitter.

4.2 PNP Active-Region Biasing

For a PNP transistor operating in the forward-active region:

  • Emitter voltage is higher than base voltage.
  • Base voltage is higher than collector voltage.
  • Emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • Collector-base junction is reverse biased.
VE > VB > VC

4.3 Current Direction in PNP

  • Conventional emitter current enters the emitter.
  • Conventional collector current leaves the collector.
  • Conventional base current leaves the base.
  • Holes move mainly from emitter toward collector.

5. Difference Between NPN and PNP Transistors

Parameter NPN PNP
Structure N-P-N P-N-P
Majority carriers Electrons Holes
Emitter arrow Points outward Points inward
Active-region voltage order VC > VB > VE VE > VB > VC
Base-emitter polarity Base positive relative to emitter Base negative relative to emitter
Conventional emitter current Out of emitter Into emitter
Common switching use Low-side switching High-side switching
The voltage order shown applies to forward-active operation. Actual voltages depend on the circuit and transistor operating point.

6. Practical Application: NPN as a Low-Side Switch

An NPN transistor is commonly used as a low-side switch. The load is connected between the positive supply and the transistor collector.

Simplified functional illustration of an NPN low-side switch.

When sufficient base current is applied, the transistor enters saturation and current flows through the load. When base drive is removed, the transistor enters cutoff and the load turns OFF.

Practical applications: LED drivers, relay drivers, small motor drivers and digital output switching.

7. Practical Application: PNP as a High-Side Switch

A PNP transistor can be used as a high-side switch. Its emitter is connected to the positive supply and its collector supplies the load.

  • When the base is sufficiently lower than the emitter voltage, the transistor turns ON.
  • When the base is brought close to the emitter voltage, the transistor turns OFF.
A PNP high-side switch may require a suitable base-drive circuit, especially when controlled by a low-voltage microcontroller. The base current must be limited by an appropriate resistor.

8. How to Identify NPN and PNP Transistors

Method 1: Check the Part Number

Read the transistor's part number and consult its datasheet. The datasheet identifies the transistor type, pin configuration, electrical ratings and maximum operating limits.

Method 2: Use a Digital Multimeter

A multimeter with diode-test mode can help identify the base terminal and distinguish NPN from PNP devices.

  • For a typical NPN transistor, the red probe on the base and black probe on either other terminal may show a forward junction voltage.
  • For a typical PNP transistor, the black probe on the base and red probe on either other terminal may show a forward junction voltage.
Disconnect the transistor from the circuit before testing. In-circuit components can affect readings. Confirm the pinout using the datasheet; package appearance alone does not guarantee terminal order.

9. Numerical Example

An NPN transistor has a collector current of 20 mA and a base current of 0.2 mA. Calculate its emitter current.

IE = IC + IB

IE = 20 + 0.2

IE = 20.2 mA

This is the magnitude relationship between the three terminal currents.

10. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What are the two types of BJT?

NPN and PNP.

Q2. Which carrier is dominant in an NPN transistor?

Electrons.

Q3. Which carrier is dominant in a PNP transistor?

Holes.

Q4. How can you identify an NPN transistor from its symbol?

The emitter arrow points outward.

Q5. How can you identify a PNP transistor from its symbol?

The emitter arrow points inward.

Q6. What are the active-region biasing conditions for NPN?

The E-B junction is forward biased and the C-B junction is reverse biased.

Q7. Why is an NPN transistor commonly used as a low-side switch?

It can conveniently switch a load connected between the positive supply and the transistor collector.

Q8. How is a PNP transistor turned ON?

Its base is driven sufficiently below its emitter voltage to forward bias the emitter-base junction.

Q9. Can a PNP transistor be used as an amplifier?

Yes. Both NPN and PNP transistors can amplify signals.

Q10. Are NPN and PNP transistors interchangeable?

Not directly. Their polarity, current direction and circuit connections differ, although complementary devices can perform corresponding functions.

12. Lesson Summary

  • NPN has an N-P-N structure; PNP has a P-N-P structure.
  • Electrons are the majority carriers in NPN transistors.
  • Holes are the majority carriers in PNP transistors.
  • NPN emitter arrow points outward.
  • PNP emitter arrow points inward.
  • For active operation, the E-B junction is forward biased and C-B junction is reverse biased.
  • NPN transistors are commonly used for low-side switching.
  • PNP transistors are commonly used for high-side switching.
  • Both types can be used for amplification and switching.
