2. Introduction to BJT Operating Regions

The operating region of a BJT depends primarily on the biasing of its emitter-base and collector-base junctions.

A transistor has four commonly described operating regions:

Cutoff

OFF state

Both junctions are reverse biased, or the emitter-base junction is not sufficiently forward biased. Collector current is approximately zero apart from leakage.

Forward-active

Amplification

Emitter-base junction is forward biased and collector-base junction is reverse biased.

Saturation

ON state

Both junctions are forward biased. The transistor is driven strongly on and collector current is limited mainly by the external circuit.

Reverse-active

Uncommon

Emitter-base junction is reverse biased and collector-base junction is forward biased. The emitter and collector effectively exchange roles, but performance is generally poor.

4. Cutoff Region

In cutoff, the base-emitter junction is not sufficiently forward biased to produce significant carrier injection.

  • Base current is approximately zero.
  • Collector current is approximately zero, apart from leakage.
  • The transistor behaves approximately like an open switch between collector and emitter.
  • In a typical NPN common-emitter circuit, collector voltage rises toward the supply voltage because collector current is negligible.
IB ≈ 0    and    IC ≈ 0

Cutoff in a switching circuit

When the input signal is low, insufficient base drive is provided. The transistor remains OFF and the load current is nearly zero.

5. Forward-Active Region

In forward-active operation, the emitter-base junction is forward biased and the collector-base junction is reverse biased.

  • The emitter injects majority carriers into the base.
  • Most carriers cross the thin base region.
  • The collector-base electric field collects these carriers.
  • Collector current is controlled primarily by base drive and transistor characteristics.
IC ≈ β IB

Here, β is the common-emitter DC current gain in the specified operating conditions. It varies with collector current, temperature and device characteristics.

Forward-active mode is used in linear amplifiers. The transistor should be biased away from cutoff and saturation to allow signal variations in both directions.

6. Saturation Region

In saturation, both the emitter-base and collector-base junctions are forward biased.

  • The transistor is strongly driven ON.
  • Increasing base current does not necessarily produce the proportional increase in collector current predicted by the active-mode equation.
  • Collector current is mainly limited by the supply, load and external circuit resistance.
  • Collector-emitter voltage becomes relatively low.
VCE(sat) is typically around 0.1–0.3 V for many small silicon switching BJTs, depending on device and test current.
Do not use IC = βIB as a precise saturation-current equation. In saturation, collector current is limited by the external load circuit, not by the active-region gain relationship.

7. Reverse-Active Region

In reverse-active operation, the emitter-base junction is reverse biased and the collector-base junction is forward biased.

  • The emitter and collector exchange their effective carrier-injection and collection roles.
  • Current gain is normally much lower than in forward-active operation.
  • Most practical transistors are not optimized for this mode.
  • Reverse-active operation is rarely used in ordinary amplifier and switching circuits.

9. BJT as a Switch

A BJT can be used as an electronic switch by operating it mainly in cutoff and saturation.

OFF: Cutoff

Base drive is removed or insufficient. Collector current is nearly zero and the load is OFF.

ON: Saturation

A sufficient base current is applied. The transistor conducts and the load receives current.

Common-emitter NPN switch

Conceptual low-side switching arrangement. A practical circuit needs a base resistor; inductive loads such as relays also require an appropriate flyback diode.

10. DC Load Line Concept

The DC load line represents the combinations of collector current and collector-emitter voltage allowed by the external collector circuit.

For a common-emitter circuit with a collector resistor:

VCC = ICRC + VCE

Rearranging:

IC = (VCC − VCE)/RC

The Q-point is the quiescent operating point, meaning the DC operating condition when no input signal is applied.

  • At cutoff: collector current is approximately zero and VCE approaches VCC.
  • At saturation: VCE is low and collector current is limited by the collector circuit.
  • For linear amplification: the Q-point is chosen within the forward-active region with adequate signal swing.

12. Worked Numerical Examples

Example 1: Collector current in the active region

An NPN transistor has β = 100 and base current of 30 µA. Assuming forward-active operation, calculate collector current.

IC ≈ βIB = 100 × 30 µA = 3 mA

Answer: Approximate collector current is 3 mA, provided the supply and load permit this current without saturation.

Example 2: Maximum collector current at saturation

A common-emitter circuit has VCC = 10 V and RC = 1 kΩ. Assume VCE(sat) = 0.2 V. Calculate the approximate collector current.

IC(sat) ≈ (10 − 0.2)/1000 = 9.8 mA

Answer: Approximately 9.8 mA, assuming the transistor and resistor ratings allow this operating condition.

14. Practical Lab Exercise

Experiment: Observe Cutoff, Active and Saturation Regions

Objective: To observe how changing base drive changes the operating region of an NPN transistor.

Components:

  • BC547 or 2N2222 NPN transistor.
  • 12 V regulated DC supply.
  • 1 kΩ collector resistor.
  • Suitable base resistor or adjustable base-drive network.
  • Digital multimeter and breadboard.

Procedure:

  1. Verify the transistor pinout using its datasheet.
  2. Connect the emitter to ground and the collector to +12 V through the collector resistor.
  3. Connect the base to a controllable input through a suitable base resistor.
  4. With the base input low, measure VCE and collector current.
  5. Increase base drive gradually and record the measurements.
  6. Observe the transition into forward-active operation.
  7. Increase base drive sufficiently to reach saturation, without exceeding device ratings.

Expected observations:

ConditionExpected behavior
Base drive absentCutoff; IC is very small and VCE is near VCC
Controlled base driveForward-active; collector current responds to base drive
Strong base driveSaturation; VCE becomes relatively low and IC is limited by RC and VCC
Use a base resistor and check the maximum base current, collector current and power dissipation. Never increase base drive without current limiting.

16. Placement Interview Questions

Q1. Name the four operating regions of a BJT.

Cutoff, forward-active, saturation and reverse-active.

Q2. Which region is used for amplification?

Forward-active region.

Q3. Which regions are used for switching?

Cutoff and saturation.

Q4. What are the junction conditions in forward-active mode?

Emitter-base forward biased and collector-base reverse biased.

Q5. What are the junction conditions in saturation?

Both emitter-base and collector-base junctions are forward biased.

Q6. What is the cutoff condition?

The emitter-base junction is not sufficiently forward biased and collector current is approximately zero apart from leakage.

Q7. What is VCE(sat)?

The collector-emitter voltage under specified saturated operating conditions.

Q8. Is collector current equal to βIB in saturation?

Not as a reliable operating equation. In saturation, current is limited mainly by the external circuit.

Q9. What is a Q-point?

The DC quiescent operating point of the transistor without an applied signal.

Q10. What is a DC load line?

A graphical representation of the collector current and collector-emitter voltage combinations allowed by the external collector circuit.

Q11. What happens to VCE in cutoff in a simple NPN common-emitter circuit?

It approaches VCC because collector current and the resistor voltage drop are approximately zero.

Q12. What happens to VCE in saturation?

It becomes relatively low, typically a few tenths of a volt for many small switching BJTs under specified conditions.

Q13. Why is reverse-active mode rarely used?

Most transistors have much lower reverse current gain and are not optimized for this mode.

Q14. Why should an amplifier Q-point be away from cutoff and saturation?

To allow the output signal to swing without clipping or entering nonlinear operating regions.

Q15. What determines the maximum collector current in a saturated switching circuit?

The supply voltage, load impedance, collector circuit and transistor limits.

19. Lecture Summary

  • Cutoff: transistor OFF, collector current approximately zero.
  • Forward-active: emitter-base forward biased and collector-base reverse biased; used for amplification.
  • Saturation: both junctions forward biased; used for switching ON.
  • Reverse-active: collector-base forward biased and emitter-base reverse biased; rarely used.
  • The DC load line describes the external circuit constraints on IC and VCE.
  • The Q-point is the transistor's no-signal DC operating point.
