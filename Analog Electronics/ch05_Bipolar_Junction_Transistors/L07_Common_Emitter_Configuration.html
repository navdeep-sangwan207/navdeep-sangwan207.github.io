2. What Is Common-Emitter Configuration?

In a common-emitter configuration, the emitter terminal is common to both the input and output circuits. The input signal is applied between the base and emitter, while the output is taken between the collector and emitter.

3. NPN Transistor Operation in CE Mode

For forward-active operation of an NPN transistor:

  • The base-emitter junction is forward biased.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased.
  • A small base current controls a larger collector current.
  • Conventional current enters the base and collector and leaves the emitter.
IE = IC + IB

In a simplified active-region model:

IC ≈ β IB

4. Current Gain in Common-Emitter Configuration

The CE current gain is represented by beta (β).

β = IC / IB

Relationship between alpha and beta:

β = α / (1 − α)
α = β / (β + 1)

Example: If α = 0.98,

β = 0.98 / (1 − 0.98) = 49

5. Input Characteristics of CE Configuration

The input characteristic is a graph of base current (IB) versus base-emitter voltage (VBE) at a constant collector-emitter voltage (VCE).

  • The curve resembles a forward-biased diode characteristic.
  • Base current rises rapidly after the base-emitter junction begins conducting.
  • For a silicon transistor, VBE is commonly around 0.6–0.8 V under typical operating conditions.
  • The exact value depends on current, temperature and transistor construction.

6. Output Characteristics of CE Configuration

The output characteristic is a graph of collector current (IC) versus collector-emitter voltage (VCE) for different fixed values of base current (IB).

In the active region, collector current is approximately controlled by base current and is relatively insensitive to VCE. The Early effect causes a small slope in practical output characteristics.

7. DC Load Line and Q-Point

The DC load line describes all possible combinations of collector current and collector-emitter voltage allowed by the collector supply and collector resistor.

VCC = ICRC + VCE
VCE = VCC − ICRC

Load-line intercepts

  • At IC = 0: VCE = VCC.
  • At VCE = 0 (idealized): IC = VCC / RC.
  • The practical saturation endpoint is near VCE(sat), rather than exactly zero.

The Q-point is the quiescent DC operating point, specified by (ICQ, VCEQ). For undistorted signal amplification, the Q-point is normally selected in the forward-active region with adequate signal swing in both directions.

8. Phase Reversal in a CE Amplifier

A common-emitter voltage amplifier produces an output voltage that is approximately 180° out of phase with its input voltage in the normal inverting amplifier configuration.

Why does phase reversal occur?

  1. When the input voltage increases, base current increases.
  2. Collector current increases.
  3. The voltage drop across RC increases.
  4. Collector voltage decreases.
  5. Therefore, the output voltage moves in the opposite direction.
Input signal

Small positive-going voltage variation at the base.

Output signal

Amplified negative-going voltage variation at the collector.

9. CE Amplifier Circuit and Voltage Gain

A practical voltage-amplifier stage commonly contains:

  • R1 and R2: base voltage-divider bias network.
  • RC: collector resistor.
  • RE: emitter stabilization resistor.
  • CE: emitter bypass capacitor, if used.
  • CIN: input coupling capacitor.
  • COUT: output coupling capacitor.
  • Q1: NPN transistor.

Small-signal voltage gain

For a suitably biased CE amplifier, a common approximation is:

Av ≈ −gm(RC || RL)

where:

  • gm = ICQ / VT is the transistor transconductance.
  • VT is the thermal voltage, approximately 25.9 mV at room temperature.
  • RC || RL is the parallel combination of collector and load resistances.
  • The negative sign represents phase inversion.

For a simplified model with an unbypassed emitter resistance, the gain is reduced by emitter degeneration. The actual gain also depends on the bias point, transistor output resistance, source resistance, coupling capacitors and load.

gm ≈ ICQ / 25.9 mV

11. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Calculate collector current and VCE

Given: VCC = 12 V, RC = 1 kΩ, β = 100, IB = 40 µA.

IC = βIB = 100 × 40 µA = 4 mA
VRC = ICRC = 4 mA × 1 kΩ = 4 V
VCE = VCC − VRC = 12 − 4 = 8 V

Answer: IC = 4 mA and VCE = 8 V. The transistor is in the forward-active region under the simplified model.

Problem 2: Determine whether the transistor is saturated

Given: VCC = 12 V, RC = 1 kΩ, β = 100, IB = 150 µA.

IC(ideal) = 100 × 150 µA = 15 mA
IC(load-line limit) ≈ (12 − 0.2) / 1 kΩ
IC(load-line limit) ≈ 11.8 mA

The theoretical active-region current exceeds the load-line limit. The transistor is driven into saturation; actual current is limited by the collector circuit.

Problem 3: Calculate beta

Given IC = 3.6 mA and IB = 30 µA.

β = 3.6 mA / 30 µA = 120

12. Practical Laboratory Experiment

Objective

To construct a common-emitter transistor circuit and observe its input, output and phase-inversion behaviour.

Suggested components

  • BC547 or another suitable small-signal NPN transistor.
  • Resistors for voltage-divider bias and collector/emitter circuits.
  • Electrolytic coupling and bypass capacitors as required.
  • Regulated DC power supply.
  • Function generator and two-channel oscilloscope.
  • Digital multimeter and breadboard.

Procedure

  1. Identify the transistor terminals using its datasheet.
  2. Construct the voltage-divider biased CE amplifier.
  3. Apply the specified DC supply with the input signal disconnected.
  4. Measure base, emitter and collector DC voltages.
  5. Calculate IC from the emitter or collector measurement, accounting for the circuit topology.
  6. Apply a small sinusoidal input signal through the coupling capacitor.
  7. Observe input and output signals simultaneously on the oscilloscope.
  8. Verify the approximate 180° phase difference.
  9. Increase input amplitude gradually and observe the onset of clipping.

Do not exceed the transistor's absolute maximum ratings. Check pinout, supply polarity, resistor power ratings and capacitor polarity before powering the circuit. Keep the input small enough to avoid unintended saturation or cutoff during linear-amplifier measurements.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a common-emitter configuration?
A BJT configuration in which the emitter is common to the input and output circuits.
2. Which terminals form the input?
Base and emitter.
3. Which terminals form the output?
Collector and emitter.
4. Define beta.
The CE current gain, β = IC/IB, under specified operating conditions.
5. What is the relationship between alpha and beta?
β = α/(1−α), and α = β/(β+1).
6. Why is the CE output inverted?
An increase in base current increases collector current and the RC voltage drop, thereby decreasing collector voltage.
7. What is the phase difference between input and output?
Approximately 180° for a conventional inverting CE voltage amplifier.
8. What is a Q-point?
The quiescent DC operating point, represented by ICQ and VCEQ.
9. What is a DC load line?
The straight-line relationship between IC and VCE imposed by the collector supply and collector resistance.
10. Why is the transistor biased in the active region for amplification?
To allow the collector current to vary approximately linearly with the input signal without entering cutoff or saturation.
11. What happens when the transistor enters cutoff?
Collector current becomes very small and the collector voltage approaches VCC in a simple resistor-loaded stage.
12. What happens in saturation?
The transistor is strongly ON; VCE falls close to its saturation voltage and is no longer controlled by the active-region beta model.
13. What is the typical silicon base-emitter voltage?
Often approximately 0.6–0.8 V, depending on operating current and temperature.
14. What is the function of the emitter resistor?
It provides negative feedback that improves bias and thermal stability.
15. What is the function of an emitter bypass capacitor?
It reduces AC emitter degeneration over the frequency range where its reactance is sufficiently low.
16. Why are coupling capacitors used?
They pass AC signals while blocking DC between circuit stages.
17. What is the Early effect?
The dependence of collector current on collector-base reverse bias due to a change in effective base width.
18. Why does beta vary?
Beta varies with transistor sample, collector current, temperature and collector-emitter voltage.
19. What is the transconductance of a BJT?
gm = IC/VT, where VT is the thermal voltage.
20. Where is the CE configuration commonly used?
Voltage amplifiers, audio amplifier stages and general-purpose signal-conditioning circuits.
