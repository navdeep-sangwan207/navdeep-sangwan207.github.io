2. What Is Common-Collector Configuration?

In a common-collector configuration, the collector terminal is common to both the input and output circuits. The input is applied between base and collector, while the output is taken between emitter and collector.

Why is it called an emitter follower?

The emitter voltage follows changes in the base voltage. For a conducting silicon NPN transistor, the emitter voltage is approximately one base-emitter junction drop below the base voltage.

VE ≈ VB − VBE
VBE ≈ 0.6 to 0.8 V (typical silicon approximation)

3. Circuit Operation

Consider an NPN emitter follower:

  1. The collector is connected to the positive supply.
  2. The base receives a DC bias and an AC input signal.
  3. The emitter develops an output voltage that follows the base voltage.
  4. An emitter resistor or load provides the emitter-current path.
  5. When the base voltage rises, emitter voltage rises and emitter current increases.
  6. When the base voltage falls, emitter voltage and emitter current decrease.
IE = IC + IB
IE ≈ (β + 1) IB

Animated emitter-follower operation

The two voltages vary together and are in phase. Their DC levels are different by approximately VBE. The AC voltage gain is slightly less than unity in a typical emitter follower.

4. Voltage Gain of Common-Collector Configuration

The voltage gain is the ratio of output voltage variation to input voltage variation.

Av = Vout / Vin

For a conventional emitter follower, voltage gain is positive and slightly less than 1.

One useful small-signal approximation is:

Av ≈ RE' / (re + RE')

where:

  • re ≈ 25.9 mV / IE at room temperature.
  • RE' is the effective AC emitter resistance, including the external emitter load and bias resistance as applicable.

The exact gain depends on the source resistance, transistor small-signal parameters, bias network, emitter resistor, load and frequency. The approximation above assumes a simplified small-signal model.

5. Current Gain

The common-collector current gain is approximately the ratio of emitter current to base current.

Ai ≈ IE / IB
Ai ≈ β + 1

The emitter current is larger than the base current. This allows the emitter follower to provide substantial current gain even though its voltage gain is slightly less than unity.

6. Input and Output Impedance

High input impedance

The base presents relatively high impedance to the signal source. This reduces loading on the preceding circuit.

Low output impedance

The emitter output can drive a comparatively lower-impedance load than the input source can directly drive.

This combination makes the common-collector configuration useful as a buffer or impedance-matching stage.

For a simplified emitter follower, the input resistance looking into the base is approximately:

Rin(base) ≈ rπ + (β + 1)RE'

The output impedance is comparatively low and depends on the transistor's small-signal parameters, source resistance and emitter network. It is not zero.

8. Input and Output Waveforms

The emitter follower does not invert the signal. The AC output is approximately in phase with the input, but its amplitude is slightly smaller.

Important distinction: The DC emitter voltage is usually about one VBE below the base voltage, but the small-signal AC variations at base and emitter are approximately in phase.

9. Common-Collector Circuit Applications

  • Impedance matching between high-resistance and low-resistance stages.
  • Buffer amplifier between a sensor and a load.
  • Output stage of signal-conditioning circuits.
  • Driving loads that would otherwise heavily load a preceding voltage source.
  • Level shifting in suitable transistor circuits.
  • Darlington emitter-follower arrangements for very high input resistance.

11. Worked Numerical Problems

Problem 1: Calculate emitter current

Given β = 100 and IB = 50 µA.

IE = (β + 1)IB
IE = 101 × 50 µA
IE = 5.05 mA

Answer: Emitter current = 5.05 mA.

Problem 2: Calculate emitter voltage

Given base voltage VB = 3.2 V and VBE = 0.7 V.

VE = VB − VBE
VE = 3.2 − 0.7 = 2.5 V

Answer: Emitter voltage ≈ 2.5 V.

Problem 3: Estimate emitter resistor current

Given VE = 4 V and RE = 1 kΩ, with the lower end of RE grounded.

IE ≈ VE / RE
IE ≈ 4 / 1000 = 4 mA

Answer: Emitter current ≈ 4 mA.

Problem 4: Estimate voltage gain

For a simplified model, let re = 10 Ω and effective emitter resistance RE' = 470 Ω.

Av ≈ RE' / (re + RE')
Av ≈ 470 / 480
Av ≈ 0.979

Answer: Approximate voltage gain = 0.979.

12. Practical Laboratory Experiment

Objective

To construct an NPN common-collector circuit and observe voltage following and impedance-buffering behaviour.

Suggested components and equipment

  • BC547 or a suitable NPN transistor.
  • Bias resistors and emitter resistor.
  • DC regulated power supply.
  • Function generator and oscilloscope.
  • Digital multimeter and breadboard.
  • Optional load resistor for observing loading effects.

Procedure

  1. Check the transistor pin configuration using its datasheet.
  2. Connect the collector to the positive supply.
  3. Provide base bias using a suitable resistor network.
  4. Connect the emitter resistor and establish the DC operating point.
  5. Measure VB, VE and VCE using a multimeter.
  6. Apply a small AC input signal to the base through a coupling capacitor.
  7. Observe base and emitter signals simultaneously.
  8. Verify that the emitter signal follows the base signal without phase inversion.
  9. Connect a suitable load and observe the effect on output amplitude.

Use a current-limited supply during initial testing. Verify the transistor pinout and power ratings before connecting the circuit. Do not short the emitter output directly to the supply.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. What is a common-collector configuration?
A BJT configuration in which the collector is common to input and output circuits.
2. Why is it called an emitter follower?
Because the emitter voltage follows the base voltage with approximately one base-emitter junction drop between their DC levels.
3. What is the voltage gain of an emitter follower?
Slightly less than unity in a typical emitter follower.
4. Is the output inverted?
No. Input and output AC signals are approximately in phase.
5. What is the current gain?
Approximately β + 1, since IE = (β + 1)IB.
6. What is the input impedance?
Relatively high compared with a common-base stage.
7. What is the output impedance?
Relatively low, which makes the circuit useful for driving loads.
8. Why is the emitter follower used as a buffer?
It provides high input impedance and low output impedance while approximately preserving signal voltage.
9. Does an emitter follower provide voltage amplification?
It normally does not provide significant voltage amplification; its voltage gain is slightly below one.
10. Does it provide current amplification?
Yes. Its current gain is approximately β + 1.
11. What is the approximate relationship between VB and VE?
For an NPN silicon transistor operating normally, VE ≈ VB − VBE.
12. What happens if the emitter load becomes too small?
The load draws more current and may pull the transistor toward saturation or cause excessive power dissipation.
13. Why is the emitter follower useful for impedance matching?
It can connect a relatively high-impedance signal source to a lower-impedance load with reduced source loading.
14. What is a Darlington pair?
A two-transistor arrangement in which the emitter of one transistor drives the base of another, producing very high effective current gain.
15. What is the approximate emitter current equation?
IE = IC + IB = (β + 1)IB.
16. Can a common-collector stage be used as a voltage amplifier?
It is generally used as a buffer rather than a voltage-gain stage.
17. What determines the output impedance?
Transistor small-signal parameters, source resistance, emitter network and operating point.
18. What is the effect of emitter current on small-signal emitter resistance?
The intrinsic emitter resistance is approximately VT/IE, so it decreases as emitter current increases.
19. Does the collector carry AC signal current?
The collector is the common node for the signal configuration, but the transistor's collector current still varies with the input signal.
20. Give two applications of a common-collector stage.
Impedance buffering and driving a relatively low-impedance load from a high-impedance source.
