Lesson 2: BJT Construction, Symbols and Working Principle

Understand transistor semiconductor structure, doping, PN junctions, circuit symbols and carrier movement.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the physical construction of a BJT.
  • Identify the three semiconductor regions.
  • Understand the doping levels of emitter, base and collector.
  • Identify the two PN junctions.
  • Understand NPN and PNP transistor symbols.
  • Explain carrier movement during active operation.
  • Understand conventional current and electron flow directions.

2. Construction of a BJT

A Bipolar Junction Transistor is constructed by placing three semiconductor regions together to form two PN junctions. The three regions are called emitter, base and collector.

Depending on the arrangement of P-type and N-type semiconductor materials, the transistor is classified as:

  • NPN transistor: N-type, P-type and N-type regions.
  • PNP transistor: P-type, N-type and P-type regions.

3. Doping Levels of BJT Regions

Doping is the process of intentionally adding impurity atoms to a semiconductor to increase its conductivity.

Region Doping Level Purpose
Emitter Heavily doped Injects a large number of majority carriers.
Base Very lightly doped Reduces recombination of injected carriers.
Collector Moderately doped Collects carriers and withstands reverse voltage.

Why is the emitter heavily doped?

The emitter must inject a large number of majority carriers into the base. Heavy doping improves carrier injection.

Why is the base lightly doped?

A lightly doped base has fewer majority carriers available for recombination. This allows most injected carriers to pass through the base and reach the collector.

Why is the collector physically larger?

The collector generally handles more power dissipation and must withstand the reverse voltage across the collector-base junction. Its physical size and design help with heat dissipation and voltage handling.

Interview Point: Emitter is heavily doped, base is very thin and lightly doped, and collector is generally larger to handle voltage and heat.

4. Two PN Junctions in a BJT

A BJT contains two PN junctions:

  1. Emitter-Base (E-B) junction.
  2. Collector-Base (C-B) junction.

4.1 Emitter-Base Junction

The emitter-base junction controls the injection of majority carriers from the emitter into the base. During normal active operation, this junction is forward biased.

4.2 Collector-Base Junction

The collector-base junction collects carriers that pass through the base. During normal active operation, this junction is reverse biased.

Junction Active Region Bias Function
Emitter-Base Forward biased Injects carriers into the base.
Collector-Base Reverse biased Collects carriers from the base.

5. NPN and PNP Circuit Symbols

The arrow on the emitter identifies the transistor type. The arrow indicates the direction of conventional current through the emitter when the emitter-base junction is forward biased.

Schematic concept: NPN arrow points out; PNP arrow points in.

Remember: NPN = Not Pointing iN is a common memory aid. PNP = Pointing iN.

6. Working Principle of an NPN Transistor

Consider an NPN transistor operating in the active region. Its emitter-base junction is forward biased and its collector-base junction is reverse biased.

Carrier Movement

  1. The positive voltage applied to the base relative to the emitter forward biases the emitter-base junction.
  2. Electrons move from the N-type emitter into the P-type base.
  3. A small fraction of these electrons recombine with holes in the base.
  4. This recombination contributes to base current.
  5. Most electrons reach the collector-base depletion region.
  6. The electric field in the reverse-biased collector-base junction sweeps these electrons into the collector.
Most electrons injected by the emitter reach the collector. A much smaller fraction recombines in the base.

7. Working Principle of a PNP Transistor

A PNP transistor has P-type emitter, N-type base and P-type collector regions.

During active operation:

  • The emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased.
  • Holes are injected from the emitter into the base.
  • Most holes pass through the thin base and reach the collector.
  • A small fraction recombines in the base, contributing to base current.
In a PNP transistor, the conventional emitter current direction is opposite to that of an NPN transistor under corresponding active-region operation.

8. Conventional Current vs Electron Flow

Conventional current is defined as the direction in which positive charge would flow. Electron flow is in the opposite direction in a conducting semiconductor path.

Concept Direction in an NPN transistor
Electron movement through transistor Emitter toward collector (for the main carrier flow).
Conventional collector current Collector toward emitter.
Conventional base current Into the base terminal.
Conventional emitter current Out of the emitter terminal.
Important: Current direction conventions are essential when analyzing transistor circuits and applying Kirchhoff's laws.

9. Important BJT Construction Facts

  • A BJT contains two PN junctions.
  • The base is much thinner than the emitter and collector regions.
  • The emitter is heavily doped.
  • The base is lightly doped.
  • The collector is generally moderately doped.
  • The collector is often physically larger than the emitter.
  • The emitter arrow identifies the transistor type.
  • Both electrons and holes participate in BJT operation.

10. Important Interview Questions

Q1. How many PN junctions are present in a BJT?

Two: emitter-base and collector-base junctions.

Q2. Which BJT region is most heavily doped?

The emitter.

Q3. Why is the collector physically larger?

It generally needs to handle higher power dissipation and withstand collector-base reverse voltage.

Q4. What does the emitter arrow indicate?

It indicates the direction of conventional current through the emitter under forward emitter-base bias.

Q5. What is the biasing condition of a BJT in the active region?

Emitter-base junction forward biased and collector-base junction reverse biased.

Q6. What is the majority carrier in an NPN transistor?

Electrons.

Q7. What is the majority carrier in a PNP transistor?

Holes.

Q8. Why does only a small base current flow?

Because the base is thin and lightly doped, only a small fraction of injected carriers recombine in the base.

12. Lesson Summary

  • A BJT consists of three semiconductor regions and two PN junctions.
  • Its regions are emitter, base and collector.
  • NPN and PNP are the two types of BJT.
  • The emitter is heavily doped, while the base is thin and lightly doped.
  • The collector is designed to collect carriers and handle power dissipation.
  • The emitter arrow indicates conventional current direction.
  • In active operation, the E-B junction is forward biased and C-B junction is reverse biased.
  • Electrons are the majority carriers in NPN; holes are the majority carriers in PNP.
