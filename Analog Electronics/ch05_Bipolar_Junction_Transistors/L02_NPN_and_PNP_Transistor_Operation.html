2. The Two PN Junctions in a BJT

A bipolar junction transistor contains three semiconductor regions and two PN junctions:

  • Emitter-base junction (EBJ): located between the emitter and base.
  • Collector-base junction (CBJ): located between the collector and base.

In the normal forward-active mode used for amplification:

Emitter-base junction: Forward biased
Collector-base junction: Reverse biased
The base is shared by both junctions. This shared thin region enables transistor action and distinguishes a BJT from two separate diodes.

3. Forward Bias and Reverse Bias

Forward-biased PN junction

  • P-side is connected to a higher potential than the N-side.
  • The depletion region becomes narrower.
  • Majority carriers can cross the junction more easily.
  • Current increases significantly once the junction is sufficiently forward biased.

Reverse-biased PN junction

  • P-side is connected to a lower potential than the N-side.
  • The depletion region becomes wider.
  • Majority-carrier conduction is strongly restricted.
  • A small reverse leakage current generally remains.
A reverse-biased collector-base junction is not intended to operate in breakdown during normal forward-active amplification.

4. NPN Transistor Operation

Consider an NPN transistor operating in the forward-active region.

Required voltage conditions

  • The base is approximately 0.6–0.7 V above the emitter for a typical silicon device at modest current and room temperature.
  • The collector is at a higher potential than the base.
  • The emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased.

Carrier movement

  1. The forward-biased emitter-base junction injects electrons from the heavily doped N-type emitter into the P-type base.
  2. Some electrons recombine with holes in the base. This recombination contributes to base current.
  3. Because the base is thin and lightly doped, most injected electrons cross it without recombining.
  4. The reverse-biased collector-base junction's electric field sweeps many of these electrons into the collector.
  5. The resulting collector current is much larger than the base current in a typical forward-active transistor.
IE = IC + IB

Animated NPN carrier movement

Electrons are injected from the emitter and move through the thin base toward the collector.

5. PNP Transistor Operation

A PNP transistor has the opposite semiconductor arrangement and opposite voltage polarities compared with an NPN transistor.

Required voltage conditions

  • The emitter is at a higher potential than the base.
  • The emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • The collector is at a lower potential than the base.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased.

Carrier movement

  1. The forward-biased emitter-base junction injects holes from the P-type emitter into the N-type base.
  2. Some holes recombine with electrons in the base.
  3. Most holes cross the thin base region.
  4. The collector-base electric field transports these holes into the collector.
  5. The base current is associated with recombination and the external current balance.

Animated PNP carrier movement

Holes are injected from the emitter and transported through the base toward the collector.

6. Electron Flow and Conventional Current

These two descriptions refer to opposite directions for electron-dominated current in an external conductor.

Electron flow

Electrons physically move from the negative side toward the positive side of an external circuit.

Conventional current

Defined as the direction positive charge would move. It is opposite to electron flow in an external metallic conductor.

9. Worked Numerical Example

An NPN transistor is operating in forward-active mode. The base current is 25 µA and the collector current is 2.5 mA. Calculate the emitter current.

IE = IC + IB

Convert the base current:

25 µA = 0.025 mA

Substitute:

IE = 2.5 + 0.025 = 2.525 mA
Answer: The emitter current is 2.525 mA.

10. Practical Lab Exercise

Experiment: Study of NPN and PNP Transistor Biasing

Objective: To observe the different voltage polarities and understand the carrier transport mechanism in NPN and PNP transistors.

Equipment:

  • NPN transistor such as BC547.
  • PNP transistor such as BC557.
  • Regulated DC power supply.
  • Resistors and breadboard.
  • Digital multimeter.
  • Datasheets for the selected components.

Procedure:

  1. Identify the transistor terminals using its datasheet.
  2. Draw the NPN forward-active bias circuit with emitter at a lower potential than base and collector.
  3. Use a current-limiting resistor in the base path.
  4. Measure base-emitter and collector-emitter voltages.
  5. Repeat using a PNP transistor with reversed supply and bias polarities.
  6. Compare the measured voltages and current directions.

Expected observations:

  • For an NPN silicon transistor, the base is typically about 0.6–0.7 V above the emitter in forward-active operation at modest current.
  • For a PNP silicon transistor, the emitter is typically about 0.6–0.7 V above the base.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased in both cases.
Safety: Use current-limiting resistors and a low-voltage regulated supply. Verify polarity and pinout before powering the circuit. Do not connect a transistor directly across a supply without current limiting.

12. Placement Interview Questions

Q1. What are the two junctions in a BJT?

Emitter-base and collector-base junctions.

Q2. Which junctions are forward and reverse biased in forward-active mode?

The emitter-base junction is forward biased and the collector-base junction is reverse biased.

Q3. What happens when the NPN emitter-base junction is forward biased?

Electrons are injected from the N-type emitter into the P-type base.

Q4. Why is base current relatively small?

The base is thin and lightly doped, so only a relatively small fraction of injected carriers recombine in it.

Q5. What happens to most injected carriers in an NPN transistor?

They cross the base and are swept into the collector by the collector-base electric field.

Q6. What is the main injected carrier in a PNP transistor?

Holes.

Q7. What is the base polarity of an NPN transistor in forward-active mode?

The base is more positive than the emitter.

Q8. What is the base polarity of a PNP transistor in forward-active mode?

The base is more negative than the emitter.

Q9. What is conventional current?

The defined direction of positive charge flow, opposite to electron motion in a metallic conductor.

Q10. Why does a reverse-biased collector-base junction collect carriers?

Its electric field sweeps carriers that reach the depletion region into the collector.

Q11. Is 0.7 V a fixed base-emitter voltage?

No. It is an approximate value for many silicon BJTs under particular current and temperature conditions. The actual voltage varies.

Q12. What happens if the collector-base junction becomes forward biased?

The transistor may enter saturation rather than remain in forward-active operation.

Q13. Why is the collector-base junction reverse biased in forward-active operation?

Its electric field enables collection of carriers crossing the base.

Q14. What is the relationship between emitter, base and collector currents?

Using standard current reference directions, IE = IB + IC.

Q15. What is the difference between electron flow and conventional current?

In an external metallic conductor, they have opposite directions.

15. Lecture Summary

  • A BJT has two PN junctions: emitter-base and collector-base.
  • Forward-active operation requires a forward-biased emitter-base junction and a reverse-biased collector-base junction.
  • NPN operation involves electron injection from emitter to base; PNP operation involves hole injection.
  • The thin base allows most injected carriers to reach the collector.
  • Conventional current and electron flow have opposite directions in an external metallic conductor.
  • Correct bias polarity is essential for transistor operation.
