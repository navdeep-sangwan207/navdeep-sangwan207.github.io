2. What is a Bipolar Junction Transistor?

A Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) is a three-terminal semiconductor device used for switching and amplification of electrical signals.

It is constructed using three semiconductor regions arranged to form two PN junctions. These regions are called the emitter, base and collector.

Definition: A BJT is a current-controlled semiconductor device in which a small base current controls a much larger collector current when the transistor operates in its active region.

Why is it called a Bipolar Junction Transistor?

The term bipolar refers to the participation of both types of charge carriers:

  • Electrons — negative charge carriers.
  • Holes — positive charge carriers.

Both electrons and holes participate in the operation of a BJT, although one carrier type is the majority carrier in each region.

3. Three Terminals of a BJT

A BJT has three terminals:

Terminal Symbol Function
Emitter E Supplies majority charge carriers into the base region.
Base B Controls the transistor operation and collector current.
Collector C Collects charge carriers passing through the base region.

3.1 Emitter (E)

  • Heavily doped region.
  • Supplies majority charge carriers.
  • Designed to inject carriers efficiently.

3.2 Base (B)

  • Very thin region.
  • Lightly doped compared with the emitter.
  • Controls the transistor operation.
  • Allows most injected carriers to reach the collector.

3.3 Collector (C)

  • Moderately doped compared with the emitter.
  • Usually physically larger than the emitter.
  • Collects carriers injected from the emitter through the base.
  • Designed to handle heat and power dissipation.
Important: The emitter is heavily doped, the base is very thin and lightly doped, and the collector is designed to collect carriers and dissipate heat.

4. Basic BJT Structure

A BJT consists of three semiconductor regions and two PN junctions:

  • Emitter-Base (E-B) junction.
  • Collector-Base (C-B) junction.

5. Basic Working Principle of a BJT

Consider an NPN transistor operating in the active region. The emitter-base junction is forward biased and the collector-base junction is reverse biased.

  1. The forward-biased emitter-base junction injects electrons from the emitter into the base.
  2. Because the base is thin and lightly doped, only a small number of electrons recombine with holes in the base.
  3. This recombination produces a small base current.
  4. Most electrons cross the base and are swept into the collector by the reverse-biased collector-base junction.
  5. This produces the collector current.
Key Concept: A small base current controls a much larger collector current in the active region.

6. Basic Current Relationships

The three currents flowing through a BJT are:

  • Emitter current (IE)
  • Base current (IB)
  • Collector current (IC)
IE = IB + IC

This relationship follows Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL). The emitter current is the sum of the base and collector currents.

Example

If the collector current is 9.8 mA and the base current is 0.2 mA, calculate the emitter current.

IE = IC + IB

IE = 9.8 + 0.2

IE = 10 mA

7. BJT as a Switching and Amplifying Device

7.1 Switching Application

A BJT can operate as an electronic switch by using two operating regions:

  • Cutoff: Transistor is OFF and collector current is approximately zero.
  • Saturation: Transistor is ON and operates with a low collector-emitter voltage.

Applications include:

  • LED control.
  • Relay drivers.
  • Motor control.
  • Digital interface circuits.

7.2 Amplification Application

A BJT can amplify a small input signal when it is properly biased in the active region.

A small variation in base current produces a larger variation in collector current. With a suitable collector load, this current variation produces an amplified output voltage.

Placement Point: Active region is used for linear amplification, while cutoff and saturation are commonly used for switching.

10. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is a BJT?

A BJT is a three-terminal semiconductor device used for switching and amplification.

Q2. Why is a BJT called a bipolar device?

Because both electrons and holes participate in its operation.

Q3. What are the three terminals of a BJT?

Emitter, base and collector.

Q4. Why is the base region very thin?

To minimize carrier recombination and allow most injected carriers to reach the collector.

Q5. Which BJT region is used for amplification?

The active region.

Q6. Which two regions are used for switching?

Cutoff and saturation.

Q7. What is the relationship between emitter, base and collector currents?

IE = IB + IC.

Q8. What are the two main types of BJT?

NPN and PNP transistors.
