Lesson 9: BJT Biasing Techniques and Thermal Stability

Fixed Bias, Collector-to-Base Bias, Voltage Divider Bias, Stability and Thermal Runaway

1. Learning Objectives

After completing this lesson, you will be able to:

  • Understand transistor biasing and its purpose.
  • Explain the different BJT biasing techniques.
  • Analyze fixed-bias and collector-to-base bias circuits.
  • Understand voltage-divider bias and emitter stabilization.
  • Calculate base current, collector current and collector-emitter voltage.
  • Understand thermal runaway and temperature effects.
  • Explain stability factor and methods of improving thermal stability.

2. What is BJT Biasing?

BJT biasing is the process of applying suitable DC voltages and currents to a transistor to establish its desired operating point (Q-point).

In an amplifier, the transistor is normally biased in the forward-active region so that an AC input signal can be amplified without unwanted cutoff or saturation.

Q-Point = (ICQ, VCEQ)

2.1 Why is Biasing Required?

  • To establish a suitable DC operating point.
  • To maintain the transistor in the active region during amplification.
  • To reduce signal distortion.
  • To minimize the effect of transistor beta variations.
  • To improve operating-point stability against temperature changes.
Important: A biasing circuit should establish a predictable Q-point even when transistor parameters and operating temperature change.

3. Types of BJT Biasing Techniques

Common BJT biasing techniques include:

  1. Fixed bias (base-resistor bias).
  2. Collector-to-base feedback bias.
  3. Fixed bias with emitter resistor.
  4. Voltage-divider bias (self-bias).
Biasing Technique Stability Common Application
Fixed bias Poor Simple switching and basic circuit analysis
Collector-to-base feedback Better than fixed bias Simple bias stabilization
Emitter-resistor bias Improved Amplifier circuits
Voltage-divider bias Generally good when properly designed Common emitter amplifiers

4. Fixed Bias (Base-Resistor Bias)

4.1 Circuit Description

In fixed bias, a resistor RB connects the base to the positive supply VCC. The emitter is connected to ground, and a collector resistor RC connects the collector to VCC.

Simplified NPN fixed-bias circuit. RB provides base current, RC limits collector current, and the emitter is grounded.

4.2 Fixed-Bias Equations

Applying KVL to the base circuit:

VCC = IBRB + VBE

IB = (VCC − VBE) / RB

Collector current in forward-active operation:

IC ≈ βIB

Collector-emitter voltage:

VCE = VCC − ICRC

4.3 Numerical Example

Given:

  • VCC = 10 V
  • RB = 470 kΩ
  • RC = 1 kΩ
  • VBE = 0.7 V
  • β = 100

Step 1: Calculate base current

IB = (10 − 0.7) / 470000

IB ≈ 19.8 µA

Step 2: Calculate collector current

IC ≈ 100 × 19.8 µA

IC ≈ 1.98 mA

Step 3: Calculate VCE

VCE = 10 − (1.98 mA × 1 kΩ)

VCE ≈ 8.02 V

Answer: Q-point is approximately (1.98 mA, 8.02 V).

The calculation assumes forward-active operation. Always check the resulting VCE and collector current against the available load line and transistor operating limits.

4.4 Disadvantage of Fixed Bias

Collector current depends strongly on beta. Since beta varies between transistors and with operating conditions, the Q-point may shift significantly.

Fixed bias also provides little negative feedback to compensate for temperature-related increases in collector current.

5. Collector-to-Base Feedback Bias

5.1 Working Principle

In collector-to-base feedback bias, the base resistor RB is connected between the collector and base rather than directly to VCC.

If collector current increases, the voltage drop across RC increases and collector voltage decreases. This reduces the available base current, opposing the original increase.

Simplified collector-to-base feedback bias circuit.

5.2 Approximate Equations

For an emitter grounded circuit, neglecting base current in the collector-path voltage drop:

IB ≈ (VCC − VBE) / (RB + βRC)
IC ≈ βIB

The feedback makes the circuit less sensitive to beta changes than ordinary fixed bias.

This is negative feedback: a rise in collector current tends to reduce the base current that drives it.

6. Fixed Bias with Emitter Resistor

An emitter resistor RE is connected between the emitter and ground. It provides negative feedback and improves thermal stability.

6.1 Working Principle

  1. Suppose temperature increases.
  2. Collector current tends to increase.
  3. Emitter current also increases.
  4. The voltage drop across RE increases.
  5. Emitter voltage rises.
  6. VBE decreases for a relatively fixed base voltage.
  7. Base current and collector current are driven downward.
VE = IERE

VB = VBE + VE
The emitter resistor provides negative feedback by opposing changes in emitter and collector current.

7. Voltage-Divider Bias (Self-Bias)

7.1 Introduction

Voltage-divider bias is one of the most commonly used BJT biasing techniques in amplifier circuits.

Two resistors R1 and R2 form a voltage divider to establish the base voltage. An emitter resistor RE provides negative feedback and improves Q-point stability.

Voltage-divider bias: R1 and R2 set base voltage, RC is the collector load, and RE provides stabilization.

7.2 Thevenin Equivalent

The voltage-divider network can be replaced by its Thevenin equivalent as seen from the transistor base.

VTH = VCC × R2 / (R1 + R2)

RTH = R1 || R2

Here, the parallel combination is:

RTH = (R1R2) / (R1 + R2)

7.3 Base Current Equation

Applying KVL to the Thevenin-equivalent base-emitter loop:

IB = (VTH − VBE) / (RTH + (β + 1)RE)

Then:

IC ≈ βIB

IE ≈ (β + 1)IB

VCE = VCC − ICRC − IERE
These equations assume the transistor is operating in the forward-active region. Check the resulting currents and voltages to confirm that assumption.

8. Numerical Example: Voltage-Divider Bias

Given:

  • VCC = 12 V
  • R1 = 40 kΩ
  • R2 = 10 kΩ
  • RC = 2 kΩ
  • RE = 1 kΩ
  • β = 100
  • VBE = 0.7 V

Step 1: Calculate Thevenin voltage

VTH = 12 × 10 / (40 + 10)

VTH = 2.4 V

Step 2: Calculate Thevenin resistance

RTH = (40 × 10) / (40 + 10) kΩ

RTH = 8 kΩ

Step 3: Calculate base current

IB = (2.4 − 0.7) / [8 kΩ + (101 × 1 kΩ)]

IB = 1.7 / 109 kΩ

IB ≈ 15.6 µA

Step 4: Calculate collector current

IC ≈ 100 × 15.6 µA

IC ≈ 1.56 mA

Step 5: Calculate emitter current

IE ≈ 101 × 15.6 µA

IE ≈ 1.57 mA

Step 6: Calculate collector-emitter voltage

VCE = 12 − (1.56 mA × 2 kΩ) − (1.57 mA × 1 kΩ)

VCE ≈ 7.31 V

Final result: The approximate Q-point is (1.56 mA, 7.31 V).

9. Thermal Stability of BJT

9.1 What is Thermal Stability?

Thermal stability refers to the ability of a transistor biasing circuit to maintain a reasonably stable operating point when temperature changes.

A transistor's collector current can change with temperature because of changes in base-emitter voltage, leakage current, and current gain.

9.2 Effect of Temperature on Collector Current

When transistor temperature increases:

  • Base-emitter voltage required for a given current generally decreases.
  • Leakage current generally increases.
  • Current gain beta may change.
  • Collector current may increase if the bias network does not compensate.
The approximate temperature coefficient of VBE for a silicon transistor at a given operating current is often around −2 mV/°C. The actual value depends on the device and operating conditions.

10. Thermal Runaway

10.1 Definition

Thermal runaway is a condition in which increasing transistor temperature causes collector current and power dissipation to increase further, creating additional heating.

10.2 Thermal Runaway Process

  1. Transistor temperature increases.
  2. Leakage current and possibly collector current increase.
  3. Power dissipation increases.
  4. The transistor generates additional heat.
  5. Temperature rises further.
  6. The process can become destructive if not controlled.
PD ≈ VCE × IC
Thermal runaway is a serious reliability problem. A transistor must operate within its datasheet limits for power dissipation, junction temperature, voltage and current.

10.3 Methods to Prevent Thermal Runaway

  • Use emitter-resistor negative feedback.
  • Use a properly designed voltage-divider bias network.
  • Use suitable heat sinks where required.
  • Select transistors with suitable power and thermal ratings.
  • Ensure adequate ventilation and thermal design.
  • Use temperature compensation where necessary.

11. Stability Factor

11.1 Definition

Stability factor describes how sensitive collector current is to changes in transistor leakage current, under specified assumptions.

A commonly used stability factor with respect to leakage current is:

S = ∂IC / ∂ICBO

Here, the derivative is evaluated while holding the other relevant circuit parameters constant.

A lower value of this stability factor indicates less sensitivity of collector current to changes in leakage current.

11.2 Fixed-Bias Stability Factor

For a simplified fixed-bias transistor model:

S = β + 1

Since beta can be relatively large, fixed bias can have poor thermal stability.

Stability factor has several definitions in transistor bias analysis. Always identify whether the factor is defined with respect to ICEO, ICBO, beta or VBE before applying a formula.

13. Important Interview Questions

Q1. What is transistor biasing?

Applying suitable DC voltages and currents to establish a desired transistor operating point.

Q2. Why is voltage-divider bias widely used?

It provides a relatively stable base voltage and, with an emitter resistor, improves Q-point stability.

Q3. What is fixed bias?

A biasing method in which a resistor supplies base current from a DC supply.

Q4. Why is fixed bias less stable?

Collector current depends strongly on beta and temperature-related transistor parameter changes.

Q5. What is negative feedback in a biasing circuit?

A feedback mechanism that opposes a change in current or voltage, helping stabilize the operating point.

Q6. What is the purpose of an emitter resistor?

It develops a voltage that opposes changes in emitter current, improving bias stability.

Q7. What is thermal runaway?

A self-reinforcing increase in temperature and current that can lead to transistor failure.

Q8. How can thermal runaway be prevented?

Use stable biasing, emitter feedback, appropriate heat sinking and proper thermal design.

Q9. What is stability factor?

A measure of how sensitively collector current responds to changes in a specified transistor parameter.

Q10. Which biasing method is commonly used in CE amplifiers?

Voltage-divider bias with an emitter resistor is commonly used.

15. Lesson Summary

  • BJT biasing establishes the desired DC operating point.
  • Fixed bias is simple but has poor stability.
  • Collector-to-base feedback reduces sensitivity to current changes.
  • An emitter resistor provides negative feedback.
  • Voltage-divider bias is widely used in amplifier circuits.
  • Thermal stability helps maintain a predictable Q-point.
  • Thermal runaway can occur when increased heating causes further increases in current and power dissipation.
  • Proper bias design, feedback and thermal management improve reliability.
