2. Practical Applications of BJT

Bipolar Junction Transistors are widely used in electronics for signal amplification, electronic switching and current control.

Common Applications

  • Relay driver circuits.
  • Audio amplifier stages.
  • Sensor signal conditioning.
  • Oscillator circuits.
  • Motor control interfaces.

3. BJT as an Electronic Switch

3.1 Switching Principle

A BJT can operate as an electronic switch by moving between cutoff and saturation.

Operating Region Switch Condition Typical Behaviour
Cutoff OFF Collector current is approximately zero.
Saturation ON Collector-emitter voltage is low.
Cutoff: IB ≈ 0

Saturation: VCE ≈ VCE(sat)

3.2 NPN Low-Side Switching Circuit

An NPN transistor can be connected between a load and ground. The load is connected between the positive supply and the transistor collector.

NPN low-side switch. A suitable base-drive signal turns the transistor on and allows load current to flow.

3.3 Working

When input is LOW:

  • Base current is approximately zero.
  • The transistor is in cutoff.
  • The load is switched OFF.

When input is HIGH:

  • Base current flows through RB.
  • The transistor turns ON.
  • With sufficient base drive, it enters saturation.
  • Current flows through the load and transistor to ground.
The input must provide sufficient base current without exceeding the GPIO pin or transistor ratings. A logic HIGH does not automatically guarantee saturation.

4. LED Driver Using BJT

An NPN transistor can drive an LED when the load current required is greater than a control signal should supply directly.

4.1 Circuit Components

  • NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222, subject to ratings.
  • LED.
  • LED current-limiting resistor RL.
  • Base resistor RB.
  • DC supply and control signal.

Conceptual NPN LED driver. The LED and its series resistor are connected in the collector path.

4.2 LED Current Calculation

The LED current is approximately determined by the supply voltage, LED forward voltage, transistor saturation voltage and series resistor.

RL = (VCC − VF − VCE(sat)) / ILED

4.3 Numerical Example

Given:

  • VCC = 5 V
  • LED forward voltage VF = 2 V
  • Desired LED current = 10 mA
  • Assume VCE(sat) = 0.2 V
RL = (5 − 2 − 0.2) / 0.01

RL = 280 Ω

A standard 270 Ω or 300 Ω resistor may be selected after checking the actual LED current and component ratings.

Always use a current-limiting resistor for a conventional LED. Never connect an LED directly across a supply without appropriate current control.

5. Relay Driver Using BJT

5.1 Why is a Transistor Used for Relay Driving?

A relay coil may require more current than a microcontroller output pin can safely provide. An NPN transistor can act as a low-side switch to control the coil.

5.2 Flyback Diode

A relay coil is an inductive load. When transistor current is suddenly interrupted, the collapsing magnetic field produces a voltage spike.

A flyback diode is connected across the relay coil to provide a path for the inductive current and limit the voltage spike at the switching transistor.

Conceptual relay driver. The flyback diode is connected across the coil, with its cathode toward the positive supply and anode toward the transistor collector.

The diode must be reverse-biased during normal coil operation. Confirm its polarity and current rating. A simple diode can slow relay release; use a suitable suppression method when faster release is required.

5.3 Relay Driver Design Considerations

  • Check relay coil voltage and current from its datasheet.
  • Choose a transistor with suitable collector-current and voltage ratings.
  • Calculate base drive for reliable saturation.
  • Use a flyback diode or suitable inductive-load suppression.
  • Check transistor power dissipation and temperature.
  • Use a separate supply if required, with a common ground for a non-isolated control interface.

6. Base Resistor Calculation for Switching

When using a BJT as a switch, the base current must be sufficient to drive the transistor into saturation.

A practical design often uses a forced beta smaller than the transistor's typical active-region current gain.

IB ≥ IC / βforced

RB ≤ (VDRIVE − VBE(sat)) / IB

Numerical Example

Given:

  • Load current IC = 100 mA
  • Drive voltage = 5 V
  • Assumed base-emitter saturation voltage = 0.8 V
  • Forced beta = 10

Step 1: Required base current

IB ≥ 100 mA / 10

IB ≥ 10 mA

Step 2: Calculate maximum base resistance

RB ≤ (5 − 0.8) / 0.01

RB ≤ 420 Ω

A resistor of 390 Ω may be considered, provided the driving source can safely supply the required base current.

Do not use a base resistor that causes excessive GPIO current. If the required base current is too high, use a suitable driver stage or a logic-level MOSFET instead.

7. BJT as an Amplifier

A BJT can amplify a small input signal when biased in the forward-active region. A small change in base current produces a larger change in collector current.

ΔIC ≈ β × ΔIB

In a Common Emitter amplifier, the collector resistor converts changes in collector current into output-voltage changes. The output voltage is generally 180° out of phase with the input signal in the usual CE configuration.

Applications

  • Audio preamplifier stages.
  • Microphone signal amplification.
  • Sensor signal conditioning.
  • Voltage amplification.
Unlike switching, amplifier operation normally requires the transistor to remain in the active region throughout the expected signal cycle.

8. Practical BJT Testing Using a Multimeter

8.1 Diode-Test Method

A typical BJT can be checked approximately as two PN junctions sharing the base terminal.

  1. Disconnect power from the circuit.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely.
  3. Isolate the transistor from parallel circuit paths if necessary.
  4. Set the multimeter to diode-test mode.
  5. Identify the base and check both base-emitter and base-collector junctions.

8.2 Expected Readings for a Silicon NPN Transistor

Red Probe Black Probe Expected Result
Base Emitter Forward voltage drop, often around 0.6–0.7 V
Base Collector Forward voltage drop, often around 0.6–0.7 V
Emitter Base OL or no forward conduction
Collector Base OL or no forward conduction

For a PNP transistor, the expected probe polarity is reversed for the forward-biased base junction tests.

These are typical checks, not universal pinout rules. Transistor pin arrangements vary by part number and package. Check the datasheet before identifying collector and emitter. In-circuit readings can also be affected by other components.

9. Measuring BJT Voltages in a Working Circuit

For an NPN Common Emitter circuit with emitter connected to ground, measure:

  • Base voltage VB relative to ground.
  • Emitter voltage VE relative to ground.
  • Collector voltage VC relative to ground.
  • Collector-emitter voltage VCE.
VBE = VB − VE

VCE = VC − VE

For an ordinary silicon NPN transistor in forward-active operation, VBE is often around 0.6–0.7 V, although the actual value depends on current and temperature.

Example Measurement

Measurement Reading
VB 0.72 V
VE 0 V
VC 6 V
VBE 0.72 V
VCE 6 V

These readings are consistent with a transistor that may be operating in the forward-active region. A complete diagnosis also requires checking collector current and circuit connections.

10. Common BJT Faults and Troubleshooting

Fault Possible Cause Checking Method
Transistor does not turn ON No base drive, wrong pinout, open circuit Check base voltage and base current.
Transistor always ON Shorted transistor or incorrect base connection Remove base drive and check collector current.
LED does not glow Reversed LED, open resistor, wiring fault Check LED polarity and supply voltage.
Transistor overheats Excessive current, high VCE, insufficient cooling Measure current and calculate power dissipation.
Relay does not operate Insufficient coil voltage or base drive Check coil voltage and transistor switching.
Unstable amplifier output Unstable bias, poor connections, supply noise Measure Q-point and inspect decoupling.
Distorted output Incorrect Q-point or excessive input signal Check bias and output waveform.

11. Step-by-Step BJT Troubleshooting Procedure

  1. Visual inspection: Check component orientation, damaged parts, solder joints and wiring.
  2. Supply check: Verify the DC supply voltage and ground connection.
  3. Pin identification: Confirm the transistor pinout from its datasheet.
  4. Junction test: Use diode-test mode to check base-emitter and base-collector junctions.
  5. Base drive check: Measure base voltage and verify adequate base current.
  6. Collector check: Measure collector voltage and estimate collector current.
  7. Operating-region check: Determine whether the transistor is in cutoff, active region or saturation.
  8. Thermal check: Check for abnormal heating and verify power ratings.
  9. Component replacement: Replace faulty components only after identifying the likely cause.
  10. Final verification: Repeat measurements and verify normal circuit operation.
A good troubleshooting process measures the circuit before replacing components. Do not assume that a transistor is faulty simply because the circuit is not working.

12. Important Design Considerations

  • Check maximum collector current and collector-emitter voltage.
  • Check transistor power dissipation and safe operating area.
  • Calculate base resistor values instead of choosing them arbitrarily.
  • Use flyback suppression for inductive loads such as relay coils.
  • Check LED forward voltage and current requirements.
  • Use suitable heat sinking when required.
  • Ensure the control signal and transistor share a suitable reference ground when using a non-isolated interface.
  • Check the actual transistor pinout before assembly.

13. Important Interview Questions

Q1. How is a BJT used as a switch?

It is operated between cutoff (OFF) and saturation (ON).

Q2. Why is an NPN transistor commonly used as a low-side switch?

It can control a load connected to the positive supply by switching its return path to ground.

Q3. Why is a base resistor required?

It limits base current and protects the transistor and driving source.

Q4. Why is a flyback diode used with a relay?

It provides a current path for the relay coil when switching off and limits the inductive voltage spike.

Q5. How do you test a BJT using a multimeter?

Use diode-test mode to check the base-emitter and base-collector junctions, with correct probe polarity.

Q6. What happens if a transistor is operated beyond its power rating?

Excessive heating can damage the transistor or cause permanent failure.

Q7. What is the difference between switching and amplification?

Switching uses cutoff and saturation, while linear amplification uses the forward-active region.

Q8. What is the purpose of a collector resistor?

It limits collector current and converts collector-current changes into voltage changes in a CE circuit.

Q9. Why can a transistor become hot?

Possible causes include excessive current, high voltage across the device, insufficient cooling and operation outside its safe operating area.

Q10. How do you identify an NPN transistor?

Use the datasheet and diode-test measurements. In a typical NPN device, the base is positive relative to emitter and collector when each junction is forward-biased.
