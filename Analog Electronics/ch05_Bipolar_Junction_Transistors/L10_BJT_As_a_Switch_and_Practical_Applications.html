2. Transistor as an Electronic Switch

A transistor can operate as an electronic switch by using two operating regions: cutoff and saturation. In a typical NPN low-side switching circuit, the load is connected between the positive supply and the collector, while the emitter is connected to ground.

OFF state — Cutoff

  • Base-emitter junction is not sufficiently forward biased.
  • Base current is approximately zero.
  • Collector current is approximately zero, apart from leakage.
  • Collector voltage is near the supply voltage for a resistive load.

Switch open: load OFF

ON state — Saturation

  • Base drive is sufficient to saturate the transistor.
  • Collector current is determined mainly by the external load.
  • Collector-emitter voltage is low, typically around 0.1–0.3 V for many small silicon BJTs under suitable conditions.
  • Load receives current.

Switch closed: load ON

Cutoff: IB ≈ 0, IC ≈ 0
Saturation: VCE ≈ VCE(sat)
Load current: IC ≈ (VCC − VCE(sat)) / RL

A transistor is not an ideal mechanical switch. It has leakage current, a finite saturation voltage, switching delays and power dissipation. The actual values must be taken from the selected transistor's datasheet.

5. Base Resistor Design

The base resistor limits the current from the control source. For reliable switching, designers commonly choose a forced beta lower than the transistor's nominal small-signal beta. A forced beta of 10 is a common starting point for small switching BJTs, but it is not universal.

IB(design) = IC(load) / βforced
RB = (Vcontrol − VBE(on)) / IB(design)

Use a suitable VBE(on) estimate from the datasheet or design conditions. A common first estimate for a silicon BJT is approximately 0.7–0.9 V. Check the control pin's source/sink current capability.

6. Worked LED Driver Design

Design requirement: Switch a 20 mA LED from a 5 V control signal using an NPN transistor.

Assumed design values

  • Supply voltage = 5 V.
  • LED forward voltage = 2 V.
  • Desired LED current = 20 mA.
  • Transistor saturation voltage = 0.2 V (design estimate).
  • Base-emitter voltage = 0.8 V (design estimate).
  • Forced beta = 10.

Step 1: Calculate LED series resistor

RLED = (VCC − VF − VCE(sat)) / ILED
RLED = (5 − 2 − 0.2) / 0.02
RLED = 140 Ω

A 150 Ω standard resistor gives an approximate current of:

ILED = (5 − 2 − 0.2) / 150
ILED ≈ 18.7 mA

Step 2: Calculate base current

IB = IC / βforced
IB = 20 mA / 10 = 2 mA

Step 3: Calculate base resistor

RB = (5 − 0.8) / 0.002
RB = 2100 Ω

A 2.0 kΩ or 2.1 kΩ standard resistor is a reasonable starting selection, subject to verification of actual transistor and control-source limits.

Step 4: Check base resistor power

PRB = IB²RB
PRB = (0.002)² × 2100
PRB = 8.4 mW

A standard 0.125 W or 0.25 W resistor provides ample nominal power margin for this example.

8. Relay Driver Circuit

A relay coil is an inductive load. When transistor current is switched off, the collapsing magnetic field can generate a high voltage across the coil. A flyback diode provides a path for this current and limits the voltage transient.

Flyback diode polarity

  • Diode cathode connects to the positive supply side of the relay coil.
  • Diode anode connects to the transistor collector side of the coil.
  • During normal energisation, the diode is reverse biased.
  • When the transistor switches OFF, the coil current circulates through the diode.
Approximate coil current: Icoil = Vcoil / Rcoil
Approximate coil power: Pcoil = Vcoil × Icoil

A flyback diode slows relay release compared with some alternative clamp circuits. For fast release or controlled transient voltage, an appropriately designed TVS or zener clamp may be more suitable. The selected protection component must be rated for the coil energy and switching conditions.

9. Relay Driver Design Example

A 12 V relay has a coil resistance of 400 Ω. A transistor is used as a low-side switch. Assume VCE(sat) = 0.2 V and forced beta = 10.

Step 1: Coil current

Icoil = (12 − 0.2) / 400
Icoil = 29.5 mA

Step 2: Base current

IB = 29.5 mA / 10
IB = 2.95 mA

Step 3: Base resistor for a 5 V control signal

RB = (5 − 0.8) / 2.95 mA
RB ≈ 1.42 kΩ

A 1.3 kΩ standard value provides somewhat more base drive than the calculated minimum. Verify the control source can supply the resulting current.

Step 4: Coil power

Pcoil = 12 × 0.0295
Pcoil ≈ 0.354 W

The transistor must be selected with adequate collector-current, voltage and power ratings, including switching transients and thermal margin.

11. Switching Waveforms

The input control signal and collector voltage are approximately opposite in a simple NPN low-side switching circuit: when the transistor turns ON, collector voltage falls; when it turns OFF, collector voltage rises toward the supply.

12. Switching Losses and Speed

A transistor dissipates power when voltage and current exist across it. In the ideal OFF state, collector current is nearly zero. In the ideal ON state, collector-emitter voltage is small. During transitions, however, both may be significant.

Instantaneous transistor power:
P(t) = VCE(t) × IC(t)
Approximate ON-state conduction loss:
PON ≈ VCE(sat) × IC

Important switching terms

  • Turn-on time: Time required for the transistor to transition from OFF toward ON.
  • Turn-off time: Time required for the transistor to transition from ON toward OFF.
  • Storage time: Time associated with removing stored charge, especially after saturation.
  • Rise time: Time for the output transition between specified levels.
  • Fall time: Time for the output to transition in the opposite direction.

Deep saturation can increase storage time and slow turn-off. In high-speed switching applications, consider base-drive design, transistor selection and appropriate charge-removal techniques.

13. Practical Lab Experiment

Objective

To implement and test an NPN transistor as a low-side electronic switch.

Components

  • Small NPN transistor such as BC547 or 2N2222, subject to datasheet limits.
  • LED and series resistor.
  • Base resistor.
  • 5 V regulated supply and suitable control source.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires.
  • Digital multimeter.

Procedure

  1. Identify the transistor's collector, base and emitter from its datasheet.
  2. Connect the emitter to ground.
  3. Connect the LED and series resistor between the positive supply and collector.
  4. Connect the control signal to the base through a calculated resistor.
  5. Apply LOW to the base control and measure collector voltage.
  6. Apply HIGH and measure collector current and VCE.
  7. Compare OFF and ON readings with theoretical calculations.
  8. Repeat using a relay coil with an appropriately rated flyback diode.

15. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

1. Which two BJT regions are used for switching?
Cutoff and saturation.
2. What is the condition of a transistor in cutoff?
Base drive is insufficient, collector current is approximately zero except for leakage, and the transistor behaves like an open switch.
3. What is the condition of a transistor in saturation?
The transistor is strongly driven ON and VCE is close to its saturation voltage.
4. Why is a base resistor required?
It limits base current and protects the transistor and control source.
5. What is forced beta?
The ratio of required collector current to the deliberately supplied base current for switching design.
6. Why is forced beta usually lower than the datasheet's small-signal beta?
It provides sufficient base drive to reach saturation under the intended load conditions.
7. What is a low-side switch?
A switching device placed between the load and ground/return path.
8. Why is a flyback diode connected across a relay coil?
It provides a path for inductive current when the transistor turns OFF and limits the resulting voltage spike.
9. How is the flyback diode connected?
Cathode to the positive coil supply and anode to the transistor collector side.
10. What happens if the transistor is not driven into saturation?
It may operate in the active region, resulting in excessive VCE and power dissipation for a switching application.
11. What is the main advantage of a transistor switch?
Fast electronic control without moving mechanical contacts.
12. Can a BJT directly drive a high-current motor?
Only if its electrical and thermal ratings and drive requirements are suitable. A properly rated power transistor or MOSFET and protection circuit may be necessary.
13. What is transistor conduction loss in saturation?
Approximately VCE(sat) multiplied by collector current.
14. What is storage time?
The delay associated with removing stored charge after a transistor has been driven into saturation.
15. Why should transistor pinout be checked?
Different packages and transistor models may use different collector, base and emitter pin arrangements.
