2. What Is a Bipolar Junction Transistor?

A Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) is a three-terminal semiconductor device used for signal amplification and electronic switching.

It is called bipolar because both types of charge carriers—electrons and holes—participate in its operation.

NPN transistor

Structure: N-type emitter, P-type base and N-type collector.

PNP transistor

Structure: P-type emitter, N-type base and P-type collector.

Important: A BJT has two PN junctions: the emitter-base junction and the collector-base junction. These junctions share a very thin base region.

3. Physical Construction of a BJT

A transistor is manufactured by forming three semiconductor regions with carefully controlled doping concentrations and dimensions.

RegionDopingPhysical characteristicFunction
Emitter Heavily doped Designed to inject a large number of majority carriers Supplies carriers into the base region
Base Very lightly doped Extremely thin compared with the other regions Controls carrier transport from emitter to collector
Collector Moderately doped Usually physically larger to handle power and dissipate heat Collects carriers and supports the collector-base electric field

Why is the base thin and lightly doped?

In normal forward-active operation, carriers injected from the emitter pass through the base. A thin, lightly doped base reduces carrier recombination, allowing most injected carriers to reach the collector.

Why is the collector generally larger?

The collector often handles higher voltage and dissipates significant power. Its physical design helps with heat removal and voltage handling. Exact geometry depends on the transistor technology and rating.

Do not confuse doping with size: The emitter is generally the most heavily doped region, while the collector is often the physically largest region. These are different properties.

5. The Three Transistor Terminals

Emitter (E)

  • Heavily doped region.
  • Injects majority carriers into the base.
  • Emitter arrow is shown on this terminal in the circuit symbol.

Base (B)

  • Very thin and lightly doped.
  • Controls transistor conduction.
  • A small base current can control a larger collector current in normal active operation.

Collector (C)

  • Collects carriers transported across the base.
  • Usually designed for higher voltage and power handling.
  • Often has a larger physical area.

Basic current relationship

IE = IB + IC

For a transistor operating with the usual current reference directions, emitter current is the sum of base current and collector current. Detailed current gain relationships are covered in Lecture 4.

6. NPN and PNP Circuit Symbols

The arrow on the emitter is the most useful visual feature for distinguishing the two symbols.

NPN symbol

NPN: Arrow points outward, away from the base.

PNP symbol

PNP: Arrow points inward, toward the base.

Memory aid: NPN = Not Pointing iN. The NPN arrow points out; the PNP arrow points in.

7. Why a BJT Is Not Simply Two Diodes

A transistor contains two PN junctions, but it cannot be treated as two independent diodes connected back-to-back.

  • The two junctions share a common, very thin base region.
  • Carrier injection at the emitter-base junction influences current through the collector-base junction.
  • The base region allows carrier transport between the two junctions.
  • Two separate diodes do not reproduce this transistor action.
A diode-mode test can help identify junctions and detect some faults. It does not prove that a transistor has correct current gain or performs properly under operating conditions.

8. Common Transistor Packages

Transistors are available in different packages depending on power dissipation, current rating, mounting method and application.

TO-92

Small through-hole package often used for low-power signal transistors.

TO-220

Power package commonly used with a heatsink or mounting surface.

TO-3

Metal power-transistor package designed for mechanical mounting and heat transfer.

Pinout warning: Never assume that every TO-92 or TO-220 transistor has the same E-B-C lead order. Pin assignment varies by part number and manufacturer. Always consult the exact datasheet and package drawing.

9. Identifying Transistor Terminals Using a Datasheet

  1. Read the complete part number printed on the transistor body.
  2. Find the datasheet from the manufacturer or a reliable component distributor.
  3. Open the package information or pin configuration section.
  4. Check the viewing direction: front view, bottom view or top view.
  5. Match the lead numbers to the E, B and C designations.
  6. Check maximum ratings before using the device in a circuit.

Datasheet parameters to check

ParameterMeaning
VCEOCollector-emitter voltage rating with base open, under specified conditions
ICSpecified collector current rating
PDMaximum power dissipation under stated thermal conditions
hFEDC current gain under specified test conditions
fTTransition frequency, a measure related to high-frequency performance
VBEBase-emitter voltage under specified current and temperature conditions

10. Basic Multimeter Testing of a BJT

A digital multimeter in diode mode can be used to check the two PN junctions of a transistor.

Typical diode-mode procedure

DeviceRed probeBlack probeExpected forward reading
NPNBaseEmitterUsually a forward-junction voltage
NPNBaseCollectorUsually a forward-junction voltage
PNPEmitterBaseUsually a forward-junction voltage
PNPCollectorBaseUsually a forward-junction voltage

Reverse the probes for each junction. A healthy isolated junction should generally show no forward conduction in the reverse direction. The exact reading depends on the device and meter.

Practical testing steps

  1. Switch off and disconnect the circuit supply.
  2. Discharge capacitors safely.
  3. Identify the part number and verify its pinout if possible.
  4. Use diode mode and test base-emitter and base-collector junctions.
  5. Reverse the probes and compare the readings.
  6. Investigate very low readings in both directions or open readings in both directions.
In-circuit components can affect readings. For reliable testing, remove the transistor or isolate at least the relevant terminals. A diode test alone cannot determine whether the transistor has adequate gain or can carry its rated load current.

11. Worked Examples and Quick Checks

Example 1: Identify the transistor type

A transistor has the semiconductor layer sequence N-P-N. Identify its type and the emitter arrow direction.

Solution: It is an NPN transistor. Its emitter arrow points outward, away from the base.

Example 2: Identify the regions

A transistor has a heavily doped region, a very thin lightly doped region and a moderately doped region. Identify the terminals.

Solution: The heavily doped region is the emitter, the thin lightly doped region is the base, and the moderately doped region is the collector.

Example 3: Emitter current

A transistor has a base current of 40 µA and collector current of 4 mA. Calculate emitter current.

IE = IB + IC = 0.04 mA + 4 mA = 4.04 mA

12. Practical Lab Exercise

Experiment: Identification of BJT Construction and Terminals

Objective

To identify NPN and PNP transistors, understand their terminal arrangement and test their PN junctions using a digital multimeter.

Equipment Required

  • One NPN transistor, such as BC547 or 2N2222.
  • One PNP transistor, such as BC557 or 2N2907.
  • Digital multimeter with diode mode.
  • Manufacturer datasheets.
  • Breadboard and connecting wires, if required for later testing.

Procedure

  1. Record the complete part number of each transistor.
  2. Find the manufacturer's pin configuration.
  3. Draw the NPN and PNP layer structures.
  4. Mark emitter, base and collector on the device pinout drawing.
  5. Use diode mode to test the base-emitter and base-collector junctions.
  6. Reverse the probes and record the results.
  7. Compare the readings with the expected junction behavior.

Observation Table

TestProbe connectionObserved reading
NPN B-ERed to B, black to ERecord meter reading
NPN B-CRed to B, black to CRecord meter reading
PNP B-ERed to E, black to BRecord meter reading
PNP B-CRed to C, black to BRecord meter reading

Expected Result

The NPN and PNP structures and their terminal designations are identified from the datasheets. Their two PN junctions show diode-like forward and reverse behavior when tested correctly in isolation.

Safety

  • Never use resistance or diode mode on an energized circuit.
  • Observe electrostatic discharge precautions when handling sensitive semiconductor devices.
  • Do not exceed transistor voltage, current or power ratings.

14. Placement Interview Questions and Answers

Q1. What is a BJT?

A three-terminal semiconductor device used for amplification and switching.

Q2. Why is it called bipolar?

Because both electrons and holes participate in its operation.

Q3. What are the three terminals?

Emitter, base and collector.

Q4. What are the two types of BJT?

NPN and PNP.

Q5. What is the emitter's doping level?

It is generally heavily doped to inject a large number of carriers.

Q6. Why is the base very thin?

To reduce carrier recombination so that most injected carriers can cross the base.

Q7. Why is the collector usually larger?

It is designed to handle voltage and power dissipation, including heat removal.

Q8. Which terminal has an arrow in the circuit symbol?

The emitter.

Q9. What is the arrow direction for an NPN transistor?

Outward, away from the base.

Q10. What is the arrow direction for a PNP transistor?

Inward, toward the base.

Q11. How many PN junctions are present in a BJT?

Two: emitter-base and collector-base.

Q12. Why are two separate diodes not equivalent to a BJT?

They do not have the shared thin base region that enables carrier transport and transistor action.

Q13. What does hFE represent?

DC current gain under specified operating conditions.

Q14. Can the TO-92 pinout always be assumed?

No. The pinout depends on the specific part and manufacturer.

Q15. How can a multimeter help identify transistor junctions?

Diode mode can test the base-emitter and base-collector PN junctions, provided the device is sufficiently isolated.

Q16. Does a successful diode test guarantee a good transistor?

No. It does not verify current gain, leakage under operating conditions or load-handling capability.

Q17. What is the basic current relationship of a BJT?

Emitter current equals the sum of base current and collector current using the standard current reference directions.

Q18. Which region controls the transistor's carrier transport?

The thin base region, through which carriers travel from emitter toward collector in forward-active operation.

17. Lecture Summary

  • A BJT is a three-terminal bipolar semiconductor device.
  • The terminals are emitter, base and collector.
  • NPN has an N-P-N structure; PNP has a P-N-P structure.
  • The emitter is heavily doped, the base is thin and lightly doped, and the collector is generally moderately doped.
  • The emitter arrow points out for NPN and in for PNP.
  • Exact terminal pinouts must be checked in the manufacturer's datasheet.
  • Multimeter diode testing is useful for preliminary junction checks but does not fully validate transistor performance.
