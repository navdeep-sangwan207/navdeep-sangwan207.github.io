Lesson 4: BJT Operating Regions and Modes of Operation

Understand cutoff, active, saturation and reverse-active regions with junction biasing and practical applications.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand the operating regions of a BJT.
  • Understand forward and reverse biasing of PN junctions.
  • Identify cutoff, active, saturation and reverse-active regions.
  • Understand the relationship between base current and collector current.
  • Understand the difference between switching and amplification modes.
  • Identify practical applications of each operating region.

2. Introduction to BJT Operating Regions

A BJT contains two PN junctions:

  • Emitter-Base (E-B) junction.
  • Collector-Base (C-B) junction.

The operating region of a transistor depends mainly on the biasing conditions of these two junctions.

A BJT has four commonly discussed operating regions:

  1. Cutoff region.
  2. Forward-active region.
  3. Saturation region.
  4. Reverse-active region.
Important: A transistor is commonly operated in cutoff and saturation for switching applications, and in the forward-active region for linear amplification.

3. Junction Biasing Conditions

Each PN junction can be forward biased or reverse biased. The combination of the two junction conditions determines the transistor operating region.

Forward Bias

A PN junction is forward biased when its P-side is at a higher potential than its N-side by a sufficient amount. This reduces the depletion barrier and allows significant current to flow.

Reverse Bias

A PN junction is reverse biased when its P-side is at a lower potential than its N-side. The depletion region widens, and only a small leakage current normally flows before breakdown.

Junction biasing is determined by comparing the voltages on the P-type and N-type sides, not simply by whether a terminal voltage is positive or negative relative to ground.

4. Four Operating Regions of a BJT

Operating Region E-B Junction C-B Junction
Cutoff Reverse biased Reverse biased
Forward Active Forward biased Reverse biased
Saturation Forward biased Forward biased
Reverse Active Reverse biased Forward biased
The table describes the standard junction-bias classification. Reverse-active operation is uncommon in ordinary circuit design because transistor gain is usually much lower in that mode.

5. Cutoff Region

In the cutoff region, both the emitter-base and collector-base junctions are reverse biased.

The transistor is effectively OFF. Ideally, collector current is zero, although a real transistor has a small leakage current.

Characteristics

  • Both junctions are reverse biased.
  • Base current is approximately zero.
  • Collector current is approximately zero, neglecting leakage.
  • Collector-emitter voltage is generally high in a typical switching circuit.
  • Transistor behaves like an open switch.
IB ≈ 0

IC ≈ 0

Practical Example: OFF State

In an NPN low-side switching circuit, if the base is not supplied with sufficient drive current, the transistor remains in cutoff and the load is OFF.

Application: The cutoff region is used to turn OFF LEDs, relays, motors and other loads through transistor switching circuits.

6. Forward-Active Region

In the forward-active region:

  • The emitter-base junction is forward biased.
  • The collector-base junction is reverse biased.

The transistor operates as an amplifier in this region. A small base-current variation produces a larger collector-current variation.

Characteristics

  • Emitter-base junction forward biased.
  • Collector-base junction reverse biased.
  • Collector current is controlled primarily by base-emitter drive.
  • Collector current is approximately proportional to base current under suitable conditions.
  • Collector-emitter voltage is above the saturation voltage.
IC ≈ β IB

Here, β is the common-emitter DC current gain. Its value depends on the transistor, collector current, temperature and operating conditions.

Practical Example: Amplifier

A common-emitter amplifier uses a transistor biased in the forward-active region so that small input signal variations produce larger output signal variations.

Application: Audio amplifiers, sensor signal conditioning, preamplifiers and analog signal-processing circuits.

7. Saturation Region

In the saturation region, both the emitter-base and collector-base junctions are forward biased.

The transistor is strongly ON. Increasing base current further does not produce a proportional increase in collector current because the external load and supply limit the current.

Characteristics

  • Both PN junctions are forward biased.
  • Collector current is limited mainly by the external circuit.
  • Collector-emitter voltage is low.
  • Transistor behaves approximately like a closed switch.
VCE ≈ VCE(sat)

For many small silicon switching transistors, VCE(sat) may be around 0.1–0.3 V under specified test conditions. Always check the datasheet.

Practical Example: ON State

When sufficient base drive is applied to an NPN switching transistor, it enters saturation and allows current to flow through the load.

Application: LED switching, relay drivers, motor drivers and digital control circuits.

8. Reverse-Active Region

In the reverse-active region:

  • The emitter-base junction is reverse biased.
  • The collector-base junction is forward biased.

In this condition, the collector and emitter effectively exchange their injection and collection roles.

Characteristics

  • Emitter-base junction reverse biased.
  • Collector-base junction forward biased.
  • Current gain is generally much lower than in forward-active operation.
  • It is rarely used in ordinary transistor circuits.
The emitter and collector are not physically interchangeable. Their doping and construction are different, which is why reverse-active operation generally has poor performance.

11. Numerical Example: Active Region

An NPN transistor operates in the forward-active region. Its DC current gain is 100 and its base current is 40 µA. Calculate the collector current.

Given:

  • β = 100
  • IB = 40 µA

Formula:

IC = β × IB

Calculation:

IC = 100 × 40 µA

IC = 4000 µA

IC = 4 mA

Therefore, the collector current is 4 mA, assuming the transistor remains in the forward-active region.

12. Numerical Example: Saturation Check

An NPN transistor has a supply voltage of 12 V and a collector load resistance of 1 kΩ. Assume its saturation voltage is 0.2 V. Calculate the approximate collector current in saturation.

Formula:

IC(sat) = (VCC - VCE(sat)) / RC

Calculation:

IC(sat) = (12 - 0.2) / 1000

IC(sat) = 0.0118 A

IC(sat) = 11.8 mA

The external circuit limits the collector current to approximately 11.8 mA.

The base drive must be sufficient to support this load current. A practical switching design uses a suitable forced beta and checks the transistor's datasheet saturation conditions.

13. Important Interview Questions

Q1. How many operating regions does a BJT have?

Four commonly discussed regions: cutoff, forward active, saturation and reverse active.

Q2. Which region is used for amplification?

Forward-active region.

Q3. Which regions are used for switching?

Cutoff and saturation.

Q4. What are the biasing conditions in cutoff?

Both emitter-base and collector-base junctions are reverse biased.

Q5. What are the biasing conditions in saturation?

Both emitter-base and collector-base junctions are forward biased.

Q6. What is the typical collector-emitter voltage in saturation?

It is low and depends on the transistor and operating current. A value around 0.1–0.3 V is common for many small silicon BJTs.

Q7. What happens when base drive is removed from a switching transistor?

The transistor moves toward cutoff and the load turns OFF.

Q8. Why is reverse-active operation rarely used?

Because the transistor generally has much lower current gain in reverse-active operation.

Q9. What limits collector current in saturation?

The supply voltage, load resistance and other external circuit conditions.

Q10. Can a transistor be used as both a switch and an amplifier?

Yes. Its operating region and circuit configuration determine its function.

15. Lesson Summary

  • Cutoff: both junctions reverse biased; transistor OFF.
  • Forward active: E-B forward biased and C-B reverse biased; used for amplification.
  • Saturation: both junctions forward biased; transistor ON.
  • Reverse active: E-B reverse biased and C-B forward biased; rarely used.
  • Collector current in active operation is approximately β times base current.
  • In saturation, collector current is limited mainly by the external circuit.
  • Correct base drive is essential for reliable switching.
