2. What is an Electronic Signal?

An electronic signal is a time-varying electrical quantity, such as voltage or current, used to represent or transmit information.

Signals are used in electronic circuits for measurement, communication, control and data processing.

Two major signal categories:
  • Analog signals
  • Digital signals

3. Analog Signals

An analog signal is a signal whose amplitude can vary continuously over a range of values.

It can represent physical quantities such as temperature, pressure, sound, light intensity and voltage.

3.1 Characteristics of Analog Signals

  • Amplitude varies continuously.
  • Can have infinitely many possible values within a specified range.
  • Commonly represented by smooth waveforms.
  • Noise can directly affect the signal amplitude.
  • Used in sensors, amplifiers and analog communication circuits.

3.2 Analog Signal Waveform

Observation: The signal changes smoothly and continuously with time.

3.3 Examples of Analog Signals

  • Microphone output voltage.
  • Temperature sensor output.
  • Audio signals.
  • Light sensor output.
  • Analog voltage from a potentiometer.

4. Digital Signals

A digital signal represents information using discrete signal levels. In binary digital systems, the two commonly used logical states are 0 and 1.

These states are represented by defined voltage ranges, rather than requiring every voltage to have one exact value.

4.1 Characteristics of Digital Signals

  • Uses discrete signal levels.
  • Binary systems commonly use Logic 0 and Logic 1.
  • Can represent data in binary form.
  • Logic thresholds help distinguish valid HIGH and LOW states.
  • Widely used in computers, microcontrollers and digital communication.

4.2 Digital Signal Waveform

Observation: The signal switches between defined LOW and HIGH levels.

4.3 Examples of Digital Signals

  • Microcontroller GPIO output.
  • Digital clock signals.
  • UART communication data.
  • Digital sensor output.
  • Computer binary data.

5. Basic Signal Parameters

5.1 Amplitude

Amplitude is the maximum magnitude of a signal measured relative to its reference level.

5.2 Time Period

Time period (T) is the time required for one complete cycle of a periodic signal.

5.3 Frequency

Frequency (f) is the number of complete cycles per second. Its SI unit is Hertz (Hz).

Frequency and Time Period Relationship:

\[ f = \frac{1}{T} \]

Where:

  • f = Frequency in Hertz
  • T = Time period in seconds

5.4 Phase

Phase describes the relative position of a periodic waveform with respect to a reference waveform.

7. Conversion Between Analog and Digital Signals

Many real-world quantities are analog, but microcontrollers and digital processors work with digital data. Therefore, conversion circuits are required.

7.1 Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC)

An ADC converts an analog input into a digital numerical representation.

Basic process:

  1. Sampling
  2. Quantization
  3. Encoding

7.2 Digital-to-Analog Converter (DAC)

A DAC converts a digital numerical value into a corresponding analog output signal.

Practical example: A microcontroller can read an analog temperature sensor through its ADC, process the digital value and use a DAC or PWM-based output circuit to control an analog device.

ADC and DAC operation will be studied in detail in the Data Converters chapter.

8. Introduction to Sampling and Quantization

8.1 Sampling

Sampling is the process of measuring an analog signal at regular time intervals to obtain discrete-time samples.

According to the Nyquist sampling criterion, the sampling frequency should be greater than twice the highest frequency component of a band-limited signal for ideal reconstruction.

\[ f_s > 2f_{max} \]

Where:

  • fs = Sampling frequency
  • fmax = Highest signal frequency

8.2 Quantization

Quantization maps each sampled amplitude to one of a finite number of available digital levels.

The difference between the original sample and its quantized representation is called quantization error.

8.3 Encoding

Encoding represents the quantized level as a binary digital code.

9. Practical Example: Reading a Potentiometer Using ADC

A potentiometer can provide a variable analog voltage to a microcontroller ADC input.

Working Principle

  1. Connect the potentiometer between the supply and ground.
  2. Connect its wiper to an ADC input pin.
  3. Rotate the potentiometer to change its output voltage.
  4. The ADC samples and converts this voltage into a digital value.
  5. The microcontroller uses that value in its program.

This is a common embedded-systems application of analog and digital signal processing.

12. Lesson Summary

  • Digital signals use discrete amplitude levels.
