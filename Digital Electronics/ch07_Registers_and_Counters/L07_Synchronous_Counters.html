2. What Is a Synchronous Counter?

A synchronous counter is a sequential digital circuit in which all flip-flops receive the same external clock signal.

All flip-flops are triggered by the same active clock edge. Their outputs change after their individual clock-to-Q propagation delays.

Unlike an asynchronous counter, a synchronous counter does not use the output of one flip-flop as the clock input of the next flip-flop.

3. Construction of a 3-Bit Synchronous Counter

A three-bit synchronous binary counter can be constructed using three JK flip-flops and combinational logic.

For a binary up-counter:

  • FF0 toggles on every active clock edge.
  • FF1 toggles when Q0 is HIGH.
  • FF2 toggles when Q1 and Q0 are both HIGH.
  • All three flip-flops receive a common clock.
The diagram shows the toggle conditions inside the flip-flops. In a practical implementation, the Q0 signal and the Q1 AND Q0 combination must be connected to the appropriate J and K inputs.

4. Working Principle

All flip-flops receive the same clock. Combinational logic determines which flip-flops must toggle at each active clock edge.

Toggle Conditions

T0 = 1
T1 = Q0
T2 = Q1 · Q0

Here, T0, T1 and T2 represent the toggle conditions for the three counter stages.

For JK flip-flops, the same conditions can be applied to both J and K inputs:

J0 = K0 = 1
J1 = K1 = Q0
J2 = K2 = Q1 · Q0

6. Next-State Equations

A T flip-flop toggles when T = 1 and holds its state when T = 0. Therefore, the next-state equations are:

Q0(next) = Q0̅
Q1(next) = Q1 ⊕ Q0
Q2(next) = Q2 ⊕ (Q1 · Q0)

The symbol ⊕ represents XOR. The equations describe a three-bit synchronous binary up-counter.

7. Construction Using T Flip-Flops

A synchronous counter can also be constructed using T flip-flops. Their toggle inputs are driven by combinational logic.

Flip-Flop Toggle Input Condition
FF0 T0 Always toggle
FF1 T1 Toggle when Q0 = 1
FF2 T2 Toggle when Q1Q0 = 11

For an n-bit binary up-counter, the toggle condition for bit i is the logical AND of all lower-order bits.

Ti = Q(i-1) · Q(i-2) · ... · Q0

For the least significant bit:

T0 = 1

8. Four-Bit Synchronous Up-Counter

A four-bit synchronous up-counter requires four flip-flops, all driven by the same clock.

Its toggle conditions are:

T0 = 1
T1 = Q0
T2 = Q1 · Q0
T3 = Q2 · Q1 · Q0

The counter has sixteen states:

MOD = 24 = 16

Its counting sequence is 0000, 0001, 0010, and so on, up to 1111, followed by 0000.

9. Timing and Propagation Delay

All flip-flops receive the same clock edge, so the counter avoids the stage-by-stage clock triggering used in a ripple counter.

However, the outputs do not change at exactly the same physical instant. Each flip-flop has its own clock-to-Q propagation delay.

The combinational toggle logic must also settle within the required timing constraints before the next active clock edge.

Synchronous counters generally offer better high-speed performance than comparable ripple counters, but their maximum clock frequency depends on flip-flop delay, logic depth, setup time and loading.

10. Advantages of Synchronous Counters

  • All flip-flops receive a common clock.
  • No ripple clock propagation through stages.
  • Generally faster than comparable asynchronous counters.
  • Suitable for high-speed digital systems.
  • Predictable state transitions at clock edges.
  • Useful for processor and controller designs.

11. Disadvantages of Synchronous Counters

  • Requires additional combinational logic.
  • Design becomes more complex for large counters.
  • Clock distribution must be carefully designed.
  • Logic propagation delay limits the maximum clock frequency.
  • May require more hardware than a simple ripple counter.

12. Applications of Synchronous Counters

  • Digital clocks and timers.
  • Microprocessor and microcontroller systems.
  • High-speed event counters.
  • Frequency measurement circuits.
  • Digital control systems.
  • Address sequencing circuits.
  • Programmable logic and FPGA designs.
  • Industrial automation systems.

13. Synchronous vs Asynchronous Counters

Parameter Synchronous Asynchronous
Clock Common clock for all stages External clock drives first stage
Triggering All stages triggered by same clock edge Stages trigger one after another
Delay Clock-to-Q and logic delay Accumulated ripple delay
Speed Generally higher Generally lower
Design More combinational logic Simple interconnection
Typical Use High-speed counting Low-speed counting and division

17. Lesson Summary

  • A synchronous counter uses a common clock for all flip-flops.
  • All stages respond to the same active clock edge.
  • Combinational logic determines which flip-flops toggle.
  • An n-bit binary counter has 2n states.
  • Synchronous counters generally provide better high-speed performance than ripple counters.
  • They are widely used in digital systems, processors and embedded applications.
