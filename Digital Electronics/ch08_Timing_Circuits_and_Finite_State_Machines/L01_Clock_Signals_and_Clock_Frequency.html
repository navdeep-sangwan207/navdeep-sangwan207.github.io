2. What Is a Clock Signal?

A clock signal is a periodic digital waveform used to synchronize the operation of sequential digital circuits.

It repeatedly switches between two logic levels:

  • Logic HIGH (1): Higher voltage level.
  • Logic LOW (0): Lower voltage level.

The clock signal controls when flip-flops, registers, counters and processors update their states.

A clock provides a timing reference for coordinated operation of digital circuits. It does not itself carry the data being processed.

3. Clock Signal Waveform

A typical digital clock signal is represented by a periodic square wave.

One complete cycle consists of one HIGH interval and one LOW interval.

4. Clock Period

The clock period is the time required to complete one full cycle of a periodic clock signal.

It is represented by the symbol T.

T = Time for One Complete Cycle

The SI unit of period is the second (s). Smaller units commonly used in digital electronics include:

  • Millisecond (ms) = 10-3 s
  • Microsecond (µs) = 10-6 s
  • Nanosecond (ns) = 10-9 s
  • Picosecond (ps) = 10-12 s

5. Clock Frequency

Clock frequency is the number of complete clock cycles generated in one second.

It is represented by f and measured in Hertz (Hz).

f = 1 / T

Similarly:

T = 1 / f
Frequency and time period are inversely proportional. A higher clock frequency means a shorter clock period.

Common Frequency Units

Unit Equivalent Value
1 Hz 1 cycle per second
1 kHz 1,000 Hz
1 MHz 1,000,000 Hz
1 GHz 1,000,000,000 Hz

6. Relationship Between Frequency and Period

Example 1: Calculate Frequency

A clock signal has a time period of 10 µs. Calculate its frequency.

Given:

T = 10 µs = 10 × 10-6 s

Formula:

f = 1 / T

Substitution:

f = 1 / (10 × 10-6)

Answer:

f = 100 kHz

Example 2: Calculate Time Period

A microcontroller operates with a clock frequency of 20 MHz. Calculate the clock period.

Given:

f = 20 MHz = 20 × 106 Hz

Formula:

T = 1 / f

Substitution:

T = 1 / (20 × 106)

Answer:

T = 50 ns

7. Duty Cycle

Duty cycle is the percentage of one clock period during which the clock signal remains HIGH.

Duty Cycle = (TON / T) × 100%

Where:

  • TON = HIGH time.
  • TOFF = LOW time.
  • T = Total clock period.

The total period is:

T = TON + TOFF

Different Duty Cycles

Duty Cycle HIGH Time LOW Time
25% 25% of period 75% of period
50% 50% of period 50% of period
75% 75% of period 25% of period

Example 3: Duty Cycle Calculation

A clock has a total period of 20 µs and remains HIGH for 8 µs. Calculate its duty cycle.

Duty Cycle = (8 / 20) × 100
Duty Cycle = 40%

8. Rising Edge and Falling Edge

A clock signal has two important transitions.

Rising Edge (Positive Edge)

The transition of a clock signal from logic LOW to logic HIGH is called the rising edge.

Falling Edge (Negative Edge)

The transition of a clock signal from logic HIGH to logic LOW is called the falling edge.

A positive-edge-triggered flip-flop responds to the active rising edge. A negative-edge-triggered flip-flop responds to the active falling edge.

9. Edge-Triggered and Level-Triggered Circuits

Edge-Triggered Circuits

An edge-triggered flip-flop changes its stored state in response to a specified clock transition.

  • Positive-edge-triggered flip-flop.
  • Negative-edge-triggered flip-flop.

Level-Triggered Circuits

A level-sensitive latch responds while its enable or clock input remains at the active logic level.

  • Active-HIGH latch.
  • Active-LOW latch.

10. Clock Signals in Sequential Circuits

Clock signals synchronize the operation of several sequential digital circuits.

  • Flip-Flops: Store one bit of data.
  • Registers: Store multiple bits of data.
  • Counters: Change their count according to clock pulses.
  • Shift Registers: Shift data at clock events.
  • Processors: Coordinate internal sequential operations.
  • Finite State Machines: Update states at defined clock edges in synchronous designs.

11. Clock Signals in Microcontrollers

A microcontroller uses clock signals to coordinate its CPU and internal peripherals.

Depending on the device architecture, the clock system may include:

  • External crystal oscillator.
  • Internal RC oscillator.
  • Phase-Locked Loop (PLL).
  • CPU clock.
  • Peripheral clock.
  • Clock prescalers and dividers.
In an embedded system, the CPU clock frequency and peripheral clock frequency may be different. Timer, UART and other peripheral timing calculations must use the appropriate peripheral clock.

Example: LPC2129 Clock Configuration

Consider an LPC2129 system configured with the following clock values:

Clock Parameter Value
Crystal Frequency (FOSC) 12 MHz
CPU Clock (CCLK) 60 MHz
Peripheral Clock (PCLK) 15 MHz
Peripheral Clock Divider 4

Peripheral clock:

PCLK = CCLK / 4
PCLK = 60 MHz / 4 = 15 MHz

The peripheral clock period is:

T = 1 / (15 × 106)
T ≈ 66.67 ns

This peripheral clock value is used in timer and peripheral configuration calculations where the selected peripheral is clocked from PCLK.

12. Clock Frequency and System Performance

Clock frequency is one factor affecting digital system performance. A higher frequency allows more clock cycles per second.

However, increasing frequency does not guarantee a proportional increase in overall performance.

Performance also depends on:

  • Processor architecture.
  • Instruction execution cycles.
  • Memory access time.
  • Pipeline design.
  • Bus and peripheral speed.
  • Power and thermal limitations.
  • Timing constraints.

16. Lesson Summary

  • A clock signal synchronizes sequential digital circuits.
  • Clock period is the time required for one complete cycle.
  • Clock frequency is the number of cycles per second.
  • Frequency and period are inversely proportional.
  • Duty cycle represents the percentage of time the clock remains HIGH.
  • Rising and falling edges are important triggering events.
  • Microcontrollers use clock sources, PLLs and clock dividers.
  • Correct clock configuration is essential for reliable embedded-system timing.
