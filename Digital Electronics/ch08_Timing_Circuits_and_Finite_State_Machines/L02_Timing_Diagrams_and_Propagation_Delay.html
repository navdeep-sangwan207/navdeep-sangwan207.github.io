2. What Is a Timing Diagram?

A timing diagram is a graphical representation of digital signals with respect to time.

It shows how logic signals change between HIGH and LOW states and how different signals relate to one another.

Timing diagrams are used to understand:

  • Clock signals.
  • Data input and output.
  • Control signals.
  • Propagation delays.
  • Signal transitions.
  • Setup and hold requirements.
Timing diagrams are especially important when analysing flip-flops, counters, registers, microcontrollers and digital communication protocols.

5. What Is Propagation Delay?

Propagation delay is the time required for a change at the input of a digital circuit to produce the corresponding change at its output.

It is commonly represented by tPD.

tPD = Output Transition Time − Input Transition Time

Propagation delay occurs because real electronic components do not respond instantaneously.

Delay depends on:

  • Logic gate technology.
  • Supply voltage.
  • Temperature.
  • Output load capacitance.
  • Input transition time.
  • Internal circuit design.

7. tPLH and tPHL

Propagation delay is commonly specified separately for output LOW-to-HIGH and HIGH-to-LOW transitions.

tPLH – LOW-to-HIGH Output Delay

Time between the input transition and the corresponding output transition from LOW to HIGH.

tPHL – HIGH-to-LOW Output Delay

Time between the input transition and the corresponding output transition from HIGH to LOW.

Parameter Meaning
tPLH Output LOW-to-HIGH propagation delay
tPHL Output HIGH-to-LOW propagation delay

A commonly used approximate average propagation delay is:

tPD(avg) = (tPLH + tPHL) / 2

This average is useful for comparison, but timing analysis must use the appropriate specified maximum or minimum delay values.

8. Rise Time and Fall Time

Rise Time (tR)

Rise time is the time required for a signal to move from a lower voltage level to a higher voltage level.

It is commonly measured between 10% and 90% of the signal's voltage transition.

Fall Time (tF)

Fall time is the time required for a signal to move from a higher voltage level to a lower voltage level.

It is commonly measured between 90% and 10% of the voltage transition.

The diagram illustrates signal transitions. The standard 10%-to-90% rise-time and 90%-to-10% fall-time definitions are measured using the specified reference levels.

9. Clock-to-Q Propagation Delay

In a flip-flop, the output does not change instantaneously when the active clock edge arrives.

The time between the active clock edge and the corresponding output transition is called clock-to-Q propagation delay.

It is commonly represented as:

tCLK-Q

Clock-to-Q delay is important when designing:

  • Registers.
  • Synchronous counters.
  • Shift registers.
  • Finite state machines.
  • Processor pipelines.

10. Maximum Operating Frequency

Digital circuits cannot operate at unlimited clock frequencies. The signals must have sufficient time to propagate through the combinational logic and meet the receiving flip-flop's setup requirement.

For a simple register-to-register path, a common setup timing relationship is:

TCLK ≥ tCLK-Q(max) + tCOMB(max) + tSETUP

This simplified relationship assumes an ideal clock with no skew and omits additional implementation margins.

The corresponding simplified maximum frequency is:

fMAX ≤ 1 / TCLK(min)
In a real design, clock uncertainty, skew, jitter, routing delays and other timing constraints must also be considered.

11. Timing Diagram in Digital Communication

Timing diagrams are also used to understand digital communication interfaces.

  • UART: Shows start bits, data bits, parity and stop bits.
  • SPI: Shows serial clock, MOSI, MISO and chip-select signals.
  • I2C: Shows SCL and SDA signal relationships.
  • CAN: Shows bus-level transitions and bit timing.
For embedded-system debugging, timing diagrams help identify incorrect clock polarity, signal timing, data setup problems and communication errors.

12. Practical Example: Propagation Delay

Example 1: Calculate Average Delay

A logic gate has:

  • tPLH = 12 ns
  • tPHL = 8 ns

Formula:

tPD(avg) = (tPLH + tPHL) / 2

Substitution:

tPD(avg) = (12 + 8) / 2

Answer:

tPD(avg) = 10 ns

Example 2: Delay Through Multiple Gates

A signal passes through four identical logic gates. Each gate has a propagation delay of 5 ns. Calculate the approximate total delay.

Total Delay = Number of Gates × Delay per Gate
Total Delay = 4 × 5 ns = 20 ns

This is a simplified calculation assuming the same delay for each stage and a single relevant propagation path.

13. Practical Importance of Timing Analysis

Timing analysis is essential for ensuring reliable operation of digital hardware.

  • Prevents incorrect data sampling.
  • Helps determine maximum operating frequency.
  • Identifies excessive logic delays.
  • Supports reliable register-to-register data transfer.
  • Helps debug digital communication interfaces.
  • Improves reliability of microcontroller-based systems.
