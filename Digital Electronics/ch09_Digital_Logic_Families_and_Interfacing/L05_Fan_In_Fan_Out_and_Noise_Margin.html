Lesson 5: Fan-In, Fan-Out and Noise Margin

Chapter 9 – Digital Logic Families and Interfacing

1. Learning Objectives

  • Understand fan-in and fan-out in digital logic circuits.
  • Learn input loading and output driving capability.

  • Understand noise margin in digital circuits.
  • Calculate HIGH and LOW noise margins.
  • Apply these parameters while designing digital and embedded systems.

2. What Is Fan-In?

Fan-in is the number of inputs provided by a logic gate.

For example, a two-input NAND gate has a fan-in of 2, while a four-input NAND gate has a fan-in of 4.

Examples of Logic Gate Fan-In
Logic Gate Number of Inputs Fan-In
2-input AND 2 2
3-input OR 3 3
4-input NAND 4 4
8-input NOR 8 8

Why Is Fan-In Important?

  • Determines how many signals a gate can process directly.
  • Affects circuit complexity and gate selection.
  • Can influence propagation delay and input capacitance.
  • Helps in designing combinational logic circuits.
Fan-in is a property of the logic gate's number of inputs. It is not the number of other gates connected to its output.

3. What Is Fan-Out?

Fan-out is the maximum number of standard logic inputs that one gate output can drive while maintaining valid logic levels and acceptable operation.

When one gate output is connected to several other gate inputs, the output must supply or absorb the required current.

Example

Suppose one logic gate output drives four other logic gate inputs. Its fan-out in that connection is four.

Factors Affecting Fan-Out

  • Output HIGH current capability.
  • Output LOW current capability.
  • Input HIGH and LOW currents of connected gates.
  • Input and wiring capacitance.
  • Required switching speed.
  • Operating voltage and temperature.

4. Source Current and Sink Current

Source Current

Source current is the current delivered by a logic output to an external load.

It is generally associated with the output HIGH state.

Sink Current

Sink current is the current absorbed by a logic output from an external load.

It is generally associated with the output LOW state.

A digital IC datasheet may specify separate source and sink current limits. Both must be considered when calculating fan-out.

5. Fan-Out Calculation

For a logic family with current-based specifications, fan-out is calculated separately for HIGH and LOW logic states.

Fan-Out (HIGH) = |IOH(max)| / IIH(max)
Fan-Out (LOW) = IOL(max) / |IIL(max)|

Where:

  • IOH = Output HIGH current specification.
  • IOL = Output LOW current specification.
  • IIH = Input HIGH current specification.
  • IIL = Input LOW current specification.

The allowable fan-out is limited by the smaller of the HIGH-state and LOW-state results.

Numerical Example

Consider a hypothetical logic device with these specifications:

  • Maximum output HIGH source-current magnitude = 400 µA.
  • Maximum input HIGH current = 40 µA.
  • Maximum output LOW sink current = 8 mA.
  • Maximum input LOW current magnitude = 1.6 mA.
Step 1: Calculate HIGH-state fan-out
Fan-Out (HIGH) = 400 / 40 = 10
Step 2: Calculate LOW-state fan-out
Fan-Out (LOW) = 8 / 1.6 = 5
Step 3: Determine allowable fan-out
Fan-Out = Minimum (10, 5) = 5

Therefore, the theoretical current-based fan-out is 5. Actual loading and timing requirements must also be checked.

6. Fan-Out in TTL Logic

TTL fan-out is determined by the current-driving capability of the output stage and the input current requirements of connected TTL gates.

Traditional standard TTL examples often use a fan-out of 10 under specified conditions. This is not a universal value for every TTL family or device.

Do not assume a fixed fan-out for all TTL ICs. Use the datasheet's maximum input current and guaranteed output current specifications.

7. Fan-Out in CMOS Logic

CMOS inputs have very high DC input impedance and draw very little steady-state input current.

Therefore, the DC current-based fan-out of CMOS logic can be very high.

However, each connected CMOS input adds input capacitance. More load capacitance can increase propagation delay and switching power.

In CMOS circuits, fan-out is often limited by capacitive loading and timing requirements, rather than only by DC input current.

8. What Is Noise in Digital Circuits?

Noise is an unwanted electrical signal or disturbance that affects the intended voltage or current in a digital circuit.

Common Sources of Noise

  • Electromagnetic interference (EMI).
  • Switching transients.
  • Ground bounce.
  • Power-supply fluctuations.
  • Long PCB tracks and wires.
  • Capacitive and inductive coupling.

Effects of Noise

  • Incorrect logic interpretation.
  • Unwanted switching.
  • Intermittent circuit malfunction.
  • Timing errors in digital systems.
  • Unreliable communication between circuits.

9. What Is Noise Margin?

Noise margin is the maximum unwanted voltage disturbance that a logic circuit can tolerate while still recognizing the correct logic state under specified conditions.

Two types of noise margin are commonly considered:

  • HIGH Noise Margin (NMH).
  • LOW Noise Margin (NML).

HIGH Noise Margin

NMH = VOH(min) - VIH(min)

It is the difference between the minimum guaranteed HIGH output voltage and the minimum input voltage recognized as HIGH.

LOW Noise Margin

NML = VIL(max) - VOL(max)

It is the difference between the maximum input voltage recognized as LOW and the maximum guaranteed LOW output voltage.

10. Noise Margin Calculation

Consider a standard TTL logic interface with the following guaranteed voltage specifications:

Parameter Value
VOH(min) 2.4 V
VIH(min) 2.0 V
VIL(max) 0.8 V
VOL(max) 0.4 V
Step 1: Calculate HIGH noise margin
NMH = 2.4 - 2.0 = 0.4 V
Step 2: Calculate LOW noise margin
NML = 0.8 - 0.4 = 0.4 V
Final Result
NMH = 0.4 V
NML = 0.4 V
These are standard TTL illustrative limits. The calculated margins apply when the output and input specifications are compatible and the devices operate under their specified conditions.

Digital logic inputs have guaranteed LOW and HIGH recognition regions. The voltage between the specified limits is not guaranteed to be interpreted as either logic state.

12. Practical Methods to Improve Noise Immunity

  • Use proper PCB grounding techniques.
  • Place decoupling capacitors near IC supply pins.
  • Keep sensitive signal tracks away from noisy switching signals.
  • Use suitable shielding where necessary.
  • Avoid unnecessarily long signal wires.
  • Use proper signal termination for transmission lines when required.
  • Use Schmitt-trigger inputs for slowly changing or noisy signals where appropriate.
  • Maintain a clean and stable power supply.

13. Practical Embedded Systems Example

Consider a microcontroller output connected to multiple digital IC inputs.

Before connecting the devices, verify:

  1. The microcontroller output voltage is compatible with the receiving input.
  2. The total load current is within the GPIO output capability.
  3. The total input capacitance is acceptable for the required switching speed.
  4. The LOW and HIGH noise margins are adequate.
  5. All devices share a suitable common reference ground.
Do not connect an unlimited number of inputs to one microcontroller GPIO pin. Check the absolute maximum ratings, recommended operating conditions and GPIO current specifications in the datasheet.

16. Lesson Summary

  • Fan-in is the number of inputs of a logic gate.
  • Fan-out is the number of standard logic inputs driven by one output under specified conditions.
  • Source current is delivered by an output.
  • Sink current is absorbed by an output.
  • Fan-out must satisfy both HIGH-state and LOW-state current requirements.
  • CMOS fan-out is often limited by capacitive loading and timing.
  • Noise is an unwanted electrical disturbance.
  • Noise margin indicates tolerance to unwanted voltage disturbances.
  • NMH = VOH(min) - VIH(min).
  • NML = VIL(max) - VOL(max).
  • Good grounding, decoupling and PCB layout improve noise immunity.
