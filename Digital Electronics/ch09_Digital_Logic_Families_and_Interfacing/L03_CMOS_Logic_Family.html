2. What Is CMOS?

CMOS stands for Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor. It is a semiconductor technology that uses complementary pairs of P-channel and N-channel MOSFETs to implement digital logic circuits.

In CMOS logic, PMOS and NMOS transistors work together so that ideally one transistor network conducts while the other is OFF during a stable logic state.

CMOS is widely used in modern microcontrollers, processors, memory devices and digital integrated circuits because of its high integration capability and low static power consumption.

Important Features

  • Uses complementary NMOS and PMOS transistors.
  • Very high input impedance.
  • Low steady-state input current.
  • Low static power consumption in normal operation.
  • Good noise immunity in many CMOS families.
  • Available in a wide range of supply-voltage families.

3. MOSFET Types Used in CMOS

CMOS circuits use two types of MOSFETs:

1. NMOS Transistor

An NMOS transistor uses an n-channel conduction path. It turns ON when its gate-to-source voltage exceeds the required threshold voltage.

  • Uses electrons as the majority carriers.
  • Conducts when an adequate positive gate-to-source voltage is applied.
  • Commonly used in the pull-down network of CMOS logic.

2. PMOS Transistor

A PMOS transistor uses a p-channel conduction path. It turns ON when its gate voltage is sufficiently lower than its source voltage.

  • Uses holes as the majority carriers.
  • Conducts when an adequate negative gate-to-source voltage is applied.
  • Commonly used in the pull-up network of CMOS logic.

4. Working Principle of CMOS

A CMOS logic circuit generally contains two complementary transistor networks:

  • Pull-Up Network (PUN): Usually constructed using PMOS transistors.
  • Pull-Down Network (PDN): Usually constructed using NMOS transistors.

The pull-up network connects the output towards the positive supply when the required logic condition is satisfied. The pull-down network connects the output towards ground under the complementary condition.

In a correctly designed static complementary CMOS gate, the pull-up and pull-down networks ideally do not conduct simultaneously in a stable logic state.

5. CMOS Inverter

A CMOS inverter is the simplest CMOS logic circuit. It uses one PMOS transistor and one NMOS transistor.

Basic Circuit Diagram

Both transistor gates receive the same input signal. Their output terminals are connected together to form the inverter output.

Case 1: Input LOW (0)

  • PMOS turns ON.
  • NMOS turns OFF.
  • Output is pulled towards VDD.
  • Output becomes HIGH (1).

Case 2: Input HIGH (1)

  • PMOS turns OFF.
  • NMOS turns ON.
  • Output is pulled towards ground.
  • Output becomes LOW (0).

6. CMOS NAND Gate

A CMOS NAND gate consists of PMOS and NMOS transistor networks arranged to implement the NAND Boolean function.

For a two-input NAND gate:

Y = (A · B)'

Transistor Arrangement

  • Two PMOS transistors are connected in parallel in the pull-up network.
  • Two NMOS transistors are connected in series in the pull-down network.
In a CMOS NAND gate, the output becomes LOW only when both inputs are HIGH because both series NMOS transistors must conduct.

8. CMOS Logic Voltage Levels

CMOS logic voltage levels depend on the particular device family and its supply voltage.

Unlike classic standard 5 V TTL, CMOS families may support several supply-voltage ranges.

Important CMOS Voltage Parameters

Parameter Meaning
VIL Maximum input voltage recognized as LOW.
VIH Minimum input voltage recognized as HIGH.
VOL Maximum output voltage in LOW state.
VOH Minimum output voltage in HIGH state.

Example: 5 V CMOS Logic

Some CMOS families operating at 5 V use input thresholds related to a fraction of the supply voltage. For example, a particular 74HC device may specify CMOS input thresholds around 0.3 VDD and 0.7 VDD.

These fractional values are illustrative, not universal. Different CMOS families such as 74HC, 74HCT and CD4000 can have different input thresholds. Always check the exact datasheet.

9. CMOS Power Consumption

One of the major advantages of CMOS technology is its low static power consumption.

1. Static Power Dissipation

Static power is consumed when the circuit is not switching. Ideally, a complementary CMOS gate has very little direct current from VDD to ground in a stable logic state.

Actual CMOS devices still consume some static power because of leakage currents and other internal circuit paths.

2. Dynamic Power Dissipation

Dynamic power is consumed mainly when internal and load capacitances are charged and discharged during switching.

Pdynamic ≈ α C VDD² f

Where:

  • α = Switching activity factor.
  • C = Effective switched capacitance.
  • VDD = Supply voltage.
  • f = Clock or switching frequency.
Dynamic power increases with switching activity, capacitance, frequency and the square of supply voltage.

10. CMOS Switching Characteristics

CMOS circuits require time to change their output after an input transition.

  • Propagation delay (tPD).
  • Rise time (tr).
  • Fall time (tf).
  • Input and output capacitance.
  • Load-dependent switching behaviour.

These parameters affect the maximum switching frequency and overall performance of a digital circuit.

12. CMOS Advantages

  • Low static power consumption.
  • High input impedance.
  • Low steady-state input current.
  • High integration density.
  • Wide availability of logic functions.
  • Good noise immunity in many CMOS families.
  • Suitable for battery-powered electronic systems.

13. CMOS Limitations

  • Dynamic power increases with switching frequency and load capacitance.
  • Input pins can be sensitive to electrostatic discharge (ESD).
  • Floating inputs can cause unpredictable behaviour and unnecessary current consumption.
  • Voltage compatibility must be checked when connecting different logic families.
  • Output drive strength varies between device families.
Unused CMOS inputs should normally be tied to a defined logic HIGH or LOW level according to the datasheet. Do not leave ordinary CMOS inputs floating.

14. Applications of CMOS

  • Microcontrollers and microprocessors.
  • RAM, ROM and flash memory devices.
  • Digital signal processing circuits.
  • Mobile phones and computers.
  • Battery-operated electronic devices.
  • Digital counters and timers.
  • Embedded control systems.
  • Digital communication equipment.
